# APUSH — MCQ Stimulus Guide
**Section I, Part A | 55 questions / 55 min | 40% of score**
**Hub:** [[../APUSH Master Study Hub]] | **Also:** [[DBQ Guide]] · [[LEQ Guide]] · [[SAQ Guide]]

---

## Format Overview

- Questions come in **sets of 3–4**, each anchored to a single stimulus
- Stimulus types: written excerpts (primary or secondary), political cartoons, maps, charts/graphs, images
- Skills tested: contextualization, causation, CCOT, comparison, argumentation
- No stimulus is there to give you the answer — it's a jumping-off point; your content knowledge decides the question

---

## Reading Written Excerpts

**Step 1 — Identify before reading:** Who wrote it? When? To whom?
**Step 2 — Find the main claim** in the first and last sentences (body is usually elaboration)
**Step 3 — Tag it:** Is this a primary source (participant/observer) or secondary source (historian's interpretation)?

**Primary source patterns:**
- Government documents (acts, proclamations, treaties) → ask: what problem was this trying to solve?
- Personal letters, speeches, diaries → ask: what is the author's purpose and audience? What might they omit or distort?
- Newspaper/pamphlet → ask: who was the intended reader? What reaction was intended?

**Secondary source patterns:**
- Historian's argument about a period → AP often pairs TWO historians with opposing interpretations; you'll be asked to identify what evidence supports or undermines each
- Look for the thesis in the opening sentence; the rest is support

> [!tip] The "Why Does This Source Exist?" Test
> Before answering any question on a written source, ask: why did someone create this? The answer usually reveals audience, purpose, and point of view — which determines which answer choices are correct.

## Reading Political Cartoons

**Step 1 — Inventory:** List every symbol, label, figure, and action in the image before interpreting
**Step 2 — Identify the cartoonist's position:** Who is being mocked or criticized? What is being praised?
**Step 3 — Date it:** Cartoons are almost always dated or dateable from context; this tells you what event they're responding to

**Common AP cartoon symbols:**
| Symbol | Typical Meaning |
|---|---|
| Octopus | Monopoly/corporation strangling competition or government |
| Uncle Sam | U.S. government or national interest |
| Eagle | American power or imperialism |
| Scales | Justice, balance, inequality |
| Chains/shackles | Oppression, slavery, economic bondage |
| Elephant / Donkey | Republican / Democratic Party |
| Oversized figure dominating small figures | Power imbalance (boss vs. worker, imperial power vs. colony) |

> [!warning] Don't Over-Interpret
> AP cartoons are usually making one clear point. If you're reading five layers of symbolism, you've gone too far. Find the main target and the cartoonist's attitude toward it.

## Reading Maps

**Step 1 — Read the title and legend first** — tells you what's being shown and what the symbols mean
**Step 2 — Note the date range** — maps are almost always asking about change over time
**Step 3 — Look for patterns:** What's expanding? What's shrinking? What's absent?

**Common AP map types:**
- Territorial expansion maps (Louisiana Purchase, Mexican Cession, etc.) → ask: what policy or event caused this expansion?
- Population/migration maps → ask: what push/pull factors drove this movement?
- Election maps → ask: what regional coalitions do these results reflect?
- Battle/military maps → ask: what strategic advantage or disadvantage does this geography show?

---

## Reading Charts and Graphs

**Step 1 — Read both axes before looking at the data** — x-axis (time usually), y-axis (what's being measured), units
**Step 2 — Identify the trend:** rising, falling, stable, volatile
**Step 3 — Look for anomalies:** sudden spikes or drops → almost always the answer to a "what caused" question
**Step 4 — Connect the anomaly to a historical event you know**

> [!tip] The Anomaly Is the Question
> AP charts almost always have one obvious inflection point. The question will ask what caused it or what effect it had. If you can name the historical event that matches the date of the inflection point, you can answer the question.

## Question Set Strategy (3–4 Questions Per Stimulus)

Within each set, question difficulty typically escalates:
- **Q1**: Describe or identify something directly from the stimulus (low difficulty)
- **Q2**: Explain a cause or effect using the stimulus + your knowledge (medium)
- **Q3/Q4**: Evaluate, compare, or connect to a different period using your knowledge (high difficulty; stimulus is less helpful here)

> [!warning] The Last Question in a Set Is a Trap
> The final question in a 3–4 question set usually asks you to go BEYOND the stimulus — to a different time period, a counterargument, or a broader trend. Students who rely only on the stimulus miss these. Use the stimulus to orient, then rely on your content knowledge.

## Wrong Answer Patterns

| Wrong Answer Type | How to Spot It |
|---|---|
| **Too broad / too vague** | Sounds correct but doesn't actually answer the specific question |
| **Anachronistic** | Uses a concept or event from the wrong time period |
| **Half-right** | First half of the answer is accurate; second half contradicts the source or your knowledge |
| **Extreme language** | "Always," "never," "all," "completely" — almost always wrong in history |
| **True but irrelevant** | Factually correct but doesn't answer what was asked |

> [!danger] The "Too Easy" Trap
> If an answer choice sounds like obvious common knowledge with no historical specificity, it's usually wrong. AP rewards specific, nuanced understanding. An answer that any random person could guess without studying is bait.

## Time Management

- **55 questions / 55 minutes = ~1 min per question**
- First pass: answer what you know confidently; mark uncertain questions
- Second pass: tackle marked questions; use process of elimination
- Never leave a question blank — no penalty for guessing
- If a stimulus set has one confusing question, don't let it sink the others in the set

---

## Process of Elimination Rules

1. Eliminate any answer with a clearly wrong date or time period
2. Eliminate answers that are factually accurate but don't answer the question asked
3. Between two remaining choices: pick the one that uses the same vocabulary/framing as the stimulus or the question stem
4. If two answers seem equally plausible, ask: which one is more *specific*? AP usually rewards specificity.
