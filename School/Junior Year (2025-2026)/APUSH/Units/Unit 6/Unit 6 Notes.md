## Study Hub Quick Reference — Unit 6

> [!check] MCQ Pattern — Gilded Age Industrial Power
> MCQ stimulus often shows a political cartoon of a robber baron (Standard Oil as octopus, Carnegie as Carnegie Hall benefactor). Key tension: industrial growth and technological innovation vs. labor exploitation, political corruption, and inequality. Both sides appear in AP sources — read them carefully.

> [!check] MCQ Pattern — Farmers' Grievances and Populism
> Farmers hated: railroad freight rates (discriminatory pricing), falling crop prices (deflation), tight money supply (gold standard), mortgage debt. Grangers → Farmers' Alliances → Populist Party. Platform: graduated income tax, direct election of senators, government ownership of railroads, bimetallism (silver + gold). Many of these were eventually adopted.

> [!warning] Trap — "Robber Baron" vs. "Captain of Industry"
> AP presents both interpretations. Progressive-era critics: robber barons exploited workers and bribed politicians. Later defenders: captains of industry who built railroads, created jobs, lowered prices. MCQ stimulus may present either view — identify which and explain its historical context.

> [!warning] Trap — Dawes Act Was Not Pro-Native
> The Dawes Act (1887) was framed as giving Native Americans land ownership and citizenship, but its actual effect was to break up tribal landholdings, destroy communal culture, and reduce Native land from 138 million acres (1887) to 48 million acres (1934). The AP wants you to see the gap between stated intent and actual effect.

> [!danger] Critical — Plessy v. Ferguson
> Plessy v. Ferguson (1896): Supreme Court ruled "separate but equal" is constitutional under the 14th Amendment. This provided the legal foundation for Jim Crow laws that lasted until Brown v. Board (1954). Know the case, the ruling, and Harlan's famous dissent: "Our Constitution is color-blind."

## Industrial Capitalism and the "Robber Barons"

**Second Industrial Revolution** fueled by: railroads, steel, oil, electricity, new management methods

### Key Industrialists
| Figure | Industry | Method | Key Fact |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Andrew Carnegie** | Steel | Vertical integration (controlled all stages: ore → steel → distribution) | Carnegie Steel → U.S. Steel (sold to J.P. Morgan 1901 for $480M); gave away 90% of fortune |
| **John D. Rockefeller** | Oil | Horizontal integration (bought out / crushed all competitors); Standard Oil Trust | Standard Oil controlled 90% of U.S. refining; broken up by Supreme Court 1911 |
| **J.P. Morgan** | Finance/Banking | Investment banking; consolidated industries (U.S. Steel, General Electric) | "Banker of last resort"; bailed out U.S. government during 1895 gold crisis |
| **Cornelius Vanderbilt** | Railroads | Consolidated New York rail lines | Built Grand Central; exemplified railroad monopoly power |

**Business Methods:**
- **Trusts**: shell company that controlled competing companies; Sherman Antitrust Act (1890) aimed to break them but rarely enforced until TR (Unit 7)
- **Social Darwinism**: Herbert Spencer applied Darwin's natural selection to society; "survival of the fittest" justified wealth inequality as natural law; used to oppose government regulation
- **Gospel of Wealth** (Carnegie, 1889): wealthy had duty to give back through philanthropy; libraries, universities, concert halls
- **Laissez-faire** economics: government should not interfere in the economy; dominant political ideology of the period

## Labor and the Working Class

**Conditions:** 12-hour workdays; 6-day weeks; child labor; dangerous conditions; no safety regulations; poverty wages; company towns (company controlled housing, stores — workers paid in scrip)

**Labor Organizations:**
| Organization | Founded | Approach | Key Facts |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Knights of Labor** | 1869 | Inclusive (all workers, Black and white, men and women, skilled and unskilled) | Peaked ~700,000 members mid-1880s; declined after Haymarket Affair |
| **American Federation of Labor (AFL)** | 1886 | Craft unions; skilled workers only; excluded women and Black workers | Samuel Gompers; "pure and simple" unionism — wages, hours, conditions; survived long-term |

**Major Labor Conflicts:**
- **Great Railroad Strike (1877)**: first national strike; B&O Railroad workers protested wage cuts; spread across country; federal troops sent in; ~100 workers killed; exposed labor-capital conflict
- **Haymarket Affair (1886)**: police clashed with workers at McCormick Reaper; next day bomb thrown at police at Haymarket Square rally; 7 police, 4 workers died; anarchist leaders arrested and executed despite no proof; devastated Knights of Labor (associated with radicalism)
- **Homestead Strike (1892)**: Carnegie Steel workers at Homestead, PA struck over wage cuts; Carnegie had Henry Clay Frick call in Pinkerton detectives (armed private police); violent battle; strike crushed; union destroyed at Carnegie plants
- **Pullman Strike (1894)**: Pullman Palace Car workers struck over wage cuts without rent/food cost reductions in company town; Eugene V. Debs led American Railway Union in sympathy boycott; halted rail traffic nationwide; Attorney General used court injunction + federal troops to break it; Debs jailed → became socialist

## Immigration and Urbanization

**New Immigration (1880s–1920s):**
- Earlier immigrants: Northern and Western Europe (Germany, Ireland, Scandinavia — "old immigration")
- Now: Southern and Eastern Europe (Italy, Poland, Russia/Jewish, Greece, Austro-Hungary) — "new immigration"
- Push factors: poverty, land shortage, religious persecution (Jewish pogroms in Russia)
- Pull factors: industrial jobs, cheap land (earlier), chain migration (family networks)
- **Ellis Island** (opened 1892): processed ~12 million immigrants; medical/legal inspection
- **Chinese Exclusion Act (1882)**: banned Chinese labor immigration; first major federal immigration restriction; extended repeatedly until 1943

**Nativist Reactions:**
- Fear of cultural dilution, job competition, Catholic/Jewish "threat"
- Social Darwinism used to "prove" racial inferiority of new immigrants
- Immigration Restriction League advocated literacy tests and quota systems (later enacted in 1920s)

**Urban Growth:**
- Cities exploded: Chicago, New York, Cleveland, Pittsburgh became industrial centers
- **Tenements**: crowded, unsanitary urban apartments; disease rampant; Jacob Riis documented conditions in *How the Other Half Lives* (1890) — photojournalism as reform tool
- **Political machines**: urban political organizations (Tammany Hall in NYC, Boss Tweed) provided jobs, housing, food to immigrants in exchange for votes; corrupt but functional
- **Hull House (1889)**: Jane Addams; settlement house movement; provided services in immigrant neighborhoods; model for Progressive reform (Unit 7)

## The New South and Jim Crow

- **New South** vision (Henry Grady): South industrialized, diversified beyond cotton; southern textile mills, railroads
- Reality: South remained economically dependent on agriculture; Black labor exploited through **sharecropping** and **crop-lien system** (merchants extended credit on next year's crop at high interest → permanent debt cycle)
- **Convict leasing**: states leased prisoners (disproportionately Black men arrested under vagrancy laws) to businesses for forced labor; effectively slavery

**Disenfranchisement:**
- Poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses, white primaries: eliminated Black political participation in the South by 1900
- **Plessy v. Ferguson (1896)**: "separate but equal" doctrine legalized racial segregation in all public facilities
- **Jim Crow laws**: comprehensive system of racial segregation and subordination throughout the South

**Black Responses:**
- **Booker T. Washington**: *Atlanta Compromise* speech (1895); accepted segregation in exchange for economic opportunity; vocational training (Tuskegee Institute); "cast down your bucket where you are"
- **Ida B. Wells**: journalist; documented and publicized lynching; anti-lynching crusade; founded NAACP with others (1909)
- **W.E.B. Du Bois** (more prominent in Unit 7): challenged Washington; demanded full civil rights immediately; "Talented Tenth"; *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903)

## Farmers' Discontent and Populism

**Farmers' Problems:**
- **Falling crop prices**: overproduction globally; crop prices fell steadily 1870s–90s
- **Railroad discrimination**: railroads charged farmers higher rates than large shippers; long-haul vs. short-haul price discrepancies
- **Tight money / deflation**: gold standard meant limited money supply; hard to repay debts; farmers wanted more money in circulation (inflation = easier debt repayment)
- **Mortgage debt**: millions of farms mortgaged; foreclosures rampant

**Farmers' Organizations:**
- **Granger Movement (1860s–70s)**: social/educational clubs; lobbied for railroad regulation; won Munn v. Illinois (1877) — states can regulate railroads in public interest; later Interstate Commerce Commission (1887) established
- **Farmers' Alliances (1880s)**: credit cooperatives; political education; southern and northern alliances; formed backbone of Populist Party

**Populist Party (People's Party, 1892):**
Platform:
- **Bimetallism**: coinage of silver at 16:1 ratio to gold → inflate money supply → help debtors
- Government ownership of railroads and telegraph
- **Graduated income tax**
- **Direct election of senators** (not yet — had to wait for 17th Amendment, 1913)
- Secret ballot; initiative and referendum (direct democracy)
- 8-hour workday
- Omaha Platform (1892); James Weaver presidential candidate: 22 Electoral votes

**Election of 1896 (Bryan vs. McKinley):**
- **William Jennings Bryan**: "Cross of Gold" speech; ran as Democrat AND Populist; silver standard
- **William McKinley**: Republican; gold standard; business/industrial support; won decisively
- Populism collapsed; its platform was later adopted piecemeal during Progressive Era

## The West: Settlement, Conflict, and Myth

**Transcontinental Railroad (completed 1869)**: linked East to West; Central Pacific (Chinese labor) met Union Pacific (Irish labor) at Promontory Summit, Utah; enabled mass settlement and commercial agriculture in Great Plains

**Homestead Act (1862)**: 160 acres free to settlers who farmed for 5 years; gave out 270 million acres; often failed (claims too small, climate too harsh)

**Native American Displacement:**
- U.S. Army pushed tribes off Great Plains through a series of wars (1850s–90s)
- **Battle of Little Bighorn (1876)**: Lakota, Cheyenne, Arapaho under Sitting Bull and Crazy Horse defeated Custer; last major Native military victory
- **Battle of Wounded Knee (1890)**: Army massacred ~250 Lakota Ghost Dance participants; effectively ended Native resistance
- **Dawes Act (1887)**: broke up tribal lands into individual allotments; goal was assimilation; result was loss of 90 million acres; destroyed communal culture
- **Indian boarding schools**: forced assimilation; children removed from families; "Kill the Indian, save the man" (Richard Pratt)
- Buffalo slaughter: commercial hunters killed 30+ million bison; destroyed Plains peoples' food supply

## AP Exam Tips — Unit 6

- **DBQ frequently draws from this period**: Gilded Age political cartoons, labor union speeches, Populist Platform, Atlantic Compromise vs. Du Bois — know both sides of these debates
- **LEQ Era 2 (1800–1898)**: common prompt — "Evaluate the extent to which industrialization changed the lives of American workers"
- **SAQ**: common — explain one cause of farmers' discontent in the Gilded Age; describe one way the government responded to labor conflict
- **Populist Platform**: many items eventually became law (income tax = 16th Amendment, direct election = 17th Amendment, Federal Reserve) — great continuity/change evidence for essays
- Know **Plessy v. Ferguson** cold; it will appear in a DBQ or SAQ and connects directly to Unit 8's civil rights movement
- The **Dawes Act's intent vs. effect** is a classic AP complexity question — stated purpose (Native assimilation) vs. actual outcome (land loss, cultural destruction)
