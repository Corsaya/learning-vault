## Prompt
> Evaluate the reasons for the United States victory in the American Revolutionary War.

**Notes section:** Unit 3 — American Revolution and New Nation (1754–1800)

**What this prompt is asking:** "Evaluate the reasons" means you must take a position — not just list reasons, but argue which were most important, how they interacted, or which type of factor (military, political, diplomatic, social) was most decisive. A strong response identifies 2–3 causes and argues for a relationship among them.

## Full Answer

**Thesis (line of reasoning):**
The United States achieved victory in the Revolutionary War primarily through a combination of French military and financial intervention — which transformed a colonial insurgency into a global conflict Britain could not win — and a Continental Army strategy focused on survival and attrition rather than battlefield victory, with colonial advantages of geography, motivation, and leadership providing the foundation that made both possible.

**Broader historical context:**
The American Revolutionary War must be understood in the context of ongoing British-French imperial rivalry stretching back through the Seven Years' War (1754–1763). France had been humiliated by its expulsion from North America in 1763 and was eager for an opportunity to weaken Britain. The War of Austrian Succession and the Seven Years' War had left both Britain and France heavily indebted, but France under Louis XVI and his foreign minister Vergennes saw American independence as a strategic tool to reduce British power — regardless of any ideological sympathy for republican principles. This rivalry gave the American cause a potential great-power patron whose involvement would be decisive. Meanwhile, Britain faced the fundamental strategic challenge of projecting military power 3,000 miles across the Atlantic to suppress a rebellion spread across a vast territory with a hostile and armed population.

**Evidence 1 — French intervention as the decisive turning point:**
The single most important factor in American victory was French military and financial intervention. Before France's formal alliance with the United States (Treaty of Alliance, February 1778), the Continental Army had suffered a string of defeats (Long Island, White Plains, Brandywine, Germantown) and barely survived the winter at Valley Forge (1777–1778). French entry transformed the strategic picture: France provided loans and subsidies that kept the Continental Army funded; French naval power challenged British control of Atlantic sea lanes; French troops participated in critical campaigns. The war's decisive military action — the Siege of Yorktown (October 1781) — could not have succeeded without the French fleet under Admiral de Grasse, which blocked British reinforcement and escape by sea, and the 7,800 French troops under Rochambeau who constituted a significant portion of the besieging force. Without France, British sea power would have ensured the American cause failed.

**Evidence 2 — Washington's strategy of survival and attrition:**
George Washington's strategic genius was understanding that the Continental Army did not need to win the war — it needed to *not lose it* while making the war too costly for Britain to continue. Washington consistently chose strategic retreat over glorious defeat, preserving his army as a functioning force even when it meant abandoning cities (New York, Philadelphia). His willingness to avoid decisive engagement frustrated British commanders who needed a knockout blow to end the rebellion quickly. Meanwhile, the guerrilla tactics used by militia forces — particularly in the South (Francis Marion, Nathanael Greene's Carolinas campaign, 1780–1781) — denied Britain control of the countryside even when it held major cities. This strategy of attrition meant that British military victories rarely translated into political pacification. The Carolinas campaign demonstrated that Britain could win battles (Camden, 1780) while losing strategic control of the South, because the American forces could reconstitute and harass British forces indefinitely.

**Evidence 3 — British strategic and political limitations:**
American victory was also enabled by Britain's own strategic disadvantages and political divisions. Britain was fighting a distant war across an ocean at enormous expense, against a population that included active Patriots, passive sympathizers, and only a minority of committed Loyalists — making pacification impossible without permanent occupation of every county in every colony. The British government was politically divided: Whig opposition leaders like Edmund Burke and Charles James Fox opposed the war as unjust and ruinous. After France entered the war, Britain also had to divert resources to defend the Caribbean sugar islands, Gibraltar, and India — the American theater was no longer the only front. The combination of logistics (supplying an army 3,000 miles away), political opposition at home, and the expanding global war made British victory increasingly expensive and unlikely even as American forces grew stronger.

**Note on thesis:** "The Americans won because of French help, good leadership, and motivation" is a list, not a line of reasoning. A line of reasoning takes a position on *mechanism*: the essay above argues that French intervention was the primary decisive factor, with Washington's attrition strategy as the necessary precondition that kept the Continental Army alive long enough for France to intervene and tip the balance. That's a causal argument with an internal logic — each element connects to the others.
