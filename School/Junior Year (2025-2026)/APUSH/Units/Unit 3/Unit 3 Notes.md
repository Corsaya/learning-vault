## Study Hub Quick Reference — Unit 3

> [!check] MCQ Pattern — Imperial Crisis Sequence
> Know the chronological cascade: French and Indian War (1754–63) → Britain in debt → end of salutary neglect → Proclamation of 1763 → Stamp Act (1765) → Townshend Acts (1767) → Boston Massacre (1770) → Tea Act/Boston Tea Party (1773) → Intolerable Acts (1774) → First Continental Congress → Shots fired (1775) → Declaration (1776). MCQ frequently tests which act caused which response.

> [!check] MCQ Pattern — Constitutional Debates
> Hamilton vs. Jefferson: broad vs. strict construction; national bank controversy; Federalist Papers (especially No. 10 — factions/republics, No. 51 — checks/balances). AP uses Federalist excerpts as MCQ stimuli constantly.

> [!warning] Trap — Articles of Confederation Weren't Useless
> AP sometimes presents Articles in a nuanced light: they successfully governed the Northwest Territory (Northwest Ordinance, 1787), won the Revolutionary War, and negotiated the Treaty of Paris. Their weakness was specifically in taxation, trade regulation, and national defense. Don't write them off entirely in essays.

> [!warning] Trap — Declaration ≠ Constitution
> The Declaration (1776) is a statement of natural rights philosophy (Locke). The Constitution (1787) is the actual governing framework. The AP distinguishes them constantly in stimulus questions. Know which document does what.

> [!danger] Critical — Republicanism vs. Liberalism
> **Republicanism**: virtue, civic participation, placing the common good over individual interest; fear of tyranny and corruption. **Liberalism** (Lockean): natural rights, consent of governed, social contract. Both ideologies drove the Revolution but can conflict. AP essays often ask which ideology is represented in a given source.

## Heimler's History — Key Frameworks
**Unit 3 Full Review Video:** [APUSH Unit 3 REVIEW (Period 3: 1754–1800)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1oNumKYNUaE)

**Heimler's big idea — Three-Act Structure:**
> **Act 1:** Causes of the American Revolution (F&I War → imperial crisis → independence)
> **Act 2:** The New Republic (Articles → Constitution → first parties)
> **Act 3:** Migration and Conflict (westward expansion, Native resistance, slavery spreading west)

### Act 1 — Causes of the Revolution

**Washington triggered the war:** The French and Indian War erupted in 1754 because of a **military blunder by George Washington** at Fort Necessity. The war soon spread globally (Seven Years' War = arguably the first World War).

**Pontiac's Rebellion (1763):** Before the Proclamation of 1763 was even issued, colonists had already sparked a conflict with a Native alliance known as Pontiac's Rebellion — one reason Britain needed the Proclamation Line to manage frontier tensions.

**Grenville's taxation and the representation debate:**
- **Virtual representation** (British argument): Parliament represents ALL English subjects everywhere, whether they elect members or not
- **Actual representation** (colonial argument): because colonists had grown accustomed to *local* self-governance, they believed representation must be local — and since no colonist sat in Parliament, they had none
- This was NOT about independence — even the Stamp Act Congress (1765) and the First Continental Congress (1774) wanted to remain *British subjects* with *British rights*

**What finally tipped colonists toward independence:**
1. **Common Sense** (Thomas Paine, January 1776) — used Enlightenment natural rights + *biblical* arguments (monarchy in the Old Testament as a sin against God) to argue that revolution was inevitable; reached ordinary colonists
2. **Second Continental Congress (1775)** — even after Lexington and Concord, many delegates still wanted to negotiate. Only by 1776 did independence become the consensus.

### Act 2 — The New Republic

**Why Americans should NOT have won the Revolution:**
- ~15–20% of colonists were **Loyalists**; only ~50% supported the Patriots; another ~30% wanted nothing to do with either side
- British military superiority was massive — the world's deadliest navy and a seasoned army that had fought constantly in global wars

**Three factors that actually won it:**
1. **Washington's leadership:** Surprise attack at Trenton (proved Americans could win); hired **Friedrich Wilhelm von Steuben** (Prussian officer) to train and discipline the Continental Army
2. **French alliance:** The Continental Army won *no* major battle victories in its first 6 years; the **Battle of Saratoga (1777)** proved Americans could win → France joined; French troops + navy at **Yorktown (1781)** were decisive
3. **Ideological commitment:** Patriots fought to *secure liberty* — an existential motivation. British soldiers were fighting to maintain *economic and political dominance*. The asymmetry in motivation mattered.

**Whiskey Rebellion vs. Shays' Rebellion — the comparison that shows how much changed:**
- **Shays' Rebellion (1786–87)** → under the Articles, no executive existed; Massachusetts governor called for federal help → *nobody answered*. The Articles had no enforcement mechanism.
- **Whiskey Rebellion (1794)** → under the Constitution, when Pennsylvania called for help, Washington personally led a militia of 13,000 men to suppress it. Federal power could now enforce laws.
- This comparison proves the Constitution solved the Articles' central problem.

**Four issues that created the first political parties:**
1. **Economic policy** — Hamilton's national bank: Federalists (pro); Democratic-Republicans (anti — no constitutional basis, power grab against farmers)
2. **Liberty vs. order** — Whiskey Rebellion: Federalists used force (order); Democratic-Republicans viewed this as tyranny
3. **Foreign policy** — French Revolution: Democratic-Republicans wanted to support French revolutionaries (like France supported us); Federalists worried war with Britain would hurt trade → Washington's Neutrality Proclamation; Jefferson resigned from cabinet in protest
4. **Federal vs. state power** — Alien and Sedition Acts (1798): criminalized criticism of the government; Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions (Madison + Jefferson) responded: states can nullify unconstitutional federal laws → **nullification doctrine** first appears here

### Act 3 — Migration, Identity, Conflict

**National identity forming — Heimler's examples:**
- **Art:** Charles Willson Peale painted Revolutionary leaders as Republican heroes → gave Americans visual icons of nationhood (Peale's Washington appears as a *stimulus* in Progress Check)
- **Literature:** Hector St. John de Crèvecoeur — considered one of the first American novelists; described the American as a "new man" blending European roots with frontier opportunity
- **Architecture:** Federal style (replacing Georgian/British style) in major buildings like the Capitol — a visual rejection of British cultural identity

**Gowen Pamphlet example:** Formerly enslaved, gained freedom, became minister of one of America's earliest Black Baptist congregations — example of marginalized voices claiming a portion of American identity even when society refused to fully deliver on its promises

**Western migration conflicts — the three-way problem:**
- **Britain** had NOT withdrawn from frontier posts (violating Treaty of Paris 1783) and was arming Ohio Valley Native groups → **Jay's Treaty (1794)** finally got Britain to withdraw
- **Spain** was arming Creeks and Cherokees in the South → **Pinckney's Treaty (1795/1796)** formally recognized borders, opened Mississippi River and New Orleans port to US merchants → ended Spanish opposition to US westward expansion
- **Native alliance:** Washington sent 2,000 troops into the Ohio country (1794) → **Battle of Fallen Timbers** → US won → **Treaty of Greenville** opened Ohio territory for settlement
- **Slavery spreading west:** Southern planters followed cotton west; tobacco/cotton depleted soil → needed new land; northern alarm bells started ringing as slavery expanded into new territories → debate not settled until the Civil War

## French and Indian War (1754–1763)

- Also called the **Seven Years' War** (global scope); North American theater pitted Britain + colonial allies vs. France + Native allies (primarily Algonquian peoples)
- Began with George Washington's skirmish at Fort Necessity (1754); British initially defeated (Braddock's defeat, 1755)
- British victory (1763): France ceded Canada and all territory east of Mississippi to Britain; Spain got Louisiana (west of Mississippi)
- **Consequences**:
  - Britain doubled its national debt → needed revenue from colonies
  - Colonists fought alongside British regulars → saw they could hold their own; confidence increased
  - Native alliances with France collapsed → accelerated westward colonial expansion
  - Britain needed to station troops in the colonies → cost more money

## Imperial Crisis: Road to Revolution

### Proclamation of 1763
- Forbade colonial settlement west of Appalachian Mountains; intended to reduce Native conflicts and military costs
- Colonists furious: denied land they'd fought to win; widely ignored

### Parliamentary Taxation
| Act | Year | Content | Colonial Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stamp Act | 1765 | First **direct** tax on colonists; taxed legal documents, newspapers, cards | Stamp Act Congress; "no taxation without representation"; Sons of Liberty; stamp distributors attacked |
| Townshend Acts | 1767 | Import duties on glass, paper, paint, tea | Boycotts; "Letters from a Pennsylvania Farmer" (Dickinson) |
| Tea Act | 1773 | Gave East India Company monopoly; lowered tea price but undercut colonial merchants | Boston Tea Party (December 1773) |
| Intolerable Acts | 1774 | Closed Boston Harbor; revoked Massachusetts charter; quartered troops in private homes | First Continental Congress; called them "Coercive Acts" |

- Colonial ideological framework: **"no taxation without representation"** — virtual representation (British argument) vs. actual representation (colonial argument)
- **Committees of Correspondence**: colonial communication network; coordinated resistance across colonies

### Key Events
- **Boston Massacre (1770)**: British soldiers killed 5 colonists; Paul Revere's engraving spread as propaganda; heightened tensions
- **First Continental Congress (1774)**: delegates from 12 colonies; boycotted British goods; pledged to reconvene if grievances not addressed
- **Lexington and Concord (April 1775)**: first shots; "shot heard round the world"; open war began
- **Second Continental Congress (1775)**: declared independence; formed Continental Army under Washington; printed money; governed during war

## The American Revolution (1775–1783)

### Ideological Foundations
- **John Locke**: natural rights (life, liberty, property); government by consent; right to overthrow tyranny — direct influence on Declaration
- **Thomas Paine, *Common Sense* (January 1776)**: first mass-market argument for independence; attacked monarchy as irrational; reached ordinary colonists
- **Declaration of Independence (July 4, 1776)**: written primarily by Jefferson; stated Lockean natural rights; listed grievances against King George; declared independence of 13 states

### Military
- Continental Army: undersupplied, poorly paid; winter at Valley Forge (1777–78) nearly destroyed it; Baron von Steuben (Prussia) trained troops
- Key turning point: **Battle of Saratoga (1777)** — American victory convinced France to ally with the colonies (Treaty of Alliance, 1778); France, Spain, Netherlands later joined against Britain
- **Lord Cornwallis surrendered at Yorktown (1781)**: effectively ended major combat
- **Treaty of Paris (1783)**: Britain recognized U.S. independence; Mississippi River as western border; no mention of Loyalists' property or British debts (which created future problems)

### Who Supported / Opposed Independence
- **Patriots**: merchants hurt by trade restrictions, planters, frontier farmers, artisans, radical Whig politicians
- **Loyalists (~20%)**: Crown officials, Anglican clergy, some merchants, many recent immigrants, some enslaved people (promised freedom by British)
- **Enslaved people**: some fought for British (Lord Dunmore's Proclamation, 1775 — promised freedom to enslaved people who escaped Patriot masters and joined British); others fought for Patriots hoping for post-war freedom (rarely granted)
- Women: managed farms and businesses during war; Abigail Adams' "Remember the Ladies" letter to John Adams (1776)

## Articles of Confederation (1781–1789)

**Structure:** unicameral Congress; each state had 1 vote; 9 of 13 states required to pass laws; unanimous consent to amend; NO executive; NO judiciary; NO power to tax; NO power to regulate interstate commerce

**Successes:**
- Managed the Revolutionary War
- **Northwest Ordinance (1787)**: organized Northwest Territory (Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, etc.); banned slavery north of Ohio River; established precedent for admitting new states; protected civil liberties — one of the most significant achievements of the Confederation period

**Weaknesses:**
- Could not tax → no money to pay war debts or soldiers
- Could not regulate interstate commerce → trade wars between states
- Could not enforce treaties (Britain kept frontier forts)
- **Shays' Rebellion (1786–87)**: Massachusetts farmers (Revolutionary War veterans) rose up against debt collection and farm foreclosures; Confederation couldn't suppress it → revealed fatal weakness; convinced leaders that a stronger government was needed

## Constitutional Convention (1787)

- 55 delegates in Philadelphia; officially met to revise Articles; instead wrote new Constitution; conducted in **secret**
- Major debates:

| Debate | Options | Resolution |
|---|---|---|
| Representation | Large states: Virginia Plan (proportional); Small states: New Jersey Plan (equal) | **Great Compromise**: bicameral Congress — House (proportional) + Senate (equal) |
| Slavery | Northern delegates uncomfortable; Southern delegates demanded protection | **3/5 Compromise**: enslaved people counted as 3/5 of a person for representation and direct taxes; slave trade protected until 1808 |
| Executive power | Fear of tyranny vs. need for strong executive | Indirect election via **Electoral College**; 4-year terms |

- **Federalism**: sovereignty divided between national and state governments — compromise between centralization and states' rights
- Constitution ratified 1788; required 9 of 13 states

### Ratification Debate
- **Federalists** (Hamilton, Madison, Jay): supported ratification; wrote **Federalist Papers** (85 essays arguing for Constitution)
  - *Federalist No. 10* (Madison): large republic better controls factions than small democracy
  - *Federalist No. 51* (Madison): checks and balances prevent tyranny — "ambition must be made to counteract ambition"
- **Anti-Federalists** (Patrick Henry, George Mason): feared strong central government; wanted Bill of Rights; feared standing army
- **Bill of Rights** (1791, first 10 amendments): added to secure ratification from holdout states

## The New Republic (1789–1800)

### Washington's Presidency (1789–1797)
- Established **precedents**: two-term limit (not formal until 22nd Amendment), cabinet system, neutrality in European wars
- **Hamilton's Economic Program**:
  - Assume state Revolutionary War debts → national credit
  - National Bank (Bank of the United States) → stabilize currency, loan to government
  - Tariffs on imports → protect infant American industries
  - Excise tax on whiskey → revenue
- **Whiskey Rebellion (1794)**: Pennsylvania farmers revolted against whiskey tax; Washington personally led militia → demonstrated federal government could enforce laws (unlike under Articles)

### Hamilton vs. Jefferson
| Issue | Hamilton (Federalist) | Jefferson (Democratic-Republican) |
|---|---|---|
| Constitution | Broad construction; implied powers (elastic clause) | Strict construction; only enumerated powers |
| Economy | Manufacturing, commerce, national bank | Agrarian ideal; "yeoman farmer" as backbone |
| Foreign policy | Pro-British (trade) | Pro-French (revolutionary alliance) |
| Federal power | Strong central government | States' rights |
| Base | Merchants, bankers, Northeast | Farmers, planters, South and West |

### Jay's Treaty (1794) and Pinckney's Treaty (1795)
- **Jay's Treaty**: resolved post-war disputes with Britain; Britain withdrew from frontier forts; unpopular — seen as too favorable to Britain; deepened Federalist/Republican split
- **Pinckney's Treaty (1795)**: with Spain; U.S. gained navigation rights on Mississippi River and deposit rights at New Orleans — popular success

### Adams's Presidency (1797–1801)
- **XYZ Affair (1797–98)**: French diplomats (X, Y, Z) demanded bribes before negotiations; Congress was outraged; quasi-war with France at sea
- **Alien and Sedition Acts (1798)**: extended residency for citizenship; allowed deportation of dangerous aliens; criminalized criticism of the government → targeted Democratic-Republicans
- **Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions (1798–99)**: Madison and Jefferson argued states could nullify unconstitutional federal laws — **nullification doctrine** (foreshadows 1830s crisis)
- **Election of 1800** ("Revolution of 1800"): Jefferson defeated Adams; peaceful transfer of power between opposing parties — a landmark in democratic governance

### FRQ Part A — SAQ Summary

**SAQ 1 — Peter Oliver, Loyalist account (1781)**
**(a) Point of view expressed [1 pt]:**
Oliver's Loyalist/pro-Crown perspective: colonial resistance was driven by the economic self-interest of merchant-smugglers protecting illegal trade networks, not by genuine constitutional principle. The rebellion was a conspiracy of criminals disguised as a constitutional protest.

**(b) Historical situation in which produced [1 pt]:**
Oliver was a Loyalist exile in Britain, writing during the ongoing Revolutionary War (published 1781). Loyalists were producing counter-narratives to the Patriots' account; Oliver was arguing from personal experience as a royal judge that the Revolution was manufactured by merchants evading British commercial law.

**(c) ONE piece of evidence Oliver uses [1 pt]:**
Extensive illegal Dutch trading (both in Holland and Dutch West Indies settlements) as his primary evidence — he argues that when Parliament passed the Townshend Acts, colonial merchants organized resistance not because duties were unconstitutional (he notes they were trivial) but because *any* enforcement of British trade law would destroy profitable smuggling networks.

**SAQ 2 — Preamble to the Constitution (1787)**
**(a) ONE historical development illustrated [1 pt]:**
New framework for national government designed to address Articles of Confederation failures. "Form a more perfect Union" directly references the Confederation's weakness; the enumerated purposes (justice, domestic tranquility, common defense, general welfare) each correspond to specific Articles deficiencies.

**(b) ONE comparison with the Declaration of Independence [1 pt]:**
Both invoke **popular sovereignty** — government derives legitimacy from the people. But they express it at different moments: the Declaration uses it to justify *overthrowing* an existing government ("consent of the governed"); the Preamble uses the same concept to *construct* a new one ("We the People . . . do ordain and establish"). The Declaration tears down; the Preamble builds up.

**(c) ONE point of view of the authors [1 pt]:**
Federalist orientation that strong national government was necessary to preserve the Republic — shaped by experience of chaos under the Articles, including Shays' Rebellion. "Insure domestic Tranquility" encodes propertied framers' fear of popular uprisings and democratic excess; they saw a stable national government as the essential guarantee against the instability of the Confederation period.

**Total: 6 pts**

### FRQ Part B — LEQ Summary

**Prompt:** *Evaluate the reasons for the United States victory in the American Revolutionary War.*

**"Evaluate the reasons" = take a position on WHICH factors were most decisive and HOW they interacted.**

**Strong thesis:** American victory resulted primarily from French military and financial intervention — which transformed a colonial insurgency into a global war Britain couldn't win — with Washington's attrition strategy as the necessary precondition that kept the Continental Army alive long enough for France to intervene.

**Evidence:**
| Factor | Specific Evidence |
|---|---|
| **French alliance** | Continental Army won no major battles in first 6 years; Battle of Saratoga (1777) proved Americans could win → Treaty of Alliance (1778); Siege of Yorktown required Admiral de Grasse's French fleet (blocked British escape) + 7,800 French troops under Rochambeau |
| **Washington's attrition strategy** | Chose strategic retreat over glorious defeat (abandoned New York, Philadelphia); surprise attack at Trenton (hired Hessians) kept hope alive; hired Von Steuben to discipline troops; guerrilla warfare in South (Francis Marion, Nathanael Greene's Carolinas campaign) denied Britain control of countryside even when holding cities |
| **British strategic limitations** | Fighting 3,000 miles away; Whig opposition in Parliament (Burke, Fox); after France entered, Britain diverted resources to Caribbean, Gibraltar, India; logistics of supplying army across Atlantic made sustained war increasingly expensive and politically unsustainable |

**Context:** British-French imperial rivalry from the Seven Years' War (France humiliated in 1763, eager to weaken Britain); France under Louis XVI saw American independence as a strategic tool regardless of ideological sympathy.

**Scoring:** Thesis (1) + Context (1) + Two specific evidence pieces (2) = **4 pts total**

**Weak thesis to avoid:** "Americans won because of French help, good leadership, and motivation." → That's a list. A line of reasoning argues *mechanism*: which factor was most decisive and how the others enabled or depended on it.
