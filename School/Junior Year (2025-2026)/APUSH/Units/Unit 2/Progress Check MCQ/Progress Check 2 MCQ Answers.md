## Stimulus Set 1: Questions 1–3

**Source:** Virginia DeJohn Anderson, "Migrants and Motives: Religion and the Settlement of New England, 1630–1640," *New England Quarterly*, 1985

**Excerpt summary:** Anderson contrasts New England and Chesapeake settler demographics. New England settlers more closely resembled the general English population (diverse ages, balanced sex ratio, family groups). Young adults flocked disproportionately to Chesapeake tobacco and sugar colonies — nearly half of Virginia residents in 1625 were aged 20–29, versus only a quarter in New England. Similarly, New England had near-equal male/female ratios from founding, while the Chesapeake ran 4:1 or 3:2 male-to-female well into the 17th century.

---

### Question 1

**Stem:** The first paragraph of the excerpt makes which of the following claims?

**Notes:** The first paragraph contrasts age distribution. Chesapeake skewed heavily toward young adults; New England's age range mirrored the general English population (wider spread). This means New England *varied in age more* than the Chesapeake.

**Key concept:** Demographic differences between New England and Chesapeake colonies (Unit 2 — colonial society and culture)

**Correct Answer: B — The settlers of New England varied in age more than emigrants to the Chesapeake did.**

The excerpt states that "most of these young adults made their way to the tobacco and sugar plantations of the South" while New England settlers "more closely resembled the non-migrating English population" — meaning New England had a broader, more representative age range. The Chesapeake was dominated by twentysomethings; New England had a more normal age distribution, which by definition means greater age variety.

**Wrong answer analysis:**
- **A (Chesapeake came as family groups more often):** Half-Right/Reversal — the opposite is true; New England had more family groups. The excerpt explicitly says New England's composition "ensured" successful transplantation of English family patterns.
- **C (Both were disproportionately young):** Half-Right — only the Chesapeake was disproportionately young. New England resembled the broader English population.
- **D (More young adults migrated to New England):** Reversal — the excerpt says most young adults went *south* to tobacco and sugar plantations, not to New England.

---

### Question 2

**Stem:** The second paragraph makes which claims about populations of men and women?

**Notes:** The excerpt says women were "scarce" in the Chesapeake (4–5 men per woman early on, still 3:2 by end of century) but "comparatively abundant" in New England, where "nearly half were women and girls." Near-equal sex ratios in New England from founding.

**Key concept:** Colonial demographic differences; family formation patterns

**Correct Answer: C — The populations of men and women in New England were roughly equal from the time of its founding.**

The excerpt directly states that women were "comparatively abundant in the northern colonies" and that "nearly half" of New England emigrants were women and girls — establishing near-parity from the outset, in sharp contrast to the Chesapeake's severe male skew.

**Wrong answer analysis:**
- **A (New England had more young men than Chesapeake):** Reversal — New England had fewer young men proportionally; Chesapeake was far more male-dominated.
- **B (Emigrants to New England were predominantly unmarried women):** Extreme Language — the excerpt says women were proportionally well-represented, not that they were the *majority* or predominantly unmarried.
- **D (Chesapeake achieved balanced sex ratio more quickly):** Reversal — the Chesapeake's sex ratio remained imbalanced (3 men per 2 women) even by end of the 17th century. New England had balance from founding.

---

### Question 3

**Stem:** Which best describes an overall argument of the excerpt?

**Notes:** The thesis of the excerpt is that New England's demographic makeup — family groups, age diversity, balanced sex ratios — enabled it to recreate English social patterns more fully than the Chesapeake's demographic composition (young males, few women, few families) allowed.

**Key concept:** Colonial regional differences; demographic determinism in colonial development

**Correct Answer: A — The makeup of emigrant populations led to greater reconstruction of English family life in New England than it did in the Chesapeake.**

The excerpt directly argues that "the character of the New England colonial population ensured" successful transplant of English patterns, while "the composition of the emigrant populations in the Chesapeake and the Carolinas *hindered* the successful transplant." This is the central comparative argument.

**Wrong answer analysis:**
- **B (British colonists were disproportionately young and male):** Half-Right — this was true of the *Chesapeake* but not New England. The excerpt's whole point is the *contrast* between regions.
- **C (Southern plantations attracted the most settlers):** True but Irrelevant — the excerpt mentions this as context, not as its main argument. The argument is about social consequences of demographic composition.
- **D (By end of 17th century, colonies resembled England):** Anachronistic/Distortion — the excerpt argues New England was demographically closer to England from the start; it's not making a claim about convergence over time.

### Question 4

**Stem:** The trend from 1660 to 1710 depicted in the table most strongly indicates which of the following?

**Notes:** The Black population exploded in every region — most dramatically in the Chesapeake (1,708 → 31,063, nearly 18x) and the West Indies (already huge, more than 4x). The Carolinas went from almost nothing to 5,000. This is the transatlantic slave trade scaling up.

**Key concept:** Transition from indentured servitude to chattel slavery; growth of the transatlantic slave trade in the late 17th–early 18th century

**Correct Answer: A — The rise of the trans-Atlantic trade of enslaved Africans.**

The dramatic multi-regional increase in Black (enslaved) population between 1660 and 1710, with the steepest increases in plantation regions (Chesapeake, West Indies, Carolinas), directly reflects the acceleration of the transatlantic slave trade during this period. Bacon's Rebellion (1676) accelerated the shift away from white indentured servants toward enslaved Africans; by 1710 that transition is visible in every column.

**Wrong answer analysis:**
- **B (Increase in indentured servitude):** Reversal — indentured servitude was *declining* during this period, replaced by chattel slavery. The table shows Black population growth, not indentured servant data.
- **C (Encomienda adopted from Spanish):** Anachronistic — the encomienda was a Spanish institution; English colonists did not adopt it. This is a fabricated connection.
- **D (People newly freed from slavery worked on plantations):** True but Irrelevant — this addresses individual post-freedom behavior, not the table's large-scale demographic trend of massive population increases.

---

### Question 5

**Stem:** The change in White and Black populations in the various regions most strongly suggests the influence of which of the following?

**Notes:** Both White and Black populations grew enormously everywhere. The Chesapeake's Black population grew 18x because of tobacco demand; the West Indies' Black population was already enormous and kept growing because of sugar. The correlation between plantation staple crops and enslaved population growth points directly to European commodity demand.

**Key concept:** Atlantic economy; tobacco and sugar driving enslaved labor demand

**Correct Answer: D — The demand in Europe for commodities such as tobacco and sugar.**

The regions with the largest enslaved populations (West Indies/sugar, Chesapeake/tobacco, Carolinas/rice) are exactly the regions producing the commodities most demanded by European consumers. European demand for these goods drove colonists to import more enslaved labor to maximize production, which explains both the scale and the regional distribution of population changes.

**Wrong answer analysis:**
- **A (Eventual decline of enslaved labor):** Reversal — the table shows enslaved labor *expanding* massively. This is a factual contradiction.
- **B (Growing resistance to British imperial control):** Too Broad/Irrelevant — colonial resistance to Britain is a later 18th-century trend; it doesn't explain population growth in 1660–1710.
- **C (Spread of epidemic diseases):** Reversal — epidemic diseases *reduced* populations, especially Native American populations. The table shows massive *growth*, which is inconsistent with epidemic die-offs.

### Question 6

**Stem:** The White and Black populations in New England shown in the table most directly suggest...

**Notes:** New England: White 1660 = 33,006 → 1710 = 115,094. Black 1660 = 562 → 1710 = 2,585. The Black population is a tiny fraction compared to Chesapeake (31,063) or West Indies (136,645). New England's economy was commercial/fishing/artisan, not plantation-based.

**Key concept:** Regional economic differences; absence of plantation agriculture in New England

**Correct Answer: C — Minimal use of enslaved labor in New England compared to other regions.**

New England's Black population of 2,585 in 1710 is dwarfed by the Chesapeake's 31,063 and the West Indies' 136,645. New England's economy — fishing, shipping, small-scale farming, artisan crafts — did not require the large forced labor force that tobacco and sugar plantations demanded, which explains both the small number and the slow growth rate of the enslaved population.

**Wrong answer analysis:**
- **A (Indentured servants moving from South to New England):** Fabricated — there is no evidence in the table for migration of indentured servants northward. This is an invented explanation.
- **B (Expansion of plantation agriculture to New England):** Reversal — the tiny enslaved population *contradicts* plantation expansion. New England's climate and geography made plantation agriculture impossible.
- **D (Spread of trans-Atlantic print culture):** True but Irrelevant — Puritan print culture is a real phenomenon but has nothing to do with population ratios between Black and White inhabitants.

## Stimulus Set 3: Questions 7–9

**Source:** Virginia Governor Alexander Spotswood, address to the Virginia House of Burgesses, 1713

**Excerpt summary:** Spotswood asks the House of Burgesses to support his plan for defending Virginia's western frontier. He argues that Allied Native Americans provide security but that rogue Rangers have attacked both settlers and allied Indians, creating dangerous instability. He requests legislative support to better organize and fund frontier defense.

---

### Question 7

**Stem:** Which best describes a purpose of the excerpt?

**Notes:** Spotswood is addressing the legislature (House of Burgesses), asking them to act on frontier defense. His goal is political and practical: get them to fund and approve his plan.

**Key concept:** Colonial governance; executive-legislative relations in colonial Virginia; frontier conflict

**Correct Answer: A — The Virginia governor is seeking support from the colonial legislature for his plan to address conflict between settlers and American Indians in frontier areas.**

Spotswood is literally speaking to the House of Burgesses and requesting that they support his frontier defense measures. Every sentence in the excerpt is framed as an appeal to the legislature — he describes the problem, explains the consequences, and asks for action.

**Wrong answer analysis:**
- **B (Warning American Indians of military action):** Wrong audience — Spotswood is speaking to the House of Burgesses, not to Native Americans. The excerpt is legislative advocacy, not a military ultimatum.
- **C (Seeking pardon for failure to protect settlers):** Misread — Spotswood is describing the problem and proposing solutions, not apologizing or seeking absolution.
- **D (Soliciting support of allied Indians):** Wrong audience again — he's addressing the legislature. He mentions allied Indians as part of his security strategy but isn't speaking to them.

---

### Question 8

**Stem:** The point of view expressed in the excerpt is that...

**Notes:** Spotswood presents himself as a governor responsible for the safety of Virginia's colonists. His concern is explicitly for "outward inhabitants" (frontier settlers). He frames everything in terms of security for White Virginians, though he also values allied Indians instrumentally (as security partners).

**Key concept:** Colonial governor's role; British imperial security concerns; point of view identification

**Correct Answer: D — As Governor, he feels a responsibility to protect White frontier settlers from violent encounters with American Indians.**

The excerpt is fundamentally about Spotswood's duty to provide security for frontier colonists. He describes attacks, proposes preventive measures, and asks the legislature to help him fulfill his governing responsibility. His concern for allied Indians is strategic, not moral — they are partners in frontier security, not the subject of his protection.

**Wrong answer analysis:**
- **A (Opposes westward expansion):** Reversal — Spotswood supports western expansion and wants to make it safer, not stop it. His plan is *for* expansion, not against it.
- **B (White Virginians must protect themselves):** Misread — Spotswood is arguing that *he* and the government must provide protection. He's not telling colonists to fend for themselves; he's asking the legislature to fund his security plan.
- **C (Values White and Native lives equally):** Extreme Language/Generous Reading — while Spotswood mentions allied Indian casualties, the whole framing is protecting White settlers. Claiming equal moral concern exceeds what the text supports.

---

### Question 9

**Stem:** Which groups would most likely oppose the ideas expressed in the excerpt?

**Notes:** Spotswood is asking the House of Burgesses to fund frontier defense. Those who benefit directly (frontier settlers, allied Indians) would support it. Those who have to pay for it but don't benefit — coastal Virginia settlers — would be most likely to oppose.

**Key concept:** Colonial political economy; competing interests within colonial society

**Correct Answer: B — White settlers in coastal Virginia.**

Coastal Virginians would be asked to support (through taxes or legislative approval) a frontier defense program that primarily benefits people living far away. This is a perennial dynamic in colonial politics: those far from the frontier resisted paying for its defense. Coastal elites in Virginia — the same class dominating the House of Burgesses — often viewed frontier defense spending as a drain on their resources for limited benefit.

**Wrong answer analysis:**
- **A (White settlers on the western Virginia frontier):** Reversal — frontier settlers would most directly benefit from Spotswood's plan and would strongly support it.
- **C (Members of allied American Indian groups):** Reversal — Spotswood explicitly includes allied Indians as partners whose safety he is also protecting; they would support his plan.
- **D (Members of the House of Burgesses):** Too Broad — the House of Burgesses was Spotswood's audience and his source of potential support; many members would be persuaded. "Coastal Virginia settlers" is more specific about who actually opposed.

## Stimulus Set 4: Questions 10–12

**Source:** Johann Martin Bolzius, German Lutheran minister, report on enslaved people in Georgia and Carolina, 1751

**Excerpt summary:** Bolzius describes the legal and social conditions of enslaved people in the Carolinas: prohibitions on racial mixing, strict runaway slave laws (nearby Spanish Florida offered freedom to some who escaped), harsh punishment regimes, and the status of mixed-race children born into perpetual slavery.

---

### Question 10

**Stem:** Which best represents a change in the labor force of the southern British colonies by the 1700s?

**Notes:** The Bolzius excerpt describes chattel slavery as the dominant labor system. By the 1700s, the transition from indentured servitude to enslaved African labor was complete in the South. Bacon's Rebellion (1676) accelerated this shift.

**Key concept:** Labor system evolution; transition from indentured servitude to chattel slavery (late 17th–early 18th century)

**Correct Answer: A — Slavery became more widespread than indentured servitude.**

By the time Bolzius wrote (1751), chattel slavery was the dominant labor institution in the southern colonies. The excerpt's description of elaborate runaway laws, racial mixing prohibitions, and hereditary slave status reflects a mature and legally entrenched slave system that had replaced the earlier reliance on white indentured servants.

**Wrong answer analysis:**
- **B (Rise of manufacturing):** Anachronistic — significant manufacturing in the South did not emerge until the 19th century. 18th-century southern colonial economy was agricultural.
- **C (Encomienda adopted from Spanish):** Fabricated — English colonists did not use the encomienda system. This is a distractor mixing Spanish and English colonial history.
- **D (People newly freed from slavery worked on plantations):** True but Irrelevant — this describes individual post-freedom behavior, not the broad labor system change the question asks about.

---

### Question 11

**Stem:** The experience of enslaved people in the southern colonies was similar to the experience of enslaved people in the northern British colonies because...

**Notes:** This question asks for a SIMILARITY between North and South regarding the enslaved experience. Despite regional differences in scale and type of labor, enslaved people everywhere developed strategies to resist: foot-dragging, sabotage, running away, forming communities, etc.

**Key concept:** Enslaved resistance strategies across colonial regions

**Correct Answer: B — Many enslaved people in both regions developed strategies to resist slavery.**

While the scale of slavery differed enormously (1.7% of New England population vs. 40%+ in South Carolina), enslaved people in both regions found ways to resist their bondage — running away, feigning illness, breaking tools, maintaining cultural practices, and petitioning courts. The Bolzius excerpt itself references runaways (those who fled to Spanish Florida), illustrating active resistance in the South, which paralleled resistance in the North.

**Wrong answer analysis:**
- **A (Recognized rights under colonial law):** Half-Right — some enslaved people brought freedom suits in court, especially in the North, but this was rare and not a shared general experience across regions.
- **C (Performed skilled labor in port cities):** Half-Right — this was true of *some* enslaved people in Northern port cities but was not the majority experience in either region, especially in the South where field labor dominated.
- **D (Opportunities to earn their freedom):** Half-Right — manumission existed in both regions but was uncommon and became increasingly restricted, especially in the South. This was not a shared general experience.

---

### Question 12

**Stem:** The reaction to the situation described in the third paragraph represented a continuity with which earlier colonial developments?

**Notes:** The third paragraph describes laws against racial mixing and the children of mixed-race unions being classified as "perpetual slaves like their mothers." This is the racially-based hereditary slave status established by laws like Virginia's 1662 *partus sequitur ventrem* statute.

**Key concept:** Development of racial slavery; colonial laws establishing hereditary slave status

**Correct Answer: A — A strict racial system was established that separated enslaved people from European colonists.**

The third paragraph's description of prohibitions on racial mixing and the enslaved status of mixed-race children reflects continuity with 17th-century laws that explicitly defined slavery by race and made it hereditary through the mother. This legal framework — established in Virginia, Maryland, and the Carolinas from the 1660s through 1690s — created the strict racial hierarchy that Bolzius is describing in 1751.

**Wrong answer analysis:**
- **B (Enslaved Native Americans incorporated into colonial society):** Irrelevant — the excerpt deals with African-descended enslaved people, and the legal mechanisms described are race-based (African/European), not about Native American labor.
- **C (Enlightenment principles arguing for racial equality):** Reversal — the excerpt describes laws *enforcing* racial inequality, not Enlightenment challenges to it.
- **D (Slavery transferred unaltered from West Africa):** Factually incorrect — colonial American chattel slavery was not transferred from West Africa; it was constructed by English colonists and lawmakers to serve their economic interests. African slavery had different forms and purposes.

## Stimulus Set 5: Questions 13–15

**Source:** Benjamin Franklin, *Autobiography*, describing George Whitefield's 1739 Philadelphia visit

**Excerpt summary:** Franklin describes Whitefield arriving from Ireland, preaching to enormous outdoor crowds, the construction of a nonsectarian meeting house for the new preachers, and the electrifying effect of Whitefield's oratory — including Franklin's famous account of emptying his pockets at the collection plate despite his skepticism.

---

### Question 13

**Stem:** Which most directly contributed to the events described in the excerpt?

**Notes:** Whitefield came from Ireland/Britain — "In 1739 arrived among us from Ireland the Reverend Mr. Whitefield." The Great Awakening was an Atlantic phenomenon, with evangelists like Whitefield crossing from Britain to spread revivalism.

**Key concept:** First Great Awakening as transatlantic Protestant revival movement

**Correct Answer: A — Protestant evangelism came to the colonies from Great Britain and Europe.**

Whitefield was a British evangelist whose preaching tour of the American colonies was part of a broader transatlantic evangelical movement that included Jonathan Edwards in America and John Wesley and George Whitefield in Britain. The excerpt explicitly places Whitefield as arriving from Ireland, confirming the transatlantic origin of the revival.

**Wrong answer analysis:**
- **B (Puritan leaders' increased impact):** Half-Right/Distortion — the Great Awakening actually *challenged* established Puritan orthodoxy. "New Light" revivalists like Whitefield competed with "Old Light" Congregationalists. Puritan clergy were often among the Great Awakening's critics.
- **C (Colonists became less interested in religion):** Reversal — Franklin's account describes exactly the opposite: massive religious enthusiasm and huge outdoor crowds hanging on Whitefield's sermons.
- **D (Local clergy welcomed diverse doctrines):** Reversal — many established clergy actively opposed revivalism. The building of a *new* meeting house (rather than using existing churches) reflects the fact that local clergy did NOT welcome Whitefield's preaching.

---

### Question 14

**Stem:** The events of the First Great Awakening illustrated by the excerpt led to...

**Notes:** The Great Awakening fragmented colonial religious life: "New Light" vs. "Old Light" divisions within Congregationalism, growth of Baptists and Methodists, challenge to established clergy's authority. It did NOT strengthen established clergy.

**Key concept:** Consequences of the First Great Awakening: denominational fragmentation, challenge to religious authority

**Correct Answer: B — New denominations attracting followers who were drawn to the dynamic sermons of the new preachers.**

The Great Awakening supercharged the growth of evangelical denominations — Baptists and Methodists especially — at the expense of established Congregationalist and Anglican churches. The nonsectarian meeting house described by Franklin is itself an example of institutional alternatives arising outside established denominations to serve the revivalist movement.

**Wrong answer analysis:**
- **A (Established clergy strengthening control):** Reversal — the Great Awakening directly challenged established clergy. "New Light" preachers bypassed church hierarchies; "Old Light" clergy denounced revivalism as enthusiasm and disorder.
- **C (Religious enthusiasm declining in cities):** Reversal — Franklin's description of massive Philadelphia crowds is the opposite of decline. Cities were often centers of Great Awakening activity.
- **D (Few changes beyond increased church attendance):** Extreme Understatement — the Great Awakening fundamentally altered colonial religious life, challenged clerical authority, contributed to democratic sensibilities, and set the stage for later political mobilization.

---

### Question 15

**Stem:** Which most directly contributed to the decision in Philadelphia to build a specific meeting house for the new preachers?

**Notes:** Pennsylvania was founded by William Penn on Quaker principles of religious tolerance. By the 1730s it was the most religiously diverse colony in British North America — Quakers, Presbyterians, German Reformed, Lutherans, Mennonites, Catholics, and Jews all coexisted. This tolerance made Philadelphia a natural place for a nonsectarian evangelical meeting house.

**Key concept:** Religious pluralism in the middle colonies; Penn's "holy experiment"

**Correct Answer: C — Religious pluralism was more accepted in the middle colonies and particularly in the colony of Pennsylvania than elsewhere.**

Pennsylvania's founding principle of religious freedom meant that no established church held enough power to block Whitefield's preaching or the construction of a new meeting house. Franklin notes that the building was "expressly for the use of any preacher of any religious persuasion" — a nonsectarian design that could only have been built in the religiously tolerant environment of Pennsylvania.

**Wrong answer analysis:**
- **A (Leaders wanted to limit spread of new denominations):** Reversal — the construction of an open meeting house was an act of *welcome*, not limitation. If authorities wanted to limit Whitefield, they would not have built him a building.
- **B (Central meeting house for censorship):** Unfounded — Franklin gives no indication that the house was built for surveillance. This is a conspiracy-minded interpretation without textual support.
- **D (Commerce more important; keeping preachers isolated):** Internally contradictory — if commerce were paramount and preachers inconvenient, building them a dedicated venue makes no sense. Franklin's account shows genuine enthusiasm, not marginalization.

## Standalone Questions

### Question 16

**Stem:** Which was the most important reason that Native American relations with English settlers differed from Native American relations with other groups of European settlers in the 1600s?

**Notes:** The French maintained fur-trade relationships with Native Americans with relatively small settler populations; the Spanish used the encomienda system but intermarried more. The English came in large numbers, settled permanently, displaced Native peoples from land, and had less use for them as trading partners. Settlement-scale land dispossession was the defining difference.

**Key concept:** Comparative colonialism; English vs. French vs. Spanish relations with Native Americans

**Correct Answer: A — Larger numbers of English colonists settled on land taken from Native Americans.**

The English colonial project was fundamentally one of *settlement* — permanent displacement of existing populations — rather than the *trade-and-convert* model of the French or the *conquest-and-extract* model of the Spanish. The sheer volume of English settlers demanding land led to repeated violent conflicts (Powhatan Wars, King Philip's War, etc.) at a scale that French or Spanish relations did not reach in this period.

**Wrong answer analysis:**
- **B (English more technologically advanced):** Factually weak — all European powers had comparable military technology. This does not explain the *relational* difference the question asks about.
- **C (Native Americans understood English better):** Factually false — there is no basis for claiming Native Americans were more fluent in English than French or Spanish.
- **D (Eastern Seaboard provided fewer opportunities for conflict):** Reversal — the opposite is true. Coastal settlement brought English and Native peoples into direct, sustained competition over land.

### Question 17

**Stem:** English colonists in North America in the 1600s and 1700s most typically sought which of the following?

**Notes:** The overwhelming majority of English colonists came for economic opportunity — land, trade, escaping poverty. Even those fleeing religious persecution (Puritans, Quakers) were simultaneously motivated by economic improvement.

**Key concept:** Primary motivations of English colonization; "better living conditions" as the dominant pull factor

**Correct Answer: D — Opportunities to improve their living conditions.**

Whether measured by indentured servants hoping to farm their own land, merchants seeking trade profits, younger sons of gentry hoping for land ownership denied them at home, or poor families seeking escape from English poverty, the dominant motivation for English colonial migration was economic improvement. Even religious migrants like the Puritans were seeking a better material and spiritual life, not just spiritual purity.

**Wrong answer analysis:**
- **A (Spread Enlightenment ideas to Native Americans):** Anachronistic — the Enlightenment was not a major force in English colonial thinking in the early 1600s–1700s. Missionary work was more a Spanish/French motivation than English.
- **B (Jobs in factories):** Anachronistic — factories did not exist in colonial North America. The Industrial Revolution was late 18th–19th century.
- **C (Political independence from England):** Anachronistic — the overwhelming majority of colonists in the 1600s and early 1700s considered themselves loyal British subjects. Independence as a goal emerged only in the 1760s–1770s.

### Question 18

**Stem:** The efforts of Spanish colonists to convert Native Americans to Christianity were most directly influenced by which of the following simultaneous developments?

**Notes:** Spanish colonization = religious conversion + economic extraction. These were intertwined: the encomienda system extracted labor from Native peoples, who were supposed to be converted. The extraction of silver (Potosí) and gold was the economic engine; missionary work provided moral and legal justification.

**Key concept:** Relationship between Spanish economic extraction and missionary activity; encomienda and religious conversion as complementary systems

**Correct Answer: B — The extraction of gold and other wealth from the land in the Americas.**

Spanish missions and the encomienda system were parallel institutions that reinforced each other. Native peoples had to be kept alive, organized, and submissive to work the mines and plantations; conversion provided ideological control and legal justification for colonial authority. The economic imperative of extraction directly shaped the urgency and methods of conversion.

**Wrong answer analysis:**
- **A (Columbian Exchange effects on European population):** Too Broad/Indirect — demographic changes in Europe don't directly motivate Spanish missionaries in America. This is too removed from the missionary enterprise itself.
- **C (Arrival of English colonists seeking religious freedom):** Anachronistic/Wrong Region — English religious colonization (Plymouth 1620, Massachusetts Bay 1630) came after the peak of Spanish missionary activity and was geographically separate from Spanish territory.
- **D (Success of French and Dutch with Native Americans):** True but Irrelevant — French and Dutch trade relations don't explain Spanish *conversion* efforts, which were driven by Catholic religious imperatives and colonial economic logic, not competition with Protestant powers' Native relations.
