The LEQ asks you to construct a full argumentative essay. Scored on: (1) a historically defensible thesis that establishes a line of reasoning — not a list; (2) broader historical context relevant to the prompt; and (3) at least two specific pieces of evidence supporting your argument. No documents are provided.

---

## Prompt
> Evaluate the extent to which imperial goals of the French and of the British fostered differences in their relationships with American Indians in the period from 1607 to 1754.

**Notes section:** Unit 2 — Colonial Society and Expansion (1607–1754)

**What this prompt is asking:** "Evaluate the extent to which" = take a position on HOW MUCH imperial goals caused the differences. You're not just comparing French and British Native relations; you're arguing that imperial goals were (or were not, or were partially) the *cause* of those differences. A strong thesis will argue *to what degree* and *in what way* imperial goals drove the distinctions.

---

## Full Answer

**Thesis (line of reasoning):**
Imperial goals largely drove the differences between French and British relations with American Indians in the period from 1607 to 1754: France's reliance on the fur trade and its thin settler population led to alliances, intermarriage, and diplomatic partnerships with Native peoples, while Britain's land-hunger settlement model brought its colonists into direct, violent competition with Native nations over territory — a contrast rooted not in cultural attitudes but in the fundamentally different economic and demographic imperatives of each empire.

**Broader historical context:**
By the early 17th century, both France and England were entering a period of intensified European competition for North American territory, following Spanish dominance of the 16th century. The configuration of each empire in North America was shaped partly by geography and partly by economic strategy. France had established a foothold through the St. Lawrence River valley and the Great Lakes basin — ideal for fur trade networks but poorly suited to mass agricultural settlement. England, by contrast, entered North America along the Atlantic seaboard, where fertile agricultural land was available and where large-scale immigration of landless peasants and younger sons created constant pressure for territorial expansion. These divergent entry points and economic models set the terms under which each empire would interact with Native peoples for the next century and a half.

**Evidence 1 — French fur trade dependency and Native alliance building:**
France's imperial goals in North America were built around the fur trade, which required Native expertise, labor, and ongoing cooperation. French traders could not harvest beaver pelts without Native hunters and trappers; French empire in the interior depended entirely on maintaining good relations with nations like the Huron, Algonquin, Ottawa, and later the Illinois. This dependency produced a distinctive French approach: traders like Samuel de Champlain cultivated military alliances with Native peoples, joined them in wars against common enemies (most famously fighting against the Iroquois Confederacy alongside the Huron), and French missionaries developed language skills and entered Native communities rather than demanding submission. French men frequently married Native women in fur-trade partnerships, producing the *Métis* mixed-ancestry population across the Great Lakes region. The French did not need Native land — they needed Native labor and loyalty. Their imperial goal (extracting furs) produced cooperation rather than displacement.

**Evidence 2 — British settlement model and land dispossession:**
British imperial goals were fundamentally different: the Crown and colonial proprietors needed settlers on land, not trading partners in the interior. The Virginia Company, the Massachusetts Bay Company, and colonial proprietors like those of Maryland and Carolina all operated on the premise that colonial success meant permanent agricultural settlement, which required acquiring land from Native peoples. The Ohio Company's 1748 petition requesting 500,000 acres west of the Appalachians illustrates how deeply the British imperial model was built around land acquisition. This settlement pressure brought British colonists into direct competition with Native peoples who used the same territories for hunting, agriculture, and habitation. The result was a cycle of dispossession and conflict: the Powhatan Wars in Virginia (1610s–1640s), King Philip's War in New England (1675–1676), and the Yamasee War in Carolina (1715–1717) were all products of Native resistance to British land encroachment. Governor Spotswood's 1713 address to the Virginia House of Burgesses — appealing for frontier defense — reflects how chronic this conflict was, and how the British imperial system of settlement continually generated violent friction with Native peoples.

**Complication / Extent qualification:**
It is worth noting that imperial goals did not fully determine every aspect of these relationships — specific geographic and political contexts mattered too. The British colonists in the Carolinas and New York fur trade *did* develop significant trade partnerships with Native peoples (particularly the Iroquois Confederacy), showing that where British imperial interests temporarily aligned with Native cooperation rather than land seizure, more diplomatic relationships were possible. Similarly, French imperialism was not without conflict — the French fought extended wars against the Iroquois Confederacy throughout the 17th century. But these were exceptions that prove the rule: French-Iroquois conflict arose because the Iroquois competed directly with French trade networks, while British-Iroquois diplomacy in New York emerged specifically when the Crown needed Native military allies against the French. In both cases, the *character* of the relationship was determined by what each empire needed from Native peoples to achieve its imperial goals.

**Note on the thesis:** Saying "French and British relations with Native Americans were different because of their imperial goals" is too thin — it just restates the prompt. A strong thesis takes a position on the *mechanism* and *degree*: arguing that the fur trade model vs. the settlement model was the *primary* driver, or that imperial goals almost entirely explain the difference (with a specific exception noted). The essay above argues the difference was *largely* explained by imperial economic goals (fur trade vs. land settlement), with a qualification that local circumstances created exceptions.
