## Study Hub Quick Reference — Unit 2

> [!check] MCQ Pattern — Chesapeake vs. New England
> AP constantly contrasts these two regions. Chesapeake: tobacco economy, indentured servants → enslaved labor, mortality-driven instability, large plantations, cash-crop monoculture, Church of England. New England: Puritan religious community, town meetings, subsistence farming + fishing + trade, longer lives, more stable families. Know both cold.

> [!check] MCQ Pattern — Bacon's Rebellion → Shift to Slavery
> Bacon's Rebellion (1676) is a pivotal cause the AP loves. Frustrated indentured servants (poor whites) joined Nathaniel Bacon against the colonial elite. After the rebellion, planters shifted to enslaved African labor — enslaved people couldn't vote, organize, or gain freedom after a term. It directly accelerated the slave codes.

> [!warning] Trap — Puritans Were Not Tolerant
> "Puritans fled persecution and supported religious freedom" is wrong. They wanted freedom *for themselves*, not for others. Massachusetts Bay expelled Roger Williams (→ Rhode Island) and Anne Hutchinson for theological dissent. The colony enforced religious conformity.

> [!warning] Trap — Salutary Neglect Is Not Unit 3
> Salutary neglect (Britain's policy of loosely enforcing trade laws, leaving colonies largely self-governing) begins in this period. Its end after 1763 is what causes Unit 3's imperial crisis. Understand it here to explain the Unit 3 reaction.

## Heimler's History — Key Frameworks
**Unit 2 Full Review Video:** [APUSH Unit 2 REVIEW (Period 2: 1607–1754)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t0wIU4R0MhI)

**Heimler's big idea:** First half = compare how the four major colonial empires (Spanish, French, Dutch, British) each developed *differently* based on their different goals. Second half = the British colonies themselves grew increasingly tense with imperial Britain over territory, self-rule, and trade.

### Comparing Colonial Empires — "Comparison Pants" Framework
Heimler's memorable summary of each empire's posture toward the New World:

| Empire | Heimler's metaphor | What they actually did |
|---|---|---|
| **Spain** | *Grabbed* the New World | Imposed social order; encomienda/hacienda for labor; casta system for hierarchy; Catholic missions for conversion and control |
| **France** | *Shook hands with* the New World | Fur trade required Native expertise and cooperation; traded, intermarried (*Métis* population), sent missionaries but rarely used brutal force; sent relatively few colonists |
| **Dutch** | *Shook hands with* the New World | Like France: primarily economic (fur trade); accommodating with Natives; no conversion agenda; New Amsterdam had 12+ languages spoken in its streets |
| **British** | *Shoved away* the New World | Came in large numbers to create *entirely separate* English society; settlement model required land → direct competition with Natives; less use for Natives than French/Dutch |

**Hacienda replacing encomienda (Unit 2 addition to Unit 1 content):**
- In the **encomienda**: encomenderos controlled Native labor but did NOT own the land
- In the **hacienda**: plantation owner *did* own the land; indigenous laborers tied to it through debt repayment — a coerced but somewhat "reformed" system

**Pueblo Revolt of 1680 — the key Native resistance example for Spanish colonies:**
1. Spanish missionaries tried to convert Pueblo people — many Pueblo adapted by adding Christian beliefs to their own (**syncretism**)
2. Spanish priests declared this unacceptable and began destroying Pueblo cultural practices; killed several medicine men
3. Pueblo responded: killed hundreds of Spaniards, destroyed every church in the area, reestablished their own customs
4. Spain returned ~12 years later and subdued the Pueblo again with military force
5. **AP point:** Spain tried to impose culture wholesale; Natives were not passive recipients — they resisted, sometimes successfully

### Four Reasons the British Came — Heimler's Framework
1. **Social mobility** — English *primogeniture* laws gave land only to the oldest son; younger sons had no inheritance → looked to colonies for land the home country denied them
2. **Economic prosperity** — Jamestown settlers wanted gold so badly they forgot to plant crops and starved; only tobacco cultivation (John Rolfe) made them profitable
3. **Religious freedom** — Puritans wanted to purify the Church of England from within; Separatists/Pilgrims thought the Church was too corrupt and fled entirely
4. **Improved living conditions** — 17th-century England faced population growth + the **enclosure movement** (wealthy landowners fencing off public grazing land) → growing class of landless poor looked to colonies for a better material life

### Regional Identity Building — British Colonial Comparison
- New England (Puritans/Pilgrims): families, religious community, town meetings, cold climate/rocky soil = subsistence farming + fishing; longer lives, stable family structures
- Chesapeake (Virginia/Maryland): young single men seeking quick wealth; tobacco monoculture; headright → indentured servants → slavery; mortality-driven instability
- Middle Colonies (NY, NJ, PA, DE): most diverse region — religious tolerance (Penn's "holy experiment"), German + Scots-Irish immigration, grain farming + trade
- Southern Colonies (NC, SC, GA): rice/indigo in Carolina; enslaved majority by 1708 in SC; Georgia initially banned slavery then reversed

### Three Growing Tensions That Set Up Unit 3 — Heimler's Bridge
1. **Territorial:** Colonists wanted to migrate west into the Ohio River Valley; Britain worried this would reignite Native conflicts AND provoke France (it did → French and Indian War)
2. **Self-rule:** Britain's habit of **impressment** — forcing American men to serve in the Royal Navy; **1747 Boston riot** (3 days) resisting impressment for King George's War shows colonists saw this as a violation of their natural rights as British subjects
3. **Trade:** Navigation Acts restricted colonial trade to Britain only; colonists began **smuggling** to Dutch and other buyers; worked fine under salutary neglect, but when Britain started enforcing → massive resentment

### Anglicanization of the Colonies
By end of this period, British colonies were starting to resemble England more:
- Wealthy merchant class in NE + Middle Colonies resembled English nobility in customs and social dominance
- Landowning elite planters in the South dominated local politics
- All colonies shared same basic gov structure: governor + 2-house legislature — mirroring Parliament's structure (though more locally representative)
- This resemblance to Britain is why colonists believed they deserved the same rights as English subjects — which is the ideological fuel for the coming revolution

## Chesapeake Colonies (Virginia and Maryland)

- **Jamestown** (1607): first permanent English settlement; Virginia Company joint-stock venture; early years catastrophic (Starving Time, 1609–10)
- **Tobacco economy**: John Rolfe introduced marketable tobacco (1612); transformed Virginia from struggling settlement to profitable colony
- **Headright system**: 50 acres per person brought to Virginia → incentivized importing laborers; initially filled with **indentured servants** from England
- **Indentured servants**: contracted labor for 4–7 years in exchange for passage; upon freedom, received land, tools, clothes — but little land was available by mid-1600s → growing class of resentful poor whites
- **Bacon's Rebellion (1676)**: Nathaniel Bacon led poor farmers and freed servants against Governor Berkeley's government; attacked Native villages and burned Jamestown; Bacon died of dysentery, rebellion collapsed, but its warning was heard — planters began shifting to enslaved African labor
- **African slavery**: began arriving 1619 (first recorded Africans in Virginia); initially some had quasi-servant status; **slave codes** (1660s–1680s) formalized permanent, hereditary, race-based slavery
- Maryland: founded 1632 by Lord Baltimore (Catholic); **Act of Toleration (1649)** protected all Christians — but later revoked

---

## New England Colonies

- **Separatists/Pilgrims**: Plymouth Colony (1620); Mayflower Compact — early self-governance precedent
- **Puritans**: Massachusetts Bay Colony (1630); John Winthrop's "city upon a hill" sermon; theocratic government; General Court (elected legislature) — early representative body
- **Dissenters expelled**:
  - **Roger Williams**: challenged Puritan land rights and separation of church/state → founded Providence, Rhode Island (1636); earliest colony with religious tolerance
  - **Anne Hutchinson**: antinomianism (grace alone, not deeds); challenged male clergy authority → expelled 1638, went to Rhode Island
- **Halfway Covenant (1662)**: allowed partial church membership for unconverted children of members; reflected declining religious intensity (jeremiad tradition)
- **King Philip's War (1675–76)**: Wampanoag leader Metacom (King Philip) led war against English settlements; bloodiest per capita conflict in American history; ended with Native defeat and collapse of Native power in New England
- Economy: subsistence farming, fishing, timber, trade (triangular trade routes); no dominant cash crop

---

## Middle Colonies

- New York (former Dutch New Netherland, seized 1664), New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware
- **Pennsylvania**: founded by **William Penn** (Quaker, 1681); advertised religious tolerance; attracted diverse European immigrants (Germans, Scots-Irish, Dutch)
- **Quakers**: believed in inner light of God in all people; pacifists; opposed slavery (first organized antislavery protests in America)
- Most ethnically and religiously diverse region; also most economically diverse (grain farming, trade, crafts)

---

## Southern Colonies

- **Carolina** (split into North/South Carolina, 1712) and **Georgia** (1733, founded by James Oglethorpe as buffer against Spanish Florida and refuge for debtors — though debtor-haven plan quickly abandoned)
- South Carolina: rice and indigo economy; heavily enslaved workforce; slave majority by 1708; **Stono Rebellion (1739)** — largest slave uprising in colonial America; led to harsher slave codes
- Georgia: initially banned slavery; reversed by 1750

---

## The Development of Slavery

- Triangular trade: New England rum → Africa (traded for enslaved people) → Caribbean/colonies (traded for molasses) → New England
- **Middle Passage**: horrific transatlantic crossing; 10–15% mortality; enslaved people packed in inhumane conditions
- African cultural retention: enslaved people maintained languages, religions (syncretized with Christianity), music, family structures despite attempts to destroy them
- **Gullah-Geechee culture** (South Carolina, Georgia): preserved African linguistic and cultural elements most completely due to geographic isolation
- Slave codes created permanent, hereditary, race-based slavery: status followed the mother (partus sequitur ventrem)

---

## Colonial Society and Culture

- **Great Awakening (1730s–1740s)**: evangelical religious revival; preachers like **Jonathan Edwards** ("Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God") and **George Whitefield**; challenged established church authority; democratized religion; first truly inter-colonial cultural event
- **Enlightenment**: rationalist philosophy from Europe; Benjamin Franklin embodied American Enlightenment — practical science, self-improvement, reason; influenced founders' political thinking
- **Colonial assemblies**: each colony developed a representative legislature; by 1750, assemblies controlled taxation and spending; set precedent for self-governance
- **Salutary neglect**: British policy of loosely enforcing trade laws (Navigation Acts); colonies grew accustomed to self-governance; when Britain ended this policy after 1763, conflict followed

### FRQ Part A — SAQ Summary

**SAQ 1 — Ohio Company Petition (Hanbury, 1748)**
**(a) ONE cause of the development depicted [1 pt]:**
End of War of Austrian Succession (1748) temporarily reduced Anglo-French conflict → British merchants pressed westward expansion before France consolidated. OR: Persistent commercial interests in Ohio Valley agricultural/fur trade potential.

**(b) ONE argument made in the petition [1 pt]:**
Ohio Company argues establishing British settlements + a fort west of the Appalachians would extend the king's empire, increase royal revenue, and allow British goods to undercut French traders with Natives — framing private land speculation as imperial service to the Crown.

**(c) ONE similarity between British and French Native American relations [1 pt]:**
Both used trade relationships with Natives as a tool of imperial expansion — Ohio Company petition references supplying Natives with British goods, just as French traders used the fur trade to maintain alliances. In both cases, commercial exchange served colonial imperial goals rather than mutual benefit.

**SAQ 2 — Winthrop's Justification (1629)**
**(a) Historical situation in which produced [1 pt]:**
1629 — eve of Puritan Great Migration to Massachusetts Bay Colony (1630–40); Puritans faced religious/political pressure under Charles I and Archbishop Laud enforcing Anglican conformity. Winthrop was pre-justifying Puritan settlement of New England against the objection that colonists had no right to take land already inhabited by Natives.

**(b) ONE argument made [1 pt]:**
Natives had only a "natural right" (unimproved common use) to land, not a "civil right" (legal title through enclosure/cultivation) — English agricultural society's definition of property meant Natives had no legal claim, so English colonists who would improve and enclose the land could legitimately settle it. This reframed dispossession as legally and morally neutral.

**(c) ONE historical effect [1 pt]:**
Dispossession of coastal NE Native peoples → King Philip's War (1675–1676), in which Wampanoag, Nipmuc, and Narragansett mounted major armed resistance; conflict resulted in near-destruction of Native political power in southern NE and acceleration of English territorial control over the entire region.

**Total: 6 pts**

### FRQ Part B — LEQ Summary

**Prompt:** *Evaluate the extent to which imperial goals of the French and of the British fostered differences in their relationships with American Indians in the period from 1607 to 1754.*

**"Evaluate the extent to which" = take a position on HOW MUCH imperial goals caused the differences.**

**Strong thesis:** Imperial goals *largely* drove the differences — France's fur trade reliance led to alliance/intermarriage; Britain's land-settlement model brought direct violent competition over territory. The contrast was rooted in different economic and demographic imperatives, not cultural attitudes.

**Evidence:**
| Side | Specific Evidence |
|---|---|
| **French** | Fur trade required Native hunters/cooperation → Champlain cultivated military alliances, joined Native wars (vs. Iroquois alongside Huron), French men intermarried (*Métis* population), missionaries learned languages rather than applying brutal force |
| **British** | Virginia Company/colonial proprietors needed land, not trading partners; Ohio Company 1748 petition = 500,000 acres; Powhatan Wars (VA, 1610s–40s), King Philip's War (NE, 1675–76), Yamasee War (Carolina, 1715–17) = cycle of land dispossession and resistance |

**Complication (extent qualification):** British in New York and Carolinas *did* develop trade partnerships with Iroquois when needed; French *did* fight the Iroquois when they threatened fur trade networks. Exceptions prove the rule: character of each relationship was determined by what each empire *needed* from Natives to meet its goals.

**Scoring:** Thesis (1) + Context (1) + Two specific evidence pieces (2) = **4 pts total**
