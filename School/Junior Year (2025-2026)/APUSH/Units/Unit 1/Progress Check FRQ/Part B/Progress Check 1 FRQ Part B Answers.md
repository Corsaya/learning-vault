# Unit 1 — Progress Check FRQ Part B Answers
**Period: 1491–1607**

---

## FRQ Type: LEQ (Long Essay Question)
The LEQ asks you to construct a full argumentative essay in response to a historical prompt. It is scored on three criteria: (1) a historically defensible thesis that establishes a line of reasoning — not just restating the prompt; (2) describing a broader historical context relevant to the prompt; and (3) providing at least two specific pieces of evidence that support your argument. No documents are provided — this is entirely from your own knowledge.

---

## Prompt
> Evaluate the reasons why European powers sought to establish colonies in the Americas in the period from 1492 to 1607.

**Notes section:** Unit 1 — European Exploration and Colonization (1491–1607)

**What this prompt is asking:** You need to take a position on *why* Europeans colonized the Americas — not just list reasons, but argue which were most important or how they interacted. "Evaluate" means weigh and prioritize, not just describe.

---

## Full Answer

**Thesis (line of reasoning):**
European powers sought to establish colonies in the Americas primarily for economic motives — the pursuit of wealth, trade goods, and land — but these goals were intertwined with religious and strategic competition among European states that made colonization a matter of national prestige and security as well as profit.

**Broader historical context:**
Before 1492, European states were seeking new trade routes to Asia to bypass Ottoman-controlled overland routes that had driven up the cost of spices and luxury goods. The Portuguese had pioneered Atlantic exploration along the African coast, and Spain's sponsorship of Columbus reflected this competitive pressure. The Protestant Reformation beginning in 1517 added religious urgency to colonization, as Catholic powers (Spain, France) saw overseas expansion as a means of spreading Christianity, and later Protestant powers (England, the Netherlands) challenged Catholic dominance in the Americas.

**Evidence 1 — Spanish economic motives (encomienda and silver):**
Spain's colonization was driven overwhelmingly by the extraction of wealth. The conquest of the Aztec Empire (1521) and Inca Empire (1532) yielded enormous quantities of gold and silver, and the encomienda system was designed to extract labor and tribute from Native populations to support that extraction. By the mid-1500s, Potosí silver was flowing across the Atlantic and reshaping the European economy. Richard Hakluyt's *Discourse on Western Planting* (1584) explicitly framed English colonization in the same terms — colonies as a source of commodities England currently had to buy from other nations.

**Evidence 2 — Religious justification and missionary activity:**
European powers, especially Spain, framed colonization in religious terms. The *Requerimiento* required Spanish conquistadors to read a legal demand that Native peoples submit to the Catholic Church and the Spanish crown or face justified war. Friars like Pedro de Gante wrote to the Spanish emperor arguing that the entire enterprise was justified by the goal of converting Native peoples — even while critiquing the brutal labor conditions that made conversion impossible. This religious framing was both sincere and politically useful: it legitimized conquest in the eyes of the Church and provided cover for economic extraction.

**Evidence 3 — Inter-European competition:**
Hakluyt's argument also reflects a third driver: strategic competition among European powers. He warns that if England doesn't colonize, "the French that swarm with a multitude of people, or other nations, might secretly fortify and settle themselves before us." The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) dividing the non-European world between Spain and Portugal shows how early this competition was formalized — and England, France, and the Netherlands all entered the Americas in part to prevent rivals from monopolizing Atlantic trade and territory.

**Note on the thesis:** Saying "Europeans colonized for economic, religious, and political reasons" is not a thesis — it's a list. A thesis takes a position on *which mattered most* or *how they interacted*. The answer above argues that economic motives were primary but were reinforced and complicated by religion and inter-European competition — that's a line of reasoning, not just a list.
