## Study Hub Quick Reference — Unit 4

> [!check] MCQ Pattern — Market Revolution Transformations
> The Market Revolution changed the U.S. economy from subsistence farming to commercial production. MCQ stimuli often show images of canals, railroads, or factory towns and ask about economic or social effects. Key effects: rise of wage labor, specialization, growth of middle class, changed gender roles ("cult of domesticity"), urbanization.

> [!check] MCQ Pattern — Jacksonian Democracy Tensions
> Jackson is always contradictory: expanded white male democracy BUT removed Native peoples, supported slavery, fought the national bank, and used executive power aggressively. AP loves to ask about this tension. Don't paint Jackson as simply a "man of the people."

> [!warning] Trap — Second Great Awakening Causes vs. Effects
> The Second Great Awakening is the *cause* of most reform movements. Questions about abolitionism, temperance, women's rights, or prison reform often trace back to it. Answer "caused by" with Second Great Awakening religious perfectionism.

> [!warning] Trap — Missouri Compromise Dates and Terms
> Missouri entered as a slave state; Maine entered as a free state; slavery prohibited north of 36°30' latitude (except Missouri). Don't confuse it with the Compromise of 1850 (which is Unit 5). The Missouri Compromise is 1820.

> [!danger] Critical — Nullification Crisis
> South Carolina (led by Calhoun) declared the Tariff of 1828 ("Tariff of Abominations") and Tariff of 1832 null and void within the state. Jackson threatened military force and pushed the Force Bill. Compromise tariff resolved it. This is a direct preview of secession logic — the AP draws a line from nullification doctrine (1798 Virginia/Kentucky Resolutions) → Nullification Crisis → Civil War.

## Early National Period (1800–1815)

- **Revolution of 1800**: Jefferson's peaceful transfer of power; "all republicans, all federalists" inaugural address; reduced federal spending, paid off debt, but kept Hamilton's bank
- **Marbury v. Madison (1803)**: John Marshall established **judicial review** — the power of the Supreme Court to declare laws unconstitutional; most significant court decision in U.S. history
- **Louisiana Purchase (1803)**: Napoleon sold Louisiana Territory to Jefferson for $15 million; doubled U.S. size; Jefferson agonized over constitutionality (strict construction) but bought it anyway; Lewis and Clark Expedition (1804–06) mapped it
- **Embargo Act (1807)**: Jefferson banned all U.S. exports to punish Britain and France for attacking American ships; backfired — devastated American merchants and farmers; repealed 1809
- **War of 1812 (1812–15)**: "Second War of Independence"; causes: British impressment of American sailors, British support for Native resistance (Tecumseh), War Hawks (Henry Clay, John Calhoun) wanted Canada
  - **Results**: Treaty of Ghent (1814) — status quo ante bellum (nothing changed); BUT Americans felt victorious; nationalism surged; British stopped arming Natives; American manufacturing grew (due to trade disruption); Native resistance east of Mississippi largely broken

## Era of Good Feelings and Nationalism (1815–1825)

- One-party system after Federalists collapsed; Democratic-Republicans dominated
- **Henry Clay's American System**: protective tariffs (revenue + protect industry); national bank (stable currency); internal improvements (roads, canals) — federal investment in infrastructure; not all passed due to states' rights opposition
- **Erie Canal (1825)**: connected Great Lakes to New York City via Hudson River; dramatically reduced shipping costs; made New York dominant commercial city; model for canal boom
- **McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)**: Marshall Court ruled states couldn't tax the national bank; affirmed implied powers (elastic clause) and federal supremacy
- **Gibbons v. Ogden (1824)**: Congress alone controls interstate commerce (commerce clause)
- **Monroe Doctrine (1823)**: Western Hemisphere closed to new European colonization; U.S. would view European intervention as a threat; European powers largely ignored it at the time but it became foundational foreign policy

## The Market Revolution (1815–1848)

**Transportation Revolution:**
- Canals (Erie Canal, 1825), National Road (Cumberland Road), steamboats (Robert Fulton, 1807), railroads (1830s–40s)
- Connected farmers to markets; goods could travel cheaply; cities grew at transportation hubs

**Industrial Revolution:**
- **Lowell System**: factory towns in Massachusetts hired young farm women; company-owned boarding houses; long hours; paternalistic control; workers organized early strikes (1830s)
- Wage labor replaced indentured servitude and artisan craft production
- Putting-out system → centralized factory production

**Social Effects:**
- **Cult of Domesticity**: middle-class ideology separating "public sphere" (men, work) from "private sphere" (women, home, morality); "True Woman" — piety, purity, submissiveness, domesticity
- Wealthy women had more leisure → joined reform movements; Working-class women had to work despite ideology
- Growth of urban middle class (managers, professionals, clerks)
- Immigration increased (Irish and German, 1840s–50s) → nativist backlash (Know-Nothing Party later)

## Second Great Awakening and Reform Movements

**Second Great Awakening (1800s–1840s):**
- Evangelical Protestant revival; camp meetings; emphasized that anyone could be saved through conversion and right behavior ("perfectionism")
- Challenged Calvinist predestination; democratized religion
- Key figures: Charles Finney (most prominent revivalist); Peter Cartwright
- The SGA is the **primary cause** of virtually all antebellum reform movements

**Reform Movements:**

| Movement | Key Features | Key People/Events |
|---|---|---|
| **Temperance** | Opposed alcohol as source of poverty, domestic abuse, moral decay; American Temperance Society (1826) | Lyman Beecher; later leads to 18th Amendment (1919) |
| **Abolitionism** | Immediate emancipation; moral argument against slavery | William Lloyd Garrison (*The Liberator*, 1831); Frederick Douglass (*Narrative*, 1845); Sojourner Truth; Harriet Tubman (Underground Railroad) |
| **Women's Rights** | Equal rights for women; inspired by abolitionism | **Seneca Falls Convention (1848)**; Declaration of Sentiments ("all men and women are created equal"); Elizabeth Cady Stanton; Lucretia Mott |
| **Education** | Free public education; professional teachers; common schools | Horace Mann; first state board of education in Massachusetts |
| **Prison Reform** | Humane treatment; rehabilitation over punishment | Dorothea Dix (also mental asylums) |
| **Utopian Communities** | Perfect communities: Brook Farm, Oneida, Shakers, New Harmony | Reflected perfectionist idealism |

> [!check] Abolitionism Spectrum
> Not all abolitionists agreed on method: Garrison: immediate emancipation, moral persuasion, rejected political action. American Anti-Slavery Society (1833). Frederick Douglass: escaped enslaved man; most powerful voice; eventually supported political action. Moderate abolitionists: gradual emancipation, colonization to Africa (American Colonization Society). Know these distinctions.

## Jacksonian Democracy (1828–1840)

### Rise of Andrew Jackson
- Election of 1824: "Corrupt Bargain" — no Electoral College majority; House chose John Quincy Adams over Jackson; Henry Clay (Speaker of House) supported Adams, got Secretary of State appointment; Jackson supporters cried corruption
- **Election of 1828**: Jackson defeated Adams; era of mass political parties, popular campaigning, and expanded white male suffrage (most states eliminated property requirements by 1828)

### Jackson's Key Actions
- **Spoils system**: replaced federal officeholders with political supporters; defended as democratic rotation in office; critics: corrupt and incompetent
- **Indian Removal Act (1830)**: authorized removal of Native peoples east of Mississippi to "Indian Territory" west of Arkansas and Missouri
  - **Trail of Tears (1838–39)**: forced removal of Cherokee; ~4,000 died; *Worcester v. Georgia (1832)*: Marshall ruled Georgia couldn't apply its laws to Cherokee territory; Jackson reportedly ignored ruling
- **Nullification Crisis (1832–33)**: South Carolina nullified Tariff of 1832; Jackson issued Force Bill (threatened military action); Calhoun resigned as VP; Clay's compromise tariff resolved it but fundamental tension remained
- **Bank War (1832)**: Jackson vetoed re-charter of Bank of the United States; called it a "monster" serving wealthy elite; destroyed bank by withdrawing federal deposits; **Specie Circular (1836)** — required payment for government land in gold/silver → triggered financial panic
- **Panic of 1837**: bank failures, credit collapse, depression; began under Van Buren

### Democratic-Republican Split
- **Democrats** (Jackson): states' rights, limited government, anti-bank, agrarian, expanding slavery into territories
- **Whigs** (Clay, Webster): American System, active federal government, internal improvements, opposed executive tyranny (called Jackson "King Andrew")

## Manifest Destiny and Territorial Expansion

- **Manifest Destiny**: belief that American expansion westward to Pacific was divinely ordained and inevitable; coined 1845 by John L. O'Sullivan
- Driven by: land hunger, nationalism, racial ideology (Anglo-Saxon superiority), commercial ambitions
- **Texas**: American settlers (Stephen F. Austin) entered Mexican Texas; Mexico abolished slavery (1829); Texans revolted; **Texas Revolution (1836)**: Battle of the Alamo (February 1836); Battle of San Jacinto (April 1836); Texas independent; U.S. debated annexation (would add slave state); annexed **1845**
- **Oregon Territory**: joint occupation with Britain since 1818; "Fifty-Four Forty or Fight!" (Polk campaign); compromise at **49th parallel (1846)**
- **Mexican-American War (1846–48)**: triggered by disputed Texas border (Rio Grande vs. Nueces River); Polk sought war; critics (Lincoln, Thoreau, Whigs) called it a pretext for expansion; U.S. won easily
  - **Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848)**: Mexico ceded California, New Mexico, Arizona, Nevada, Utah, Colorado; U.S. paid $15 million
- **Wilmot Proviso (1846)**: proposed banning slavery from all territory gained from Mexico; passed House, failed Senate; exposed slavery as the central issue — launched sectional crisis of Unit 5

## AP Exam Tips — Unit 4

- **Market Revolution** is heavily tested in MCQ stimulus (images of canals, factory towns, railroad maps) — know economic AND social effects
- **Second Great Awakening → reform movements** is a classic causation chain for SAQ and LEQ
- Jackson appears in DBQ, LEQ, and SAQ; always balance his democratic appeal with his record on Native removal and slavery
- **Nullification Crisis** is the single most important constitutional precedent before the Civil War — know it for DBQ and LEQ
- LEQ Era 2 (1800–1898) often asks about the extent to which reform movements changed American society — Seneca Falls, abolition, temperance are your go-to evidence
- **Trail of Tears and Worcester v. Georgia** appear in MCQ; know that Jackson defied/ignored the Court ruling
- Connect Wilmot Proviso to Unit 5 immediately — it opens the slavery-in-territories debate that drives the 1850s
