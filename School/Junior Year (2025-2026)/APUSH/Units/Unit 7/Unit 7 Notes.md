## Study Hub Quick Reference — Unit 7

> [!check] MCQ Pattern — Progressive Era Sources
> AP loves muckraker excerpts (Upton Sinclair, Jacob Riis, Ida Tarbell) and political cartoons about monopolies. Identify the reform being advocated and the structural cause. Progressives believed in using government power to fix market/social failures — a direct shift from Gilded Age laissez-faire.

> [!check] MCQ Pattern — WWI and WWII U.S. Entry
> Both wars follow the same pattern: neutrality → provocation → U.S. entry. WWI: Zimmermann Telegram + unrestricted submarine warfare → April 1917. WWII: Pearl Harbor (December 7, 1941). MCQ often asks why the U.S. abandoned neutrality or what changed American public opinion.

> [!warning] Trap — New Deal Was Not Socialism
> AP may present conservative critics (Liberty League, Huey Long from the left) alongside FDR's defense. The New Deal did NOT fundamentally restructure capitalism — it used government programs to save the capitalist system. FDR himself said he was saving capitalism, not ending it.

> [!warning] Trap — 1920s Was Not Simply Prosperous
> Beneath the "Roaring Twenties" surface: farmers remained in depression all decade; Black Americans faced racial violence and the Klan; immigrants faced nativism and quota restrictions; labor unions were crushed. The prosperity was real but uneven and brittle.

> [!danger] Critical — Isolationism vs. Intervention Debate (1930s–41)
> **Isolationists** (America First Committee, Charles Lindbergh): U.S. should stay out of European wars; Neutrality Acts (1935–37) restricted arms sales and loans. **Interventionists**: Hitler was an existential threat; aid Britain now. FDR threaded the needle: Lend-Lease (1941), Destroyers-for-Bases, "Arsenal of Democracy" speech — supporting Allies without formal war entry until Pearl Harbor.

## American Imperialism (1890–1914)

**Ideological Foundations:**
- **Alfred Thayer Mahan**, *The Influence of Sea Power upon History* (1890): strong navy required overseas bases; fueled naval buildup
- **Social Darwinism + Anglo-Saxon superiority**: Josiah Strong; "civilizing mission"; white man's burden
- **Frederick Jackson Turner's Frontier Thesis (1893)**: American democracy was shaped by the frontier; with the frontier closed, needed new outlets for expansion
- Economic: need for new markets for American industrial production

**Spanish-American War (1898):**
- Cuba: Spain's brutal suppression of Cuban independence; yellow journalism (Hearst, Pulitzer) inflamed American opinion
- USS Maine explosion in Havana harbor (February 1898) → "Remember the Maine!"; cause still unclear
- "Splendid little war" (Sec. of State John Hay): 4 months; U.S. won decisively
- **Treaty of Paris (1898)**: Spain ceded Cuba (independent but U.S.-controlled), Puerto Rico, Guam, and **Philippines** to U.S. for $20 million

**Philippine-American War (1899–1902):**
- Filipinos expected independence; instead became U.S. territory; **Emilio Aguinaldo** led revolt
- 200,000+ Filipino civilians died; brutal U.S. counterinsurgency; "water cure" torture
- Anti-Imperialist League (Mark Twain, Andrew Carnegie, Jane Addams): imperial expansion contradicted American democratic values

**Other Interventions:**
- **Platt Amendment (1901)**: Cuba could not sign treaties or borrow money without U.S. approval; U.S. reserved right to intervene; effectively a protectorate until 1934
- **Panama Canal (1904–14)**: TR engineered Panamanian independence from Colombia; U.S. built canal; linked Atlantic and Pacific; strategic and commercial importance
- **Roosevelt Corollary to Monroe Doctrine (1904)**: U.S. would intervene in Latin American nations unable to maintain order or pay debts — "international police power"; used repeatedly in Caribbean

**Dollar Diplomacy (Taft)** and **Moral Diplomacy (Wilson)**: varied approaches but all maintained U.S. dominance in Western Hemisphere

## The Progressive Era (1900–1920)

**Key Features:** Middle-class reformers used government power to regulate business, improve conditions, and restore democracy. Belief in expertise, scientific management, and government activism.

**Muckrakers (investigative journalists who exposed corruption and abuses):**
| Journalist / Author | Work | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Upton Sinclair | *The Jungle* (1906) | Meatpacking industry conditions; food safety |
| Ida Tarbell | *History of the Standard Oil Company* (1904) | Rockefeller's monopoly practices |
| Jacob Riis | *How the Other Half Lives* (1890) | Tenement conditions (Unit 6 crossover) |
| Lincoln Steffens | *The Shame of the Cities* (1904) | Urban political machine corruption |

**Federal Legislation:**
| Act | Year | What It Did |
|---|---|---|
| Pure Food and Drug Act | 1906 | Banned adulterated/mislabeled food and drugs |
| Meat Inspection Act | 1906 | Federal inspection of meatpacking; direct result of *The Jungle* |
| Sherman Antitrust Act (enforcement) | 1890/1902+ | TR used it to break Northern Securities railroad trust; "trust-busting" |
| Clayton Antitrust Act | 1914 | Strengthened antitrust laws; exempted unions from antitrust |
| Federal Reserve Act | 1913 | Created central banking system; regulated currency and credit |
| 16th Amendment | 1913 | Graduated federal income tax |
| 17th Amendment | 1913 | Direct election of senators (Populist demand finally enacted) |

**Social Reforms:**
- **18th Amendment (1919)**: Prohibition of alcohol (temperance movement victory)
- **19th Amendment (1920)**: Women's suffrage — result of 70+ years of organizing; Seneca Falls (1848) → NAWSA → protests → ratification

**Progressive Presidents:**
| President | Key Actions |
|---|---|
| **TR (Roosevelt)** | Trust-busting; "Square Deal"; conservation (National Parks); Pure Food/Meat Inspection Acts; Panama Canal; Bully Pulpit |
| **Taft** | More trust-suits than TR but seen as conservative; 16th and 17th Amendments ratified; alienated progressives |
| **Wilson** | "New Freedom"; Federal Reserve; Clayton Act; Federal Trade Commission; Keating-Owen (child labor); re-segregated federal workforce |

**Race and Progressivism:**
- Progressivism largely excluded Black Americans; Wilson re-segregated federal government
- **NAACP (founded 1909)**: W.E.B. Du Bois, Ida B. Wells, others; legal strategy for civil rights
- **Booker T. Washington**: accommodationist; criticized by Du Bois's "Talented Tenth" who demanded full civil rights

## World War I (1914–1919)

**U.S. Neutrality (1914–17):**
- Wilson declared neutrality; "too proud to fight"
- U.S. supplied Allies (Britain, France) with loans and goods — not truly neutral
- **Lusitania sunk (1915)**: British passenger liner; 1,198 dead including 128 Americans; turned public opinion against Germany; Germany temporarily stopped unrestricted submarine warfare
- **Sussex Pledge (1916)**: Germany promised not to attack neutral ships without warning

**U.S. Entry (April 1917):**
- Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare (February 1917) — violated Sussex Pledge
- **Zimmermann Telegram (1917)**: Germany proposed Mexico attack U.S. in exchange for Texas, New Mexico, Arizona; British intercepted; outraged Americans
- Wilson asked Congress to declare war: "make the world safe for democracy"

**Home Front:**
- **Selective Service Act (1917)**: military draft; 4 million men served
- **War Industries Board**: government coordinated industrial production
- **Committee on Public Information (George Creel)**: propaganda; Four-Minute Men; anti-German hysteria
- **Espionage Act (1917) and Sedition Act (1918)**: criminalized opposition to the war; Eugene Debs imprisoned for anti-war speech
- **Schenck v. United States (1919)**: Supreme Court upheld Espionage Act; "clear and present danger" test; Holmes's opinion
- Women and Black workers filled industrial jobs vacated by soldiers

**Great Migration (1910s–1920s):**
- Hundreds of thousands of Black Americans moved from South to Northern cities (Chicago, Detroit, New York) seeking industrial jobs and escape from Jim Crow
- Northern racism and housing discrimination created urban ghettos
- **Chicago Race Riot (1919)**: one of "Red Summer" race riots; white mobs attacked Black neighborhoods

**Treaty of Versailles (1919) and Wilson's 14 Points:**
- Wilson's **Fourteen Points**: freedom of the seas, self-determination, no secret treaties, **League of Nations** (most important)
- Treaty was punitive toward Germany: war guilt clause, $33 billion reparations, territory loss — NOT what Wilson wanted
- Senate rejected Treaty (Henry Cabot Lodge led opposition); U.S. never joined League of Nations; Wilson had stroke campaigning for it; died 1924
- U.S. returned to **isolationism**

## The 1920s

**Economic Prosperity:**
- Mass production (Ford's assembly line); consumer culture; installment buying; advertising
- Automobile transformed American life: suburbs, oil industry, highways, roadside businesses
- Stock market speculation; buying on margin (10% down)

**Cultural Tensions:**
| Conservative / Traditional | Modern / Liberal |
|---|---|
| **KKK revival**: 4 million members by 1924; targeted Black people, Catholics, Jews, immigrants | **Harlem Renaissance**: Black artistic and cultural flowering; Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston, Louis Armstrong, Duke Ellington |
| **Fundamentalism**: Scopes Trial (1925) — Tennessee teacher tried for teaching evolution; Bryan vs. Darrow | **Flappers** and changing gender norms: women voted, worked, wore shorter skirts |
| **Prohibition** (18th Amendment): bootlegging, organized crime (Al Capone) | **Jazz Age**: spread of jazz, blues; radio and film spread popular culture nationally |
| **Nativism**: Emergency Quota Act (1921), National Origins Act (1924) — severely restricted immigration from Southern/Eastern Europe | **First Red Scare** (1919–20): anti-communist hysteria; Palmer Raids; Sacco and Vanzetti |

**African American Life:**
- UNIA and Marcus Garvey: "Back to Africa" movement; Black Pride; largest Black mass movement of era
- Harlem Renaissance: cultural expression asserted Black humanity and dignity

## The Great Depression and New Deal

**Causes of Great Depression:**
- **Stock market crash (October 1929, "Black Tuesday")**: speculation bubble; margin buying collapsed
- **Bank failures**: 9,000 banks failed 1930–33; life savings wiped out
- **Overproduction**: farms and factories produced more than consumers could buy
- **Wealth inequality**: top 1% owned 40% of wealth; too little purchasing power in middle/working class
- **Smoot-Hawley Tariff (1930)**: raised tariffs → trading partners retaliated → global trade collapsed
- **Hoover's response**: volunteerism; Reconstruction Finance Corporation; opposed direct relief as "the dole" — inadequate

**New Deal (FDR, 1933–39):**

**First Hundred Days (1933):**
| Program | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Emergency Banking Act | Stabilized banks; restored confidence |
| FDIC | Insured bank deposits up to $5,000 |
| Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC) | Young men employed in national parks/forests |
| Agricultural Adjustment Act (AAA) | Paid farmers to reduce production → raise prices |
| National Industrial Recovery Act (NIRA) | Industry codes; protected labor's right to organize; ruled unconstitutional 1935 |
| Public Works Administration (PWA) | Large-scale construction projects; employed millions |

**Second New Deal (1935):**
| Program | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Works Progress Administration (WPA) | Employed 8 million; built schools, bridges, public art |
| Social Security Act (1935) | Old-age pensions; unemployment insurance; permanent safety net |
| National Labor Relations Act (Wagner Act) | Protected workers' right to unionize; established NLRB |
| Rural Electrification Administration | Brought electricity to rural America |

**Opposition to New Deal:**
- **Liberty League** (business conservatives): New Deal was socialism, unconstitutional
- **Huey Long** (left): "Share the Wealth" — taxed rich to give $5,000 to every family
- **Father Coughlin** (radio priest): initially pro-FDR, later anti-Semitic, fascist sympathies
- **Supreme Court**: struck down NIRA and AAA; FDR proposed **court-packing plan** (add 6 justices) → Congress rejected it; but Court began upholding New Deal ("the switch in time that saved nine")

**Legacy:**
- Did NOT end Depression (unemployment still 14% in 1940; WWII spending ended it)
- Created the **welfare state**: Social Security, FDIC, SEC, labor protections — permanent
- Created coalition: Democrats aligned with labor, urban immigrants, Black voters, South (for now)

## World War II (1939–1945)

**Rise of Fascism and Totalitarianism:**
- Germany (Hitler, Nazi Party), Italy (Mussolini), Japan (militarist government)
- Appeasement: Britain and France gave Hitler Czechoslovakia's Sudetenland at Munich (1938); "peace for our time"
- Germany invaded Poland (September 1, 1939) → Britain and France declared war

**U.S. Neutrality and Path to Entry:**
- Neutrality Acts (1935–37): arms embargo; no loans; no travel on belligerent ships
- Fall of France (June 1940) shocked Americans; FDR began preparing for war
- **Lend-Lease Act (1941)**: loaned military equipment to Allies; "arsenal of democracy"
- Selective Service (peacetime draft) began 1940
- **Pearl Harbor (December 7, 1941)**: Japanese surprise attack; 2,400 Americans killed; 8 battleships sunk; "a date which will live in infamy"; Congress declared war the next day; Germany declared war on U.S.

**Home Front:**
- **War Production Board**: converted civilian industry to war production; U.S. produced more war material than all Axis powers combined
- **Rationing**: food, rubber, gasoline; victory gardens
- **Women**: "Rosie the Riveter"; 6 million women entered workforce; WACs, WAVES in military; but mostly returned to home roles after war
- **African Americans**: Double V Campaign (victory at home AND abroad); A. Philip Randolph threatened March on Washington → FDR issued **Executive Order 8802** (1941) banning discrimination in defense industry — first federal anti-discrimination order; Black soldiers served in segregated units
- **Japanese American Internment**: **Executive Order 9066 (1942)**: 120,000 Japanese Americans (70% U.S. citizens) forced into internment camps; **Korematsu v. United States (1944)**: Supreme Court upheld as wartime necessity; condemned by later courts
- **Bracero Program**: brought Mexican guest workers to replace agricultural labor

**Military Strategy:**
- Europe first strategy (defeat Germany before Japan)
- **North Africa → Sicily → Italy** (1942–43)
- **D-Day (June 6, 1944)**: Normandy invasion; largest amphibious assault in history; opened Western Front; turned tide in Europe
- Germany surrendered **May 8, 1945 (V-E Day)**
- Pacific: island hopping (Guadalcanal, Iwo Jima, Okinawa); fire bombing of Tokyo; **atomic bombs on Hiroshima (August 6) and Nagasaki (August 9, 1945)** → Japan surrendered August 15, 1945 **(V-J Day)**
- **Manhattan Project**: secret U.S. program to develop atomic bomb; J. Robert Oppenheimer led scientific team

**Holocaust:**
- Nazi Germany systematically murdered 6 million Jews and 5–6 million others (Roma, disabled, LGBTQ+, Slavs, political prisoners)
- U.S. awareness limited until late in war; refugee policy restrictive; turned away MS St. Louis (1939)
- Nuremberg Trials (1945–46): international tribunal; established individual accountability for war crimes and crimes against humanity

## AP Exam Tips — Unit 7

- **DBQ most commonly set in this unit**: Gilded Age-to-Progressive Era continuity; WWI home front; New Deal debate; WWII home front — all heavily documented
- **LEQ Era 3 (1890–2001)**: common prompts — "Evaluate the extent to which the New Deal transformed the role of the federal government" OR "Evaluate the causes of U.S. involvement in WWI/WWII"
- **SAQ**: common — explain one way the Progressive Era expanded federal power; explain one reason the U.S. abandoned neutrality in WWI; compare WWI and WWII home fronts
- **Continuity**: Progressive Era expanded government → New Deal expanded it further → Great Society (Unit 8) continues this trend — use this for complexity points
- **Japanese internment** always appears; know Korematsu and the contrast with civil liberties principles
- Know the **Four Freedoms** (speech, worship, want, fear) — FDR's articulation of what the U.S. was fighting for; useful for LEQ/SAQ contexts
