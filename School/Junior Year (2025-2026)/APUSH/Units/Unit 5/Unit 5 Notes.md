## Study Hub Quick Reference — Unit 5

> [!check] MCQ Pattern — Slavery Compromise Sequence
> Know the cascade of failed compromises: Missouri Compromise (1820) → Compromise of 1850 → Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854) → Bleeding Kansas → Dred Scott (1857) → Lincoln-Douglas debates (1858) → John Brown's Raid (1859) → Election of 1860 → Secession. Each event escalated the crisis. MCQ frequently asks what each compromise did and why it failed.

> [!check] MCQ Pattern — Reconstruction Amendments
> 13th (1865): abolished slavery. 14th (1868): birthright citizenship, equal protection, due process. 15th (1870): voting rights regardless of race. MCQ frequently presents a Reconstruction-era source and asks which amendment it reflects or what it changed. Know the order and content cold.

> [!warning] Trap — Emancipation Proclamation Was Limited
> The Emancipation Proclamation (January 1, 1863) freed enslaved people only in **Confederate states in rebellion** — NOT in border states, NOT in Union-controlled Confederate areas. It was a war measure, not a comprehensive abolition. Full abolition came with the **13th Amendment (1865)**.

> [!warning] Trap — Reconstruction Did Not Simply "Fail"
> AP pushes nuance: Radical Reconstruction achieved real gains (amendments, Freedmen's Bureau, Black officeholders, schools). Its end was not inevitable — it was actively destroyed by violence (KKK), Northern exhaustion, and the Compromise of 1877. "Failure" papers over the agency of white supremacists who dismantled it.

> [!danger] Critical — Causes of the Civil War
> AP expects a multi-causal argument: slavery as the central cause; states' rights (primarily over slavery); sectionalism (economic differences); political breakdown of the party system; Lincoln's election as the triggering event. Don't oversimplify to "states' rights" alone — the secession documents explicitly cited slavery's preservation.

## Escalating Sectional Crisis (1844–1860)

### Territorial Question After Mexican War

- **Wilmot Proviso (1846)**: ban slavery in all Mexican cession territory; passed House, blocked in Senate; exposed North-South split on slavery in territories
- **Free Soil Party (1848)**: "Free Soil, Free Labor, Free Men" — opposed slavery's expansion into territories; not necessarily abolitionist; focused on keeping territories open for white settlers
- **Compromise of 1850** (Henry Clay, Daniel Webster, Stephen Douglas):
  - California admitted as free state
  - New Mexico and Utah territories — **popular sovereignty** (voters decide on slavery)
  - Slave trade (not slavery) abolished in Washington D.C.
  - **Fugitive Slave Act**: required Northerners to return escaped enslaved people; federal commissioners paid more to return than to free; outraged Northern public; galvanized abolitionism
  - *Uncle Tom's Cabin* (Harriet Beecher Stowe, 1852): fictionalized slavery's horrors; sold 300,000 copies in first year; shifted Northern opinion

### Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854)
- Stephen Douglas proposed: organize Kansas and Nebraska territories with **popular sovereignty** on slavery → **repealed the Missouri Compromise** (36°30' line)
- **Bleeding Kansas (1854–61)**: pro-slavery and anti-slavery settlers flooded Kansas; guerrilla violence; two competing territorial governments; John Brown's Pottawatomie Massacre (1856)
- **Birth of Republican Party (1854)**: coalition of Whigs, Free-Soilers, Northern Democrats united against slavery's expansion; NOT abolitionist; "free soil, free labor, free men"

### Further Escalation
- **Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)**: Chief Justice Taney ruled: enslaved people are not citizens; Congress cannot ban slavery from territories (Missouri Compromise was unconstitutional); inflamed North, thrilled South; Republican Party rallied against it
- **Lincoln-Douglas Debates (1858)**: Illinois Senate race; made Lincoln nationally known; Douglas's "Freeport Doctrine" — territories can effectively exclude slavery despite Dred Scott by refusing to pass slave codes (lost him Southern Democrats)
- **John Brown's Raid on Harpers Ferry (1859)**: attempted to spark slave rebellion; captured, tried, executed; Northerners saw him as a martyr; Southerners saw Northern celebration as proof of abolitionist threat
- **Election of 1860**: Lincoln won with entirely Northern electoral votes; South Carolina seceded before his inauguration; six more states seceded; Confederate States of America formed (February 1861)

## The Civil War (1861–1865)

### Causes of the War
- **Confederate secession declarations** (South Carolina, Mississippi, Texas) explicitly cited slavery's preservation
- Lincoln's election: first president from a party that opposed slavery's expansion
- Lincoln's goal at war's start: **preserve the Union**, NOT abolish slavery

### Early War (1861–62)
- **Fort Sumter (April 1861)**: Confederate attack opened hostilities; Lincoln called for 75,000 volunteers
- Four border states stayed in Union (Maryland, Missouri, Kentucky, Delaware) — crucial
- Confederate advantages: defensive war, interior lines, strong military leadership (Lee, Jackson), motivated by defending home territory
- Union advantages: larger population, industrial capacity, railroad network, navy

### Turning Points
- **Battle of Antietam (September 1862)**: bloodiest single day in American history (~23,000 casualties); tactical Union victory; gave Lincoln political cover to issue Emancipation Proclamation
- **Emancipation Proclamation (January 1, 1863)**: freed enslaved people in Confederate states; changed war's character from union-preservation to slavery-abolition; prevented European recognition of the Confederacy (Britain couldn't support a pro-slavery cause once abolition was explicit); ~180,000 Black soldiers joined Union Army
- **Battle of Gettysburg (July 1–3, 1863)**: largest battle in Western Hemisphere; Confederate invasion of North repelled; turned tide in East; Pickett's Charge failed catastrophically
- **Siege of Vicksburg (July 4, 1863)**: Union controlled Mississippi River; split Confederacy
- **Gettysburg Address (November 1863)**: Lincoln redefined war as fulfillment of Declaration's promise — "a new birth of freedom"
- **Sherman's March to the Sea (1864)**: total war strategy; destroyed Confederate will and infrastructure
- **Lee surrendered at Appomattox (April 9, 1865)**; Lincoln assassinated April 14, 1865

### Home Front
- **Conscription**: both sides drafted; wealthy could pay substitutes ($300 for Union) → class resentment
- **New York City Draft Riots (1863)**: Irish working-class men attacked Black neighborhoods; fear of job competition + resentment at wealthy avoiding draft
- Women: took over farms, ran businesses, served as nurses (Clara Barton, Dorothea Dix)
- Economy: Union's industrial output expanded; Confederate economy strangled by blockade

## Reconstruction (1865–1877)

### Presidential Reconstruction (1865–67)
- **Lincoln's 10% Plan**: 10% of voters swear loyalty → state restored; "with malice toward none"
- **Wade-Davis Bill (1864)**: Congress demanded 50% + barred former Confederates; Lincoln pocket-vetoed it
- Andrew Johnson (Lincoln's VP, Democrat): pardoned most Confederates; allowed Southern states to restore themselves with minimal conditions
- Southern states passed **Black Codes**: restricted Black freedom of movement, work, and rights; effectively re-enslaved in all but name; outraged Northerners

### Radical Reconstruction (1867–1877)
- **Reconstruction Acts (1867)**: divided South into 5 military districts; required new state constitutions; required states to ratify 14th Amendment; barred former Confederate leaders from voting/holding office
- **13th Amendment (1865)**: abolished slavery
- **14th Amendment (1868)**: birthright citizenship; equal protection; due process; defined citizenship nationally, not by state
- **15th Amendment (1870)**: cannot deny voting rights based on race, color, or previous servitude (not sex — excluded women)
- **Freedmen's Bureau**: federal agency providing food, hospitals, schools, legal assistance to formerly enslaved people; remarkable achievements in education; chronically underfunded
- **Black political participation**: ~2,000 Black men held office during Reconstruction; 16 U.S. congressmen; state legislatures; South Carolina had Black majority legislature

### Gains of Reconstruction
- Historically Black colleges established (Howard, Morehouse, Fisk)
- Public school systems created in South (first time)
- Sharecropping emerged: formerly enslaved people farmed land for share of crop; became cycle of debt (debt peonage)

### Destruction of Reconstruction
- **Ku Klux Klan (founded 1865)**: terrorist organization; attacked Black voters, officeholders, and white Republicans; **Enforcement Acts (1870–71)** temporarily suppressed it
- **Liberal Republicans and "Grantism"**: Grant administration riddled with corruption (Credit Mobilier, Whiskey Ring); Northern political will for Reconstruction waned
- **Panic of 1873**: economic depression; Northerners focused on economic recovery, not Southern affairs
- **Redeemers**: Southern Democrats who "redeemed" states from Republican control through violence and intimidation
- **Compromise of 1877**: disputed Hayes-Tilden election; Congress awarded Hayes presidency in exchange for withdrawing federal troops from South; Reconstruction effectively ended
- **Aftermath**: sharecropping, Jim Crow laws (Unit 6), Black disenfranchisement

## AP Exam Tips — Unit 5

- **DBQ frequently set here (1754–1980 window)**: Reconstruction-era sources are AP favorites — Freedmen's Bureau reports, political cartoons about KKK, congressional debates on amendments
- **LEQ Era 2 (1800–1898)**: common prompt — "Evaluate the extent to which Reconstruction succeeded in guaranteeing rights to formerly enslaved Americans"
- **SAQ**: common prompts — explain one cause of the Civil War; explain one reason Reconstruction ended; compare Lincoln and Johnson's Reconstruction plans
- Emancipation Proclamation's **limits** are always worth noting: it was a war measure, not a universal abolition
- **Continuity**: note that post-Reconstruction conditions (sharecropping, Black Codes → Jim Crow) represent continuity of racial oppression into Unit 6
- Know the Reconstruction Amendments in order, content, and what specifically each protected
