## Q1. Net ionic equation for NaCl(aq) + AgNO₃(aq) precipitation

NaCl and AgNO₃ are both soluble ionic salts → fully dissociate. The precipitate is AgCl (silver halide → insoluble by solubility rules). Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ are spectator ions and cancel. The NIE shows only the ions that form the solid.

**Answer: A — $\text{Ag}^+(aq) + \text{Cl}^-(aq) \rightarrow \text{AgCl}(s)$**

The NIE includes only $\text{Ag}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ because they are the only species that undergo a chemical change — forming the insoluble ionic solid AgCl. Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ remain dissociated in solution and are spectator ions excluded from the NIE.

> **Trap:** Choice C and D show full molecular formulas — those are molecular equations, not net ionic equations. The NIE requires splitting all strong electrolytes and canceling spectators.

## Q2. Evidence that ionic bonds formed during precipitation

Ionic bonds are characterized by properties of ionic solids: high melting points (due to strong electrostatic attraction in the crystal lattice), brittleness, and electrical conductivity when melted or dissolved. A high melting point is direct evidence of strong ionic bonding in the precipitate.

**Answer: C — The precipitate has a high melting point.**

Ionic solids have high melting points because breaking the crystal lattice requires overcoming strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions (Coulomb's law: $F \propto q_1 q_2 / r^2$). A high melting point is direct structural evidence that ionic bonds were formed in the precipitate.

> **Trap:** B (solution conducts electricity) is tempting but describes the ions remaining in solution (spectators), not the precipitate itself. Conductivity of the filtrate does not demonstrate bond formation in the solid.

## Q3. Effect of doubling NaCl concentration on precipitate formed

Original: 50.0 mL of 1.0 M NaCl + 50.0 mL of 1.0 M AgNO₃.

$$n_{\text{NaCl}} = (1.0\ \text{M})(0.050\ \text{L}) = 0.050\ \text{mol}$$
$$n_{\text{AgNO}_3} = (1.0\ \text{M})(0.050\ \text{L}) = 0.050\ \text{mol}$$

Both react in a 1:1 ratio → both are limiting (exactly stoichiometric). Theoretical yield: 0.050 mol AgCl.

New scenario: 50.0 mL of 2.0 M NaCl + 50.0 mL of 1.0 M AgNO₃.

$$n_{\text{NaCl}} = (2.0\ \text{M})(0.050\ \text{L}) = 0.100\ \text{mol}$$
$$n_{\text{AgNO}_3} = (1.0\ \text{M})(0.050\ \text{L}) = 0.050\ \text{mol}$$

Reaction is 1:1. $\text{AgNO}_3$ is now the limiting reactant (only 0.050 mol). NaCl is in excess. The yield of AgCl is still limited by $\text{AgNO}_3$ → **same 0.050 mol AgCl**.

**Answer: D — not change, because the amount of AgNO₃(aq) did not change**

Because $\text{AgNO}_3$ is the limiting reactant in the new scenario, the amount of AgCl produced is determined entirely by the moles of $\text{AgNO}_3$, which did not change. Excess NaCl beyond the stoichiometric amount contributes no additional product.

> **Trap:** B (double, because one reactant doubled) is the most common wrong answer. Students forget to check which reactant is limiting after the change. Doubling a reactant only doubles the yield if that reactant was the original limiting reactant — here, doubling NaCl simply converts it from stoichiometric to excess.

## Q4. Evidence of chemical reaction for Na₂CO₃ + HCl → NaCl + H₂O + CO₂

The equation produces $\text{CO}_2(g)$, a gas. Gas evolution is a classic evidence of chemical change (new substance with different composition formed). The two starting solutions are both colorless aqueous solutions, so color, volume, and conductivity alone do not confirm a new substance formed.

**Answer: A — Bubbles formed when the two solutions were combined.**

Bubble formation indicates gas production — $\text{CO}_2(g)$ is generated from the reaction of carbonate with acid: $\text{CO}_3^{2-}(aq) + 2\text{H}^+(aq) \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \text{CO}_2(g)$. Gas evolution is unambiguous evidence of a chemical change because a new substance with a different composition ($\text{CO}_2$) is formed.

> **Trap:** D (solution conducts electricity) is tempting because ions are present, but the original solutions also conducted electricity. Conductivity in the product does not distinguish chemical change from physical mixing of electrolytes.

## Q5. Observation consistent with a physical change only

Physical change: only IMFs or interparticle forces broken; composition of substances unchanged. No new compound formed.

- A: Color change (colorless → pink) → evidence of new substance → chemical change.
- B: Sparks produced → energy released, new species → chemical change.
- C: Solid broken into small pieces → composition unchanged, only mechanical → **physical change**.
- D: Precipitate formed → new insoluble compound → chemical change.

**Answer: C — One piece of solid substance was changed into small pieces.**

Breaking a solid into smaller pieces does not alter the chemical composition of the substance. No covalent bonds or ionic lattice bonds are broken in a way that produces a new compound — only the macroscopic shape changes. This is the definition of a physical change.

> **Trap:** A (color change) is incorrectly viewed as harmless mixing by some students. Any color change from colorless to colored strongly suggests formation of a new compound and is AP-accepted evidence of chemical change.

## Q6. Best evidence of chemical change when two liquids are combined

- A: Cloudy mixture → could be two immiscible liquids forming an emulsion (physical) → not definitive.
- B: Volume ≈ sum of initial volumes → expected for any mixing, physical or chemical.
- C: Light blue result → mixing a dark-blue and colorless liquid could yield light blue by dilution (physical) → not definitive.
- D: Two separate layers → immiscible liquids, a physical property → not chemical.

Among these, cloudiness in a mixture of two initially clear liquids is best explained by precipitate formation — a new insoluble solid that was not present in either starting liquid.

**Answer: A — The resulting mixture was cloudy.**

Cloudiness in a mixture of two initially clear liquids is best explained by formation of a precipitate — a new insoluble solid compound. Precipitate formation is chemical-change evidence because a new substance with a different composition is produced. The other choices (volume additivity, color dilution, layer separation) are consistent with physical mixing of immiscible liquids.

> **Trap:** C (light blue) seems like a color change, but diluting a dark-blue solution with a colorless one would naturally give light blue — no new compound needed. Color change is only strong evidence of chemical change when it is unexpected from simple mixing.

## Q7. NIE for hydrofluoric acid + sodium hydroxide neutralization

HF is a **weak acid** — it is NOT fully dissociated and stays molecular in the NIE. NaOH is a strong base → splits: $\text{Na}^+(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq)$. Na⁺ is a spectator ion.

The NIE retains HF(aq) as a molecule:

$$\text{HF}(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq) \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \text{F}^-(aq)$$

**Answer: D — $\text{HF}(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq) \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \text{F}^-(aq)$**

Since HF is a weak acid, it is not fully dissociated in aqueous solution and is written in molecular form in the NIE. NaOH fully dissociates; Na⁺ is a spectator and is canceled. The NIE shows HF donating its proton to $\text{OH}^-$ to form water, with $\text{F}^-$ remaining in solution.

> **Trap:** C ($\text{H}^+ + \text{OH}^- \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}$) is the NIE for a **strong** acid + strong base. Students who forget HF is weak incorrectly write H⁺ as a free ion. HF is one of the most commonly tested weak acids — it does NOT fully dissociate despite F being highly electronegative.

## Q8. NIE for NH₃(aq) + HCl(aq)

$\text{NH}_3$ is a **weak base** — stays molecular. HCl is a **strong acid** → dissociates: $\text{H}^+(aq) + \text{Cl}^-(aq)$. $\text{Cl}^-$ is a spectator.

NIE:
$$\text{NH}_3(aq) + \text{H}^+(aq) \rightarrow \text{NH}_4^+(aq)$$

**Answer: B — $\text{NH}_3(aq) + \text{H}^+(aq) \rightarrow \text{NH}_4^+(aq)$**

$\text{NH}_3$ is a weak base and is written in molecular form. HCl is a strong acid and fully dissociates; $\text{Cl}^-$ is a spectator ion that cancels. The NIE shows the Brønsted–Lowry proton transfer: $\text{H}^+$ from HCl is accepted by $\text{NH}_3$'s lone pair to form $\text{NH}_4^+$.

> **Trap:** A ($\text{NH}_3 + \text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$) is the molecular equation, not the NIE — it retains spectator Cl⁻. D splits HCl correctly but leaves $\text{Cl}^-$ in the equation, which should be canceled as a spectator.

## Q9. Which gas in bubbles supports the claim that boiling water is a physical change?

Boiling water at 100°C is a physical change — $\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$. If the bubbles contain $\text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$ (steam/water vapor), the same molecular composition is present in the gas phase as the liquid — no new substance formed.

- A ($\text{H}_2(g)$): would indicate electrolysis/decomposition → chemical change.
- B ($\text{O}_2(g)$): would indicate decomposition → chemical change.
- C ($\text{CO}_2(g)$): entirely different compound → chemical change.
- D ($\text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$): same composition as liquid water → physical change.

**Answer: D — $\text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$**

If the gaseous contents of the bubbles are $\text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$ (water vapor), the composition of the substance is unchanged from the liquid — only the phase changed. This is the defining criterion for a physical change: same chemical identity, different state. Boiling breaks only hydrogen-bond IMFs, not O–H covalent bonds.

> **Trap:** Students sometimes choose A or B, reasoning that bubbles must contain dissolved gases. But the question specifies freshly distilled $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (minimal dissolved gas) on a hot plate. The bubbles at 100°C are water vapor — confirmed by finding $\text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$ inside.

## Q10. NaI crystals + Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) → yellow precipitate; which claim is best supported?

Data: NaI crystals (white, 2.0 g) → yellow solid recovered (3.0 g); colorless filtrate.

- A: "Physical change — new yellow compound formed." Contradiction: new compound forming IS a chemical change.
- B: "Physical change — NaI changed color when mixed with water." NaI dissolves colorlessly; yellow solid is NOT NaI — it is a new compound ($\text{PbI}_2$, yellow). Also wrong classification.
- C: "Chemical change — yellow insoluble compound with larger mass formed." $\text{PbI}_2$ is insoluble (Pb²⁺ with halide → exception → insoluble), yellow, and has mass > original NaI because Pb²⁺ from solution incorporated into the solid. ✓
- D: "Chemical change — covalent bonds broken during drying." Ionic compounds don't have covalent bonds broken during drying; incorrect mechanism.

**Answer: C — A chemical change occurred when a yellow, insoluble compound with a larger mass than the original NaI formed.**

The reaction $2\text{NaI}(aq) + \text{Pb}(\text{NO}_3)_2(aq) \rightarrow \text{PbI}_2(s) + 2\text{NaNO}_3(aq)$ produces $\text{PbI}_2$, a yellow insoluble precipitate. New substance formation (different color, different composition, insoluble) is definitive evidence of chemical change. The recovered solid (3.0 g) exceeds the NaI added (2.0 g) because Pb²⁺ from solution is incorporated into the precipitate.

> **Trap:** A says "physical change" despite mentioning a "new yellow compound" — students may miss the internal contradiction. If a new compound formed, it is by definition a chemical change.

## Q11. Amount of HC₂H₃O₂ remaining after 5.0 mL NaOH vs. 1.0 mL NaOH added (equivalence point at 20.0 mL)

The NIE is: $\text{HC}_2\text{H}_3\text{O}_2(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq) \rightarrow \text{C}_2\text{H}_3\text{O}_2^-(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$

Ratio is 1:1. Each mole of NaOH added reacts with and consumes one mole of $\text{HC}_2\text{H}_3\text{O}_2$.

After 1.0 mL NaOH: some acid consumed, most remains.
After 5.0 mL NaOH: more NaOH added → more acid consumed → **less acid remains** than after 1.0 mL.

**Answer: A — It is less because more HC₂H₃O₂(aq) reacted with the base.**

Each incremental addition of NaOH neutralizes acid in a 1:1 stoichiometric ratio per the NIE. By the time 5.0 mL of NaOH has been added (compared to 1.0 mL), an additional 4.0 mL worth of $\text{OH}^-$ has reacted with $\text{HC}_2\text{H}_3\text{O}_2$, consuming more of the acid. The equivalence point is not reached until 20.0 mL, confirming acid is still present at both points but decreasing.

> **Trap:** B and C ("it is the same") tempt students who confuse the 1:1 coefficient ratio with the idea that "amounts don't change until the equivalence point." The coefficient ratio tells you the mole ratio of reaction — not that the acid is static. Every drop of base added reacts with and reduces the acid.

## Q12. Which change doubles volume of SO₂ produced at same T and P?

Reaction: $\text{K}_2\text{SO}_3(aq) + 2\text{HNO}_3(aq) \rightarrow 2\text{KNO}_3(aq) + \text{SO}_2(g) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$

Original: 100.0 mL of 0.100 M $\text{K}_2\text{SO}_3$ + 100.0 mL of 0.200 M $\text{HNO}_3$.

$$n_{\text{K}_2\text{SO}_3} = (0.100)(0.100) = 0.0100\ \text{mol}$$
$$n_{\text{HNO}_3} = (0.200)(0.100) = 0.0200\ \text{mol}$$

Stoichiometric ratio needed: 1 mol $\text{K}_2\text{SO}_3$ : 2 mol $\text{HNO}_3$. Available: 0.0100 : 0.0200 = exactly 1:2. Both are exactly stoichiometric (neither is in excess). 1 mol $\text{K}_2\text{SO}_3$ → 1 mol $\text{SO}_2$, so 0.0100 mol $\text{SO}_2$ produced.

To double SO₂, need 0.0200 mol $\text{SO}_2$ → need 0.0200 mol $\text{K}_2\text{SO}_3$ AND 0.0400 mol $\text{HNO}_3$.

Check option C: 200.0 mL of 0.100 M $\text{K}_2\text{SO}_3$ → $n = 0.0200$ mol; 200.0 mL of 0.200 M $\text{HNO}_3$ → $n = 0.0400$ mol. Ratio: 0.0200 : 0.0400 = 1:2 ✓. Both doubled, correct stoichiometric ratio maintained. SO₂ doubles. ✓

Check option A: 200.0 mL of 0.100 M $\text{K}_2\text{SO}_3$ → 0.0200 mol; HNO₃ unchanged at 0.0200 mol. Ratio: 0.0200 : 0.0200 = 1:1. Need 1:2, so HNO₃ is now limiting → only 0.0100 mol $\text{K}_2\text{SO}_3$ reacts → same SO₂ as before. ✗

**Answer: C — Using 200.0 mL of 0.100 M K₂SO₃(aq) and 200.0 mL of 0.200 M HNO₃(aq), because this provides double the number of moles with the correct stoichiometric ratio**

To double the product yield, both limiting reactants must be doubled while maintaining their stoichiometric ratio (1:2). Option C doubles both volumes, exactly doubling moles of each reactant while preserving the 1:2 ratio, yielding double the $\text{SO}_2$.

> **Trap:** A is very tempting — it doubles $\text{K}_2\text{SO}_3$ (which students think should double product). But it creates an imbalance: HNO₃ becomes limiting and only 0.0100 mol $\text{SO}_2$ is still produced. The explanation in A ("it becomes the reactant in excess") is actually why it doesn't work.

## Q13. Best description of Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)

Check oxidation numbers:
- Zn: 0 → +2 in $\text{ZnCl}_2$ → oxidation number increases → **oxidized**
- H: +1 in HCl → 0 in $\text{H}_2(g)$ → oxidation number decreases → **reduced**

Electron transfer occurs → this is a redox (oxidation-reduction) reaction. The Zn is the reducing agent; H⁺ is the oxidizing agent.

**Answer: D — It is an oxidation-reduction reaction because zinc is oxidized and hydrogen is reduced.**

Zn goes from ON = 0 to ON = +2 (loses 2 electrons → oxidized). H goes from ON = +1 in HCl to ON = 0 in $\text{H}_2$ (gains electrons → reduced). Electron transfer between species defines an oxidation-reduction reaction.

> **Trap:** C (acid-base because HCl donates H⁺) is plausible because H⁺ is transferred — but the AP distinguishes: in this reaction, H⁺ is actually **reduced** (ON changes from +1 to 0), making it primarily a redox reaction. A proton transfer that also involves electron transfer is classified as redox on the AP exam.

## Q14. Description of NH₃ + H₂O reaction (Lewis structure shown: NH₃ accepts H from H₂O → NH₄⁺ + OH⁻)

The diagram shows $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ donating a proton (H⁺) to $\text{NH}_3$, which accepts it via its lone pair. Products: $\text{NH}_4^+(aq)$ and $\text{OH}^-(aq)$. This is a Brønsted–Lowry acid-base reaction: $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ is the acid (proton donor); $\text{NH}_3$ is the base (proton acceptor).

**Answer: B — It is an acid-base reaction in which a proton is exchanged from H₂O to NH₃.**

Per Brønsted–Lowry theory, an acid donates a proton (H⁺) and a base accepts it. Here, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ transfers a proton to $\text{NH}_3$'s lone pair, forming $\text{NH}_4^+$ (conjugate acid) and $\text{OH}^-$ (conjugate base). No change in oxidation numbers occurs, confirming this is not redox.

> **Trap:** D (redox, N changes from −3 to −4) is wrong — in $\text{NH}_3$, N is −3 (H = +1: $3(+1) + N = 0 \Rightarrow N = -3$). In $\text{NH}_4^+$: $4(+1) + N = +1 \Rightarrow N = -3$. N stays −3; no oxidation number change → not redox.

## Q15. 2Mg(s) + SiCl₄(l) → 2MgCl₂(s) + Si(s) — correct statement about redox

Assign oxidation numbers:
- Mg(s): ON = 0
- Si in $\text{SiCl}_4$: Cl = −1 each; $\text{Si} + 4(-1) = 0 \Rightarrow \text{Si} = +4$
- Mg in $\text{MgCl}_2$: Mg = +2; Cl = −1 ✓
- Si(s): ON = 0

Changes:
- Mg: 0 → +2 → **oxidized** (loses electrons) ✓
- Si: +4 → 0 → **reduced** (gains electrons)
- Cl: −1 → −1 → no change

Check choices:
- A: "Mg is oxidized" ✓ — Mg goes 0 → +2.
- B: "Electrons transferred from SiCl₄ to Mg" ✗ — Mg loses electrons TO Si (Mg is oxidized, meaning electrons go from Mg to Si).
- C: "Cl changes from +4 to +2" ✗ — Cl is always −1 here; it's Si that has the +4 (and goes to 0).
- D: "Not redox because no ON changes" ✗ — Mg and Si both change ON.

**Answer: A — It is an oxidation-reduction reaction, and Mg is oxidized.**

Mg changes from ON = 0 to ON = +2, losing 2 electrons per atom → oxidized. Si changes from ON = +4 (in $\text{SiCl}_4$) to ON = 0 (elemental Si) → reduced. This electron transfer between Mg and Si defines the reaction as oxidation-reduction.

> **Trap:** C confuses which element has the +4 oxidation number. Students misread "$\text{SiCl}_4$" and assign +4 to Cl rather than Si. Remember: Cl is always −1 in compounds (unless bonded to F or O); it's Si that must be +4 to balance the 4 Cl atoms at −1 each.

## Q16. Conjugate acid-base pair in C₆H₅COOH + NaOH → C₆H₅COONa + H₂O

A conjugate acid-base pair differs by exactly one proton (H⁺). The acid donates H⁺ to become the conjugate base.

- $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COOH}$ donates H⁺ → becomes $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COO}^-$ (conjugate base). Pair: $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COOH}$ / $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COO}^-$ ✓
- $\text{OH}^-$ accepts H⁺ → becomes $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (conjugate acid). Pair: $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ / $\text{OH}^-$, but the acid is $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and conjugate base is $\text{OH}^-$.

Option B: Acid = $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COOH}$, conjugate base = $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COO}^-$ ✓ (differ by one H⁺)

**Answer: B — Acid: C₆H₅COOH | Conjugate base: C₆H₅COO⁻**

$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COOH}$ donates one proton (H⁺) to $\text{OH}^-$, forming $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COO}^-$. The acid and its conjugate base differ by exactly one H⁺, which is the definition of a conjugate acid-base pair.

> **Trap:** A pairs $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COOH}$ with $\text{OH}^-$ — these are the two reactants (Brønsted acid and base), NOT a conjugate pair. A conjugate pair must be related by a single proton transfer on the same side of the reaction (acid → conjugate base, or base → conjugate acid).

## Q17. Role of C₅H₅N (pyridine) reacting with HCl: gains H⁺ from HCl → [C₅H₅NH]⁺ + Cl⁻

The diagram shows the N lone pair on pyridine accepting H⁺ from HCl. Pyridine gains a proton → it is the proton acceptor → **Brønsted–Lowry base**.

**Answer: A — a Brønsted-Lowry base**

$\text{C}_5\text{H}_5\text{N}$ accepts a proton (H⁺) from HCl via the lone pair on nitrogen, forming $[\text{C}_5\text{H}_5\text{NH}]^+$. By Brønsted–Lowry definition, a base is a proton acceptor. $\text{C}_5\text{H}_5\text{N}$ is therefore acting as a Brønsted–Lowry base in this reaction.

> **Trap:** D ("conjugate acid of $[\text{C}_5\text{H}_5\text{NH}]^+$") reverses the relationship. $[\text{C}_5\text{H}_5\text{NH}]^+$ is the conjugate ACID of $\text{C}_5\text{H}_5\text{N}$ — meaning $\text{C}_5\text{H}_5\text{N}$ is the conjugate BASE. The question asks what $\text{C}_5\text{H}_5\text{N}$ acts as, which is a Brønsted–Lowry base, not "the conjugate acid of its product."

## Q18. Which species can act as both a conjugate acid AND a conjugate base?

A species acts as both conjugate acid and conjugate base if it is **amphoteric** — it can both donate and accept a proton.

- A: $\text{HS}^-$: can donate H⁺ → $\text{S}^{2-}$ (acid); can accept H⁺ → $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ (base). **Amphoteric** ✓
- B: $\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-$: has no ionizable proton to donate (it IS the conjugate base of acetic acid — can only accept H⁺, not donate). Only a base.
- C: $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$: fully protonated water — can donate H⁺ → $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (acid only). Cannot meaningfully accept another proton.
- D: $\text{NH}_4^+$: can donate H⁺ → $\text{NH}_3$ (acid). But $\text{NH}_4^+$ accepting H⁺ would form $\text{NH}_5^{2+}$ — not chemically reasonable. Only an acid.

**Answer: A — HS⁻**

$\text{HS}^-$ is amphoteric: as a conjugate base of $\text{H}_2\text{S}$, it can donate its remaining proton to a base ($\text{HS}^- \rightarrow \text{S}^{2-} + \text{H}^+$); as a conjugate acid of $\text{S}^{2-}$, it can accept a proton from an acid ($\text{HS}^- + \text{H}^+ \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{S}$). This dual capability defines amphoteric behavior.

> **Trap:** B ($\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-$) is often chosen because students remember it as the conjugate base of acetic acid. But being a conjugate base doesn't make it amphoteric — it has no hydrogen to donate as an acid. $\text{HS}^-$ retains one ionizable hydrogen, making it uniquely able to do both.

## Q19. Oxidation half-reaction from: 5Fe²⁺(aq) + MnO₄⁻(aq) + 8H⁺(aq) → 5Fe³⁺(aq) + Mn²⁺(aq) + 4H₂O(l)

Oxidation = loss of electrons = ON increases.
- Fe: +2 → +3 (ON increases) → **oxidized** → this is the oxidation half-reaction.
- Mn: +7 → +2 → reduced.

Oxidation half-reaction for Fe: $\text{Fe}^{2+}(aq) \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{3+}(aq) + e^-$

Electrons are **produced** (on the right) in an oxidation half-reaction (OIL: Oxidation Is Loss).

**Answer: B — $\text{Fe}^{2+}(aq) \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{3+}(aq) + e^-$**

Fe is oxidized: its oxidation number increases from +2 to +3, meaning it loses one electron. In a half-reaction, the electrons are written on the product side for oxidation (OIL — Oxidation Is Loss of electrons). Option B correctly shows $e^-$ as a product.

> **Trap:** A ($\text{Fe}^{2+} + e^- \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{3+}$) places the electron on the reactant side — that is a reduction half-reaction format. Students mix up which side electrons belong on. Mnemonic: OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss (electrons on product side); Reduction Is Gain (electrons on reactant side).

## Q20. Balanced ionic equation from half-reactions: Al(s) → Al³⁺(aq) + 3e⁻ and Sn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Sn(s)

To cancel electrons, find LCM of 3 and 2 = 6.

Multiply oxidation half-reaction × 2: $2\text{Al}(s) \rightarrow 2\text{Al}^{3+}(aq) + 6e^-$

Multiply reduction half-reaction × 3: $3\text{Sn}^{2+}(aq) + 6e^- \rightarrow 3\text{Sn}(s)$

Add and cancel 6e⁻:
$$2\text{Al}(s) + 3\text{Sn}^{2+}(aq) \rightarrow 2\text{Al}^{3+}(aq) + 3\text{Sn}(s)$$

**Answer: C — $2\text{Al}(s) + 3\text{Sn}^{2+}(aq) \rightarrow 2\text{Al}^{3+}(aq) + 3\text{Sn}(s)$**

The electrons must cancel: Al produces 3e⁻ per atom; Sn²⁺ consumes 2e⁻ per ion. The LCM of 3 and 2 is 6, requiring 2 Al atoms and 3 Sn²⁺ ions. After multiplying each half-reaction by the appropriate coefficient and adding, the 6 electrons cancel cleanly.

> **Trap:** B ($3\text{Al} + 2\text{Sn}^{2+} \rightarrow 3\text{Al}^{3+} + 2\text{Sn}$) reverses the coefficients. Students intuitively assign coefficient 3 to Al (matching the 3e⁻ Al loses) and 2 to Sn (matching 2e⁻ Sn gains) — but this is backwards. The LCM method requires you to multiply the HALF-REACTION by the other's electron count: Al half-reaction ×2, Sn half-reaction ×3.

## Q21. Reduction half-reaction for Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → Cu(s) + ZnSO₄(aq)

Reduction = gain of electrons = ON decreases.
- Cu: +2 (in $\text{CuSO}_4$) → 0 (elemental Cu) → ON decreases → **reduced** → reduction half-reaction.
- Zn: 0 → +2 → oxidized.

Reduction half-reaction: $\text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^- \rightarrow \text{Cu}(s)$

Electrons on the **reactant side** (RIG: Reduction Is Gain).

**Answer: A — $\text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^- \rightarrow \text{Cu}(s)$**

$\text{Cu}^{2+}$ gains 2 electrons to become elemental Cu(s), with ON decreasing from +2 to 0 — this is reduction. In a half-reaction, electrons appear on the reactant side for reduction (RIG — Reduction Is Gain). $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ is a spectator ion and does not appear in the half-reaction.

> **Trap:** D ($\text{Zn}(s) \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^-$) is the **oxidation** half-reaction, not the reduction. Students sometimes pick Zn because it is the solid reactant that visibly "does something" in the experiment. The question asks specifically for the **reduction** half-reaction — always identify which species gains electrons (ON decreases).
