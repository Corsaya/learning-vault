## Study Hub Quick Reference — Unit 4

> [!check] Q2 — Electrolytes and Electrical Conductivity
> **Strong electrolytes** (strong acids, strong bases, soluble ionic salts) fully dissociate → many ions → high conductivity. **Weak electrolytes** (weak acids, weak bases) partially dissociate → low conductivity. **Non-electrolytes** (glucose, methanol) don't dissociate → no conductivity. Solid ionic compounds do NOT conduct — ions are fixed.

> [!check] Q6 — Lab: Separation Techniques Decision Tree
> **Filtration:** solid suspended in liquid (insoluble). **Distillation:** miscible liquids with different boiling points. **Chromatography (TLC/column):** dissolved components separated by differential IMF affinity. **Extraction:** two immiscible solvents, separate by solubility. Match the method to what type of mixture you have.

> [!check] Q10 — Activity Series: Single Displacement Redox
> A metal **higher** on the activity series displaces a metal **lower** from its aqueous salt (reaction occurs). Metals **above H₂** react with dilute acids → produce H₂ gas. Metals **below H₂** (Cu, Ag, Au) do NOT react with dilute acids. If free metal is lower than the ion → **no reaction (NR)**.

> [!check] Q12 — Stoichiometry: g → mol → mol ratio → g
> 1. Convert mass to moles: $n = m/M$
> 2. Apply mole ratio from balanced equation: $n_A \times (\text{coeff}_B / \text{coeff}_A) = n_B$
> 3. Convert back: $m = n \times M$
> Always use the **balanced equation's coefficients** for the mole ratio. Never use unbalanced ratios.

> [!check] Q22 — Limiting Reagent from Particulate Diagrams
> Count each reactant particle type in the diagram. Apply the mole ratio from the balanced equation to determine which runs out first. The **limiting reagent** gives fewer moles of product. Excess reagent particles appear **unchanged and uncombined** on the products side.

> [!check] Q35 — Net Ionic Equations: Removing Spectator Ions
> Split ALL strong electrolytes into ions (strong acids, strong bases, soluble salts). Keep molecular: weak acids, weak bases, water, solids (s), gases (g). Cancel any ion appearing **identically on both sides** (spectator ions). What remains is the NIE.

> [!check] FRQ Q1C — Stoichiometry Chain: mol CO₂ → mol EtOH → mass → volume
> Step 1: $n_{CO_2}$ from combustion data. Step 2: $n_{EtOH} = n_{CO_2} \times (\text{coeff}_{EtOH}/\text{coeff}_{CO_2})$ from balanced equation. Step 3: $m_{EtOH} = n_{EtOH} \times M_{EtOH}$. Step 4: $V = m/\rho$ (mass ÷ density). Each step uses a different conversion factor.

> [!check] FRQ Q2A — Particulate Model: Stoichiometric Product Counts
> Product diagram must show particles in the **correct stoichiometric ratio** from the balanced equation. Count reactant particles → apply mole ratio → determine product count. Spectator ions or unreacting molecules appear identically on both sides. Precipitates appear as clustered solid particles.

> [!check] FRQ Q2C — Mass Lost = Mass of Gas Produced
> In a closed system where gas escapes: $\Delta m = m_{before} - m_{after}$ = mass of gas produced. Then $n_{gas} = \Delta m / M_{gas}$. Apply mole ratio from balanced equation to find moles of other species. For $2H_2O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O + O_2$: mole ratio $H_2O_2 : O_2 = 2:1$.

> [!check] FRQ Q2F — Systematic Error Analysis
> A systematic error shifts **all** results the same direction (consistently too high OR too low). Identify: (1) what quantity was measured incorrectly, (2) does the error make it too large or too small, (3) how does that propagate through the formula to affect the final result. Random errors scatter in both directions.

> [!warning] Trap — Silver Halides: AgCl, AgBr, AgI All Insoluble
> AgCl, AgBr, and AgI are all **insoluble** (precipitates form). AgF is **soluble**. Also: BaSO₄, PbSO₄, CaSO₄ are insoluble sulfate exceptions. Most carbonates and phosphates insoluble except Group 1 and NH₄⁺.

> [!danger] Dissolution of Ionic Compound = PHYSICAL Change
> On the AP exam, dissolving NaCl is **physical** — ions separate but no new compound is formed. Electrolysis of water = **chemical** (covalent O–H bonds broken). Criterion = bond type broken, not reversibility.

## Topic 4.1: Introduction to Reactions; Balancing Equations

**Essential Knowledge (Krug 4.1/4.2a, Farabaugh CED):**
- A chemical equation is a symbolic representation of a chemical reaction. Reactants are on the left; products are on the right.
- **Law of conservation of mass:** Atoms are neither created nor destroyed. The number of each type of atom must be equal on both sides of a balanced equation.
- **Law of conservation of charge:** In ionic equations, total charge is conserved. The sum of charges on the left must equal the sum on the right.
- **Balancing rule:** Only coefficients (numbers in front of formulas) may be changed. Subscripts within formulas are fixed — changing a subscript changes the identity of the substance.

**Balancing Procedure (Farabaugh):**
1. Start with the most complex molecule.
2. Balance metals first, then nonmetals, then hydrogen, then oxygen.
3. Use fractions if needed, then multiply through to clear fractions.
4. Verify all atoms and charges are balanced.

**Common Mistake:** Changing subscripts to balance. Writing $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ instead of $2\text{H}_2\text{O}$ changes the compound entirely. Always adjust coefficients only.

**State symbols (essential for NIEs and particulate diagrams):**
- (aq) = dissolved in water (ions or molecules are separate)
- (s) = solid
- (l) = pure liquid
- (g) = gas

## Topic 4.2: Net Ionic Equations

**Essential Knowledge (Krug 4.2b–c, Farabaugh CED):**
- **Molecular equation:** Full formulas for all species.
- **Complete ionic equation:** All strong electrolytes are written as dissociated ions; weak electrolytes, water, solids, and gases stay molecular.
- **Net ionic equation (NIE):** Cancel all spectator ions (ions that appear unchanged on both sides). Only species that actually change are included.

**Rules for what to split into ions:**
- Strong acids: HCl, HBr, HI, $\text{HNO}_3$, $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$, $\text{HClO}_4$ — split into $\text{H}^+$ and anion.
- Strong bases: Group 1 and 2 hydroxides (NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂) — split into metal ion and OH⁻.
- Soluble ionic salts: any salt that is (aq) per solubility rules — split into constituent ions.
- **Do NOT split:** weak acids (CH₃COOH, HF, H₂CO₃), weak bases (NH₃), water, insoluble precipitates (s), gases (g).

![[solubility_rules_u4.svg|697]]
*Left: always-soluble ions (Group 1, NH₄⁺, NO₃⁻, and most halides/sulfates with exceptions). Right: generally insoluble anions with their Group 1/NH₄⁺ exceptions. Check BOTH products when predicting precipitation.*

**Solubility Rules (Farabaugh — must memorize):**

| Always Soluble | Always Insoluble (exceptions noted) |
|---|---|
| Group 1 ions (Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺, Rb⁺, Cs⁺) | Most carbonates (CO₃²⁻) — except Group 1 + NH₄⁺ |
| NH₄⁺ compounds | Most phosphates (PO₄³⁻) — except Group 1 + NH₄⁺ |
| NO₃⁻ (all nitrates) | Most sulfides (S²⁻) — except Group 1, 2, NH₄⁺ |
| ClO₄⁻ (all perchlorates) | Most hydroxides (OH⁻) — except Group 1, Ba²⁺, Sr²⁺ |
| Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻ — except Ag⁺, Pb²⁺, Hg₂²⁺ | AgCl, AgBr, AgI (silver halides all insoluble) |
| SO₄²⁻ — except Ba²⁺, Pb²⁺, Ca²⁺ | BaSO₄, PbSO₄, CaSO₄ |

**Predicting products (Krug 4.2c):**
For double displacement (two aqueous ionic compounds mixed):
1. Swap the cations between the two anions to form two new combinations.
2. Check each combination against solubility rules.
3. If one combination is insoluble → precipitate forms → write NIE.
4. If both products are soluble → no reaction (NR).
5. Write NIE for only the ions that form the precipitate.

**Gas-forming reactions from acidic conditions (Farabaugh):**
These are double-displacement reactions where an unstable product decomposes to a gas:
- Carbonate + acid: $\text{CO}_3^{2-} + 2\text{H}^+ \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \text{CO}_2(g)$
- Sulfite + acid: $\text{SO}_3^{2-} + 2\text{H}^+ \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \text{SO}_2(g)$
- Sulfide + acid: $\text{S}^{2-} + 2\text{H}^+ \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{S}(g)$

The intermediate weak acid ($\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$, $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_3$) is unstable and immediately decomposes. Write the gas directly in the NIE; do not show the intermediate.

## Topic 4.3: Representations of Reactions

**Essential Knowledge (Krug 4.3/4.4, Farabaugh CED):**
- Particulate diagrams (particle models) show individual atoms, molecules, or ions as circles. They must be fully consistent with the balanced chemical equation.
- A correctly drawn particulate diagram:
  1. Has the same number of each type of atom on both sides.
  2. Has the same total charge on both sides (for ionic reactions).
  3. Shows correct stoichiometric ratios between all species.
  4. Represents phase correctly: (aq) ions are separate; (s) solids are clustered; (g) molecules are spread apart; (l) molecules are close together.

**Reading the stoichiometric ratio from a diagram:**
If a balanced equation is $a\text{A} + b\text{B} \rightarrow c\text{C}$:
- If the diagram shows $n$ molecules of A, it must show $(b/a) \times n$ molecules of B and $(c/a) \times n$ molecules of C.
- Count the particles on each side and reduce to the lowest whole-number ratio — that gives you the balanced equation coefficients.

**Identifying the reaction type from a particulate diagram (Farabaugh):**
- Precipitation: ions visible in solution on the left are absent (or fewer) on the right; a clustered solid appears.
- Gas-forming: a diatomic or small molecule appears in the gas phase on the right that was not present on the left.
- Acid-base/neutralization: H⁺ count decreases; new molecule (H₂O) appears; or weak acid molecule appears on products side.
- Redox: oxidation state of an element visibly changes (e.g., separate metal atoms + solution ions → metal-coated solid + dissolved metal ions).

**Common mistakes in particulate diagrams (MCQ-critical):**
- Drawing spectator ions as reacting (they must appear identically on both sides).
- Wrong particle ratio (e.g., showing 1:1 when the balanced equation is 2:1).
- Drawing dissolved ionic compound as a formula pair (e.g., drawing NaCl as a bonded pair instead of separate Na⁺ and Cl⁻ in solution).
- Showing a precipitate as separate ions instead of a clustered solid.

**Completing a partial diagram (AP FRQ pattern):**
Given the reactants side, you must draw the products side. Procedure:
1. Identify the type of reaction from the reactants (ions present → check solubility for precipitate).
2. Write the balanced NIE.
3. Draw product particles in the correct ratio; remove any ions that form the precipitate; keep spectator ions present and unchanged.

## Topic 4.4: Physical and Chemical Changes

**Essential Knowledge (Krug 4.3/4.4, Farabaugh CED):**
- **Physical change:** Only intermolecular (or interparticle) forces are broken or formed. The chemical identity of each substance is unchanged — the same molecules or ions are present before and after.
- **Chemical change:** Covalent bonds are broken and/or formed. New substances with different compositions are produced.

**Classification Framework:**

| Change | Type | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Melting, boiling, sublimation | Physical | Only IMFs broken; same molecules |
| Dissolving an ionic compound | Physical | Ions separate; no new compound formed; reversible by evaporation |
| Dissolving a molecular compound | Physical | Molecules disperse; same molecules intact |
| Precipitation reaction | Chemical | New ionic solid (different composition) forms |
| Acid–base neutralization | Chemical | New bonds in H₂O formed; new salt forms |
| Combustion | Chemical | C–H bonds broken; C=O and O–H bonds formed |
| Electrolysis of water | Chemical | O–H covalent bonds broken; H₂ and O₂ formed |
| Dissolving a metal in acid | Chemical | Metal–metal bonds broken; new H₂ and metal ion form |
| Phase changes of a pure substance | Physical | Composition is identical in all phases |

**AP Classification Rules (Farabaugh):**
- The criterion is **bond type broken/formed**, not reversibility. Reversibility is NOT the test.
- Dissolution of ionic compounds is classified as **physical** on the AP exam — the ionic bonds of the crystal lattice separate, but no new compound with a different formula is produced.
- Dissolution of molecular compounds is always **physical**.
- Any reaction where a precipitate, gas, or new molecular compound forms is **chemical**.

**Evidence of chemical change (Farabaugh — AP FRQ vocabulary):**
- Formation of a precipitate (solid)
- Evolution of a gas
- Color change (that is NOT reversible)
- Significant temperature change (exothermic or endothermic reaction)
- Emission of light
- An irreversible change in composition

**Common Mistake:** Calling a phase change "chemical" because "the substance changes form." Phase changes never break or form covalent or ionic bonds — only IMFs. The molecular formula is identical in all phases.

## Topic 4.5: Stoichiometry

**Essential Knowledge (Krug 4.5a–d, Farabaugh CED):**
- Stoichiometry uses the mole ratio from the balanced equation to convert between amounts of substances.
- **Limiting reactant (LR):** The reactant that is completely consumed first; determines the maximum theoretical yield.
- **Excess reactant:** Remains after the LR is completely consumed.
- **Theoretical yield:** Maximum product calculated from the LR.
- **Percent yield:** $\% \text{ yield} = \dfrac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100$

**Stoichiometry Roadmap:**
$$\text{mass A} \xrightarrow{\div M_A} \text{mol A} \xrightarrow{\times \text{mole ratio}} \text{mol B} \xrightarrow{\times M_B} \text{mass B}$$

**Limiting Reactant — Two-Method Approach:**
- **Method 1 (preferred):** Convert each reactant to moles of product using the mole ratio. The reactant producing the least product is the LR.
- **Method 2:** Convert one reactant to moles of the other needed. If the amount needed exceeds what's available, that reactant is the LR.

**Solution Stoichiometry:** $n = MV$ (molarity × volume in liters).

**Gas Stoichiometry:**
- At non-STP: use $n = PV/RT$ to find moles of gas.
- At STP (0°C, 1 atm): 1 mol of any ideal gas = 22.4 L.
- In a constant-$T$, constant-$V$ container: $P \propto n$, so if moles increase by a factor $k$, pressure increases by $k$.

**Limiting Reactant Warning — Doubling One Reactant (Farabaugh):**
If the original reaction is stoichiometric (exactly no excess), doubling only one reactant creates a new limiting reactant — **yield does not double.** To double the yield, both reactants must be doubled while preserving the mole ratio. Always recalculate which reactant is limiting after any change to amounts.

## Topic 4.6: Introduction to Titration

**Essential Knowledge (Krug 4.6, Farabaugh CED):**
- **Titration:** A technique to find the concentration of an unknown (analyte) using a standard solution (titrant) of known concentration, delivered from a buret.
- **Equivalence point:** Moles of titrant added = moles of analyte (adjusted for stoichiometric ratio). Detected by an indicator color change or pH meter inflection.
- For acid–base titrations: equivalence point = moles H⁺ = moles OH⁻.
- pH curve features: slow change far from equivalence point; very steep slope at the equivalence point.

**Titration Calculation:**
$$n_{\text{acid}} \times V_{\text{acid}} = n_{\text{base}} \times V_{\text{base}} \quad \text{(for 1:1 stoichiometry)}$$
For non-1:1 ratios (e.g., $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ + NaOH): adjust with the mole ratio from the balanced equation.

**Back Titration (Farabaugh — AP FRQ pattern):**
Used when direct titration is impractical: the analyte reacts too slowly, has no suitable indicator, or is insoluble in the titrant.

Procedure:
1. Add a **known, measured excess** of reagent A to the analyte. A reacts completely with the analyte.
2. **Titrate the leftover excess A** with a second titrant B of known concentration.
3. Calculate: moles of A that reacted with analyte = (moles A added) − (moles A remaining from titration).
4. Use the stoichiometric ratio to find moles of analyte, then concentration.

**Classic back titration example:** Determining CaCO₃ content.
- Add excess HCl of known concentration to the CaCO₃ sample.
- Remaining HCl is back-titrated with NaOH.
- Moles of HCl that reacted with CaCO₃ = (total HCl added) − (HCl remaining from NaOH titration).

**Common Titration Errors (Farabaugh):**

| Error | Effect on Calculated Concentration |
|---|---|
| Buret rinsed with distilled water (not titrant) before filling | Titrant diluted → more volume needed to reach equivalence → calculated analyte concentration **too high** |
| Overshoot the equivalence point (too much titrant added) | Extra moles counted → calculated analyte concentration **too high** |
| Indicator changes color before true equivalence point | Endpoint ≠ equivalence point → systematic error |
| Analyte flask rinsed with distilled water (not with analyte) | No effect — only volume and moles of analyte matter, not the water in the flask |

**Buret error memory rule:** Diluted titrant means more volume is needed to neutralize the same analyte → you think you used more moles of titrant than you actually did → calculated analyte concentration is **too high**. This is the most commonly tested error.

## Topic 4.7: Types of Chemical Reactions

**Essential Knowledge (Krug 4.7a–b, Farabaugh CED):**

**1. Synthesis (Combination):** Two or more reactants combine to form a single product.
- $\text{A} + \text{B} \rightarrow \text{AB}$
- Example: $2\text{Na}(s) + \text{Cl}_2(g) \rightarrow 2\text{NaCl}(s)$

**2. Decomposition:** One reactant breaks into two or more products.
- $\text{AB} \rightarrow \text{A} + \text{B}$
- Example: $2\text{H}_2\text{O}_2(l) \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \text{O}_2(g)$ (catalytic decomposition)
- Example: $2\text{HgO}(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{Hg}(l) + \text{O}_2(g)$

**3. Combustion:** Rapid reaction with O₂.
- Complete combustion of a hydrocarbon: $\text{C}_x\text{H}_y + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$
- Incomplete combustion produces CO or C (soot).

**4. Single Replacement — Activity Series (Krug 4.7a, Farabaugh):**
A free element displaces an ion of a less reactive element from a compound.

**Activity Series of Metals (most → least reactive):**
$$\text{Li} > \text{K} > \text{Ba} > \text{Ca} > \text{Na} > \text{Mg} > \text{Al} > \text{Zn} > \text{Fe} > \text{Ni} > \text{Sn} > \text{Pb} > \mathbf{H_2} > \text{Cu} > \text{Ag} > \text{Au}$$

![[activity_series_u4.svg|697]]
*Left: metals ranked by reactivity with H₂ as the reference line. Right: three prediction rules — metal displacing metal ion, metal reacting with acid (above H₂ only), and the halogen activity series.*

Rules for single replacement predictions:
- A metal higher on the series **displaces** a metal lower from its aqueous salt: $\text{Zn}(s) + \text{CuSO}_4(aq) \rightarrow \text{ZnSO}_4(aq) + \text{Cu}(s)$ ✓
- A metal lower on the series does **not** displace a metal higher: $\text{Cu}(s) + \text{ZnSO}_4(aq) \rightarrow \text{NR}$
- Metals **above H₂** on the series react with dilute acids to produce H₂ gas: $\text{Zn}(s) + 2\text{HCl}(aq) \rightarrow \text{ZnCl}_2(aq) + \text{H}_2(g)$
- Metals **below H₂** (Cu, Ag, Au) do NOT react with dilute acids — they require oxidizing acids (e.g., $\text{HNO}_3$).

**Activity series for halogens:** F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂. A halogen higher on the series displaces a lower halide from solution: $\text{Cl}_2(aq) + 2\text{KBr}(aq) \rightarrow 2\text{KCl}(aq) + \text{Br}_2(aq)$.

**5. Double Replacement (Metathesis):**
Ions from two ionic compounds switch partners.
- Precipitation: product is insoluble → NIE shows ions forming solid.
- Neutralization: acid + base → salt + water.
- Gas-forming: see Topic 4.2 for carbonate, sulfide, sulfite reactions.

**Oxidation Number Assignment (Krug 4.7b):**
Assign oxidation numbers before identifying redox reactions.

Rules (in order of priority):
1. Pure elements: ON = 0.
2. Monoatomic ions: ON = ion charge.
3. F in compounds: always −1.
4. O in compounds: −2 (except peroxides: −1; OF₂: +2).
5. H in compounds: +1 (except metal hydrides: −1).
6. Sum of all ONs in a neutral compound = 0; in a polyatomic ion = ion charge.

## Topic 4.8: Introduction to Acid–Base Reactions

**Essential Knowledge (Krug 4.8, Farabaugh CED):**

**Brønsted–Lowry Definitions:**
- **Acid:** Proton (H⁺) donor.
- **Base:** Proton (H⁺) acceptor.
- An acid–base reaction is a proton transfer from acid to base.

**Strong vs. Weak Acids and Bases:**
- **Strong acids** (memorize 6+1): HCl, HBr, HI, $\text{HNO}_3$, $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$, $\text{HClO}_4$ — fully dissociated; written as ions in NIEs.
- **Weak acids:** CH₃COOH, HF, $\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$, HCN, $\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4$ — partially dissociated; written as molecular formula in NIEs.
- **Strong bases:** Group 1 and 2 hydroxides — split into ions. Note: Ca(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂ are strong bases but have limited solubility.
- **Weak bases:** $\text{NH}_3$, amines — written molecular in NIEs.

**Conjugate Acid–Base Pairs (Farabaugh MCQ-critical):**
- When an acid donates H⁺, it becomes its **conjugate base** (differs by one H⁺).
- When a base accepts H⁺, it becomes its **conjugate acid** (differs by one H⁺).
- Conjugate pairs always differ by exactly one proton — no more, no less.
- **The trap:** The acid and base on the reactant side are NOT a conjugate pair. Each reactant is paired with its own product across the reaction arrow.
  - Example: $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{OH}^- \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COO}^- + \text{H}_2\text{O}$
  - Conjugate pair 1: CH₃COOH / CH₃COO⁻ (acid/conjugate base)
  - Conjugate pair 2: H₂O / OH⁻ (conjugate acid/base)
  - NOT a pair: CH₃COOH and OH⁻ (these are the acid and base reacting)

**Amphoteric Species:**
A species that can act as both an acid and a base (Brønsted–Lowry test: must have both an ionizable H to donate AND a lone pair/basic site to accept H⁺).

| Species | Amphoteric? | Acid reaction | Base reaction |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\text{HCO}_3^-$ | Yes | $\text{HCO}_3^- + \text{OH}^- \rightarrow \text{CO}_3^{2-} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ | $\text{HCO}_3^- + \text{H}^+ \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{CO}_3 \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2$ |
| $\text{HS}^-$ | Yes | $\text{HS}^- + \text{OH}^- \rightarrow \text{S}^{2-} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ | $\text{HS}^- + \text{H}^+ \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{S}$ |
| $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ | Yes | $\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{OH}^- \rightarrow \text{OH}^- + \text{OH}^-$ (autoionization) | $\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{H}^+ \rightarrow \text{H}_3\text{O}^+$ |
| $\text{HSO}_4^-$ | Yes | donates H⁺ → $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ | accepts H⁺ → $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ |
| $\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-$ | **No** | No ionizable H to donate | Can accept H⁺ → CH₃COOH (base only) |
| $\text{NH}_4^+$ | **No** | Can donate H⁺ → NH₃ (acid only) | N has no lone pair (4 bonds used) |

**Lewis Acid–Base Definitions (Krug 4.8, Farabaugh):**
- **Lewis acid:** Electron pair **acceptor** — has an empty orbital or low-electron site.
  - Examples: $\text{BF}_3$, $\text{Al}^{3+}$, $\text{H}^+$, $\text{Fe}^{3+}$, any cation.
- **Lewis base:** Electron pair **donor** — has a lone pair to share.
  - Examples: $\text{NH}_3$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{F}^-$, $\text{OH}^-$, any species with lone pairs.
- Lewis acid–base is the broadest definition: all Brønsted–Lowry reactions are Lewis reactions, but not vice versa.
- $\text{BF}_3 + \text{NH}_3 \rightarrow \text{BF}_3\text{NH}_3$: BF₃ is the Lewis acid (accepts lone pair from N); NH₃ is the Lewis base (donates lone pair). No proton transfer — not Brønsted–Lowry — but still a Lewis acid–base reaction.

**NIE Patterns for Acid–Base (Farabaugh):**
- Strong acid + strong base: $\text{H}^+(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq) \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$
- Weak acid + strong base: $\text{HA}(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq) \rightarrow \text{A}^-(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$
- Strong acid + weak base: $\text{H}^+(aq) + \text{B}(aq) \rightarrow \text{BH}^+(aq)$
- Carbonate + acid: $\text{CO}_3^{2-}(aq) + 2\text{H}^+(aq) \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \text{CO}_2(g)$

## Topic 4.9: Oxidation–Reduction (Redox) Reactions

**Essential Knowledge (Krug 4.9a–c, Farabaugh CED):**

![[redox_overview_u4.svg|697]]
*Left: OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain. Reducing agent is oxidized; oxidizing agent is reduced. Right: eight-step half-reaction method for balancing redox in acidic solution.*

**OIL RIG:**
- **Oxidation Is Loss** of electrons → oxidation number increases.
- **Reduction Is Gain** of electrons → oxidation number decreases.
- **Oxidizing agent:** Accepts electrons → is itself reduced (ON decreases).
- **Reducing agent:** Donates electrons → is itself oxidized (ON increases).
- Memory: "The reducing agent gets oxidized; the oxidizing agent gets reduced."

**Half-Reaction Method — Acidic Solution (Farabaugh):**
1. Split into oxidation and reduction half-reactions.
2. Balance atoms other than O and H.
3. Balance O by adding H₂O to the oxygen-deficient side.
4. Balance H by adding H⁺ to the hydrogen-deficient side.
5. Balance charge by adding electrons (e⁻) to the more positive side.
6. Multiply each half-reaction by an integer so the electrons cancel (LCM method).
7. Add half-reactions; cancel e⁻ and any water/H⁺ that appear on both sides.
8. Verify: all atoms balanced, all charges balanced, no e⁻ remain.

**Half-Reaction Method — Basic Solution (Krug 4.9c, Farabaugh):**
1. Follow all steps for acidic solution above (steps 1–7).
2. Count the number of H⁺ ions in the combined equation.
3. Add the same number of OH⁻ to **both** sides.
4. Combine H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O on the side where they're together.
5. Cancel any H₂O that appears on both sides.
6. Final check: no H⁺ should remain in the final equation; only H₂O and OH⁻ are acceptable.

**Combining Two Given Half-Reactions — LCM Method (Farabaugh):**
When the AP exam gives you two already-balanced half-reactions and asks you to combine them:
1. Identify which is oxidation (e⁻ on product side) and which is reduction (e⁻ on reactant side).
2. Find the LCM of the two electron counts.
3. Multiply each half-reaction by the appropriate factor so electrons cancel.
4. Add and cancel electrons (and any redundant water or H⁺).
- Example: oxidation produces 3e⁻, reduction consumes 2e⁻ → LCM = 6 → multiply oxidation ×2, reduction ×3.

**Disproportionation Reactions (Krug 4.9b — AP MCQ pattern):**
A disproportionation reaction is one in which a **single species is simultaneously oxidized AND reduced.** The same element in the same compound appears in two different products — one with a higher ON and one with a lower ON than in the reactant.

**Examples:**
- $2\text{H}_2\text{O}_2(l) \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \text{O}_2(g)$: O in H₂O₂ is −1 → some goes to −2 (in H₂O), some to 0 (in O₂). H₂O₂ is both oxidized and reduced.
- $\text{Cl}_2(aq) + 2\text{OH}^-(aq) \rightarrow \text{Cl}^-(aq) + \text{OCl}^-(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$: Cl₂ at 0 → Cl⁻ (−1, reduced) and OCl⁻ (+1, oxidized).

**Identifying a disproportionation reaction:** Look for a single reactant element whose ON splits into two different values in the products. If an element appears in only one reactant species and in two product species with different ONs, it's a disproportionation.

**Activity Series and Redox Connection (Farabaugh):**
The activity series predicts whether a metal will reduce a metal ion or H⁺:
- Higher metal on series = stronger reducing agent (more easily oxidized).
- Lower metal on series = weaker reducing agent (more stable in reduced form).
- Reaction is spontaneous if the metal is higher than the ion being displaced.

## Critical Reminders — Unit 4

> [!danger] Dissolution of Ionic Compound = PHYSICAL Change
> Ions separate but no new compound is formed. Criterion = **bond type broken**, NOT reversibility. Electrolysis of water = chemical (covalent O–H bonds broken). Dissolution of NaCl = physical.

> [!warning] NIEs: Only Species That CHANGE Are Included
> Water, weak acids, weak bases, precipitates (s), and gases stay molecular. Only strong electrolytes are split into ions. Cancel spectator ions that appear identically on both sides.

> [!check] Solubility Rules — Key Exceptions to Memorize
> ALL nitrates soluble. ALL Group 1 compounds soluble. AgCl/AgBr/AgI **insoluble**. BaSO₄/PbSO₄/CaSO₄ insoluble. Most carbonates and phosphates insoluble (except Group 1 and NH₄⁺).

> [!warning] Theoretical Yield: Use Limiting Reactant ONLY
> Never calculate yield from the excess reactant. Always identify the LR first: convert each reactant to moles of product → smallest result = LR.

> [!warning] Doubling One Reactant Does NOT Always Double Yield
> If originally stoichiometric (no excess), doubling one reactant just changes which is limiting. To double yield, BOTH reactants must be doubled in the correct stoichiometric ratio.

> [!danger] Buret Rinsed With Water → Calculated Concentration Too HIGH
> Diluted titrant → more volume needed → more moles of titrant counted → overestimates analyte concentration. Analyte flask rinsed with water → **no effect** on calculated concentration.

> [!check] OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain
> Oxidizing agent is **reduced** (ON decreases — it gains electrons). Reducing agent is **oxidized** (ON increases — it loses electrons).

> [!warning] ON Exceptions: H in Metal Hydrides = −1; O in Peroxides = −1
> H is −1 in NaH, CaH₂, LiH (NOT +1). O is −1 in H₂O₂, Na₂O₂. F is ALWAYS −1 in compounds. O is +2 in OF₂.

> [!warning] Conjugate Pair Trap
> The acid and base on the **reactant side** are NOT a conjugate pair. CH₃COOH and OH⁻ are reacting — the conjugate pairs are CH₃COOH/CH₃COO⁻ and H₂O/OH⁻.

> [!warning] Activity Series: Metals Below H₂ Don't React With Dilute Acids
> Cu, Ag, Au are below H₂ and do NOT react with dilute HCl or H₂SO₄. They require oxidizing acids (HNO₃). A metal higher than the ion it would displace → reaction; lower → NR.

> [!danger] Never Leave Electrons in the Final Balanced Redox Equation
> After the LCM step, all electrons must cancel. If e⁻ remain in the combined equation, redo the multiplication step.

> [!check] Basic Solution Redox: Balance Acidically First, Then Convert
> Always balance as if acidic (add H₂O and H⁺). Then add OH⁻ to both sides = number of H⁺. Combine H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O. Cancel redundant H₂O.

> [!warning] Disproportionation: Same Element Both Oxidized AND Reduced
> Signal: one reactant element appears in two products with different oxidation numbers. H₂O₂ → H₂O + O₂: O goes from −1 to −2 (reduced) AND 0 (oxidized).

> [!check] Gas Stoichiometry
> At STP (0°C, 1 atm): 1 mol ideal gas = 22.4 L. At non-STP: use $n = PV/RT$. In constant-T, constant-V container: $P \propto n$ (more moles → higher pressure).
