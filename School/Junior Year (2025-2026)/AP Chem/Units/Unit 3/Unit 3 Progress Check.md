## Q1. Which diagram shows the strongest IMF between two ethanol molecules?

Ethanol ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$) has an O–H group → can H-bond. H-bonding requires the H on an O (donor) interacting with a lone pair on O of another molecule (acceptor).

Diagram 2 shows the H atom bonded to O of one ethanol molecule pointing toward the O lone pair of the second molecule — the correct H-bond geometry.

**Answer: C — Diagram 2, because it shows the formation of a hydrogen bond between an H atom bonded to an O atom with an O atom from another molecule.**

H-bonding (H directly bonded to O interacting with O lone pair of a neighbor) is the strongest IMF in ethanol and requires the specific donor–acceptor geometry shown in Diagram 2.

> **Trap:** Choosing B (Diagram 1, "strong directional dipole-dipole"). Ethanol IS polar and DOES have dipole-dipole forces — but dipole-dipole is NOT the strongest IMF between ethanol molecules. Ethanol has an O–H group, making it capable of H-bonding, which is stronger. Diagram 1 shows generic alignment of two polar molecules (dipole-dipole geometry), but does NOT show the specific O–H···O hydrogen bond geometry required for H-bonding. The question asks for the *strongest* force, so you must go to H-bonding (Diagram 2), not dipole-dipole (Diagram 1).

## Q2. Which cation has the strongest ion–dipole attraction to water?

By Coulomb's law ($F \propto q/r^2$), ion–dipole force increases with higher charge and smaller radius.

- $\text{Li}^+$: charge $+1$, smallest group 1 radius
- $\text{Mg}^{2+}$: charge $+2$, small radius (Period 3)
- $\text{Na}^+$: charge $+1$, larger than $\text{Li}^+$
- $\text{Ca}^{2+}$: charge $+2$, larger radius (Period 4)

$\text{Mg}^{2+}$ has both higher charge (+2) and smaller radius than $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ → highest charge-to-size ratio.

**Answer: B — $\text{Mg}^{2+}$, because it has the largest charge-to-size ratio.**

By Coulomb's law, ion–dipole force is proportional to ionic charge and inversely proportional to $r^2$. $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ has twice the charge of $\text{Li}^+$ and a smaller radius than $\text{Ca}^{2+}$, giving it the highest charge-to-size ratio and therefore the strongest attraction to water's dipole.

> **Trap:** Choosing A ($\text{Li}^+$) because it is the smallest. Radius alone does not determine IMF strength — charge is equally critical. $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ wins because its doubled charge outweighs $\text{Li}^+$'s slightly smaller size.

## Q3. Group 1 metal + group 17 halide dissolves in water — what is the solute particle and interaction?

A group 1 + group 17 compound is an ionic salt. It dissociates into a cation ($\text{M}^+$) and an anion ($\text{X}^-$). Reading the diagram: the water molecules orient with their **oxygen ends** ($\delta^-$) pointing inward toward the central particle. By Coulomb's law, opposite charges attract — the negative $\delta^-$ oxygen orients toward a **positive** ion. Therefore the diagram represents the cation, and the interaction is ion–dipole.

Key diagram-reading rule: identify which end of the water dipole points toward the ion.
- O end ($\delta^-$) pointing inward → ion is **positive** (cation)
- H end ($\delta^+$) pointing inward → ion is **negative** (anion)

**Answer: C — The particle is a positive ion, and the interactions are ion–dipole attractions.**

By Coulomb's law, the negative end of the water dipole (oxygen, $\delta^-$) orients toward the positively charged ion. The group 1 cation (e.g., $\text{Na}^+$, $\text{K}^+$) is surrounded by water molecules whose oxygen atoms point inward. The attraction between the ion and the polar water molecule is an ion–dipole force — the strongest intermolecular interaction.

> **Trap:** Choosing B (negative ion, ion–dipole). B gets the force type right but the ion sign wrong. The oxygen end of water is $\delta^-$ and points toward the ion — so the ion must be positive for opposite charges to attract. If water oriented with H inward ($\delta^+$), that would indicate a negative ion.

## Q4. Which phosphorus allotrope has a higher melting point?

- Allotrope I: discrete $\text{P}_4$ molecules → molecular solid, held by LDF only
- Allotrope II: extended covalent network with P–P bonds throughout the lattice

Breaking covalent bonds (network solid) requires far more energy than overcoming LDFs (molecular solid).

**Answer: B — Allotrope II, because it has covalent bonds between phosphorus atoms that are stronger than the dispersion forces between the $\text{P}_4$ molecules in allotrope I.**

Allotrope II is a covalent network solid; melting requires breaking P–P covalent bonds throughout the lattice, which demands far more energy than overcoming the weak LDFs between discrete $\text{P}_4$ molecules in Allotrope I.

> **Trap:** Choosing A, reasoning that $\text{P}_4$ molecules contain covalent bonds. The covalent bonds inside $\text{P}_4$ are intramolecular — they are NOT broken during melting. Only the intermolecular LDFs between $\text{P}_4$ units are broken when Allotrope I melts.

## Q5. Crystalline NaBr(s) vs. molten NaBr(l) — electrical conductivity?

In crystalline NaBr, $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Br}^-$ are locked in fixed lattice positions — they cannot move, so no conductivity. When melted, ions become free to move and carry charge → good conductor. Conduction is via mobile ions, not electrons.

**Answer: D — Crystalline NaBr contains no freely moving electrons to conduct electricity, but molten NaBr is composed of freely moving $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Br}^-$ ions, which allows it to be a good conductor.**

Ionic solids conduct only when ions are mobile (molten or dissolved). In the solid, electrostatic attractions hold ions in fixed positions, preventing charge flow.

> **Trap:** Choosing A (about electrons). Ionic compounds in the molten state conduct via moving ions, not free electrons. Free electron conduction is characteristic of metals.

## Q6. Why is diamond harder than graphite?

- Diamond: each C forms 4 covalent bonds in a 3D tetrahedral network → rigid, hard
- Graphite: C forms 3 covalent bonds within planar layers; layers held together by weak LDFs → slides easily → soft

**Answer: C — Carbon atoms in diamond have four covalent bonds, whereas graphite is made of layers that are held together by relatively weak dispersion forces.**

Diamond's 3D covalent network requires breaking strong C–C bonds to deform the structure. Graphite's layers are bonded internally by covalent bonds but the layers themselves are held together only by LDFs and slide over one another.

> **Trap:** Choosing D (mobile electrons in diamond). Diamond does NOT have mobile/delocalized electrons — it is a non-conductor. It is graphite that has delocalized electrons within its layers, not diamond.

## Q7. Boyle's Law: 600 mL at 1.0 atm compressed to 50 mL at constant T

$$P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$$

$$P_2 = \frac{P_1 V_1}{V_2} = \frac{(1.0\ \text{atm})(600\ \text{mL})}{50\ \text{mL}} = 12\ \text{atm}$$

Volume decreased by factor of $600/50 = 12$.

**Answer: B — About 12 atm, because the volume of the gas mixture decreased by a factor of 12.**

By Boyle's law ($P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$ at constant T and n), pressure and volume are inversely proportional. A 12× decrease in volume produces a 12× increase in pressure.

> **Trap:** Choosing C (120 atm). The ratio is $V_1/V_2 = 600/50 = 12$, not 120. Students who subtract volumes ($600 - 50 = 550$) and use that as the factor arrive at ~550 atm (choice D), also wrong.

## Q8. Gay-Lussac's Law: $\text{O}_2$ at 10°C, 2.1 atm → heated to 40°C

Convert to Kelvin: $T_1 = 283\ \text{K}$, $T_2 = 313\ \text{K}$.

$$\frac{P_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2}{T_2} \Rightarrow P_2 = 2.1 \times \frac{313}{283} \approx 2.3\ \text{atm}$$

**Answer: B — 2.3 atm, because the pressure P increases by the proportion $\frac{313}{283}$.**

By Gay-Lussac's law ($P \propto T$ at constant V and n), pressure and Kelvin temperature are directly proportional. 10°C = 283 K, 40°C = 313 K; pressure scales up by $313/283 \approx 1.11$.

> **Trap:** Choosing D (8.4 atm) by using Celsius directly: $2.1 \times (40/10) = 8.4$. Temperature MUST be in Kelvin for all gas law calculations.

## Q9. Dalton's Law: $\text{O}_2$ (0.8 atm) transferred into $\text{N}_2$ container (1.0 atm), same volume

Contents of container 1 (oxygen, 0.8 atm) are transferred into container 2 (nitrogen, 1.0 atm), both 5.0 L rigid containers. Same volume, same T → partial pressures remain unchanged.

$$P_{total} = P_{O_2} + P_{N_2} = 0.8 + 1.0 = 1.8\ \text{atm}$$

**Answer: A — The total pressure is the sum of the initial pressures because pressure only depends on the total amount of gas when volume and temperature are held constant.**

By Dalton's Law, each gas exerts its partial pressure independently. At constant V and T, total pressure equals the sum of all partial pressures.

> **Trap:** Choosing C (higher than the sum due to IMFs). IMFs between gas molecules would actually reduce pressure below ideal, not increase it. Under ideal assumptions there are no IMFs, so pressures simply add.

## Q10. Which particle diagram best represents gas speeds at a fixed temperature?

At a fixed temperature, KMT states that gas particles have a distribution of speeds — some faster, some slower. The correct diagram shows particles with arrows of varying lengths (different speeds) and varying directions.

**Answer: D — Diagram 2, because the particles have a variety of different speeds.**

At any fixed temperature, gas particles have a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution of speeds — not all equal. The diagram showing particles with arrows of different lengths correctly represents this distribution.

> **Trap:** Choosing A (Diagram 1, same speed). Fixed temperature determines the average KE, not a single speed. Only at T = 0 K would all particles be stationary; at any real temperature, particles have a range of speeds.

## Q11. Two gases at same T: Gas X (narrower peak) vs. Gas Z (broader peak, shifted right) — what is correct?

Same temperature → same average KE for both. Gas Z peak is shifted to higher speeds → Gas Z molecules move faster on average → Gas Z has lower molar mass (lighter molecules move faster at the same KE by $KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$).

**Answer: C — Gas Z has a smaller molar mass than gas X.**

At the same temperature, all gases have the same average kinetic energy ($KE_{avg} = \frac{3}{2}RT$). Since $KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$, a lighter molecule must move faster to have the same KE. Gas Z's distribution shifted to higher speeds indicates lighter (lower molar mass) molecules.

> **Trap:** Choosing A (Gas Z has higher KE). Same temperature = same average KE by KMT, regardless of molar mass. The speed difference is entirely due to molar mass, not KE.

## Q12. Maxwell-Boltzmann: same $\text{O}_2$ sample at higher temperature (dashed line)

Higher temperature → molecules have higher average KE and higher average speeds. The distribution must:
1. **Shift right** — peak moves to higher speed (higher average speed)
2. **Broaden** — wider range of speeds (more spread)
3. **Lower peak height** — fewer molecules clustered at any single speed
4. **Same total area** — same number of molecules

Answer B showed the peak moving LEFT (lower speeds) with a larger height — this would represent LOWER temperature, not higher. The correct graph (C) shows the dashed curve with a peak shifted to the right and a lower maximum height.

**Answer: C — Dashed curve is right-shifted, broader, and has a lower peak than the original solid curve.**

At higher temperature, molecules have greater average kinetic energy ($KE_{avg} = \frac{3}{2}RT$), meaning higher average speeds. The Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution broadens and shifts right. Because the total area is conserved (same number of $\text{O}_2$ molecules), a broader curve necessarily has a lower peak.

> **Trap:** Choosing B (peak shifts left, gets taller). B shows a curve that would represent a LOWER temperature — more molecules at lower speeds, higher peak concentration. Students sometimes confuse "higher peak = more energy" with the actual shape rule. Higher T always = right-shifted + lower peak. The peak gets shorter because the distribution spreads out over a wider range of speeds while the total number of molecules is unchanged.

## Q13. $\text{C}_2\text{H}_6$ deviates more from ideal than $\text{CH}_4$ — why?

Both are nonpolar hydrocarbons with only LDFs. $\text{C}_2\text{H}_6$ is larger (more electrons, $M = 30\ \text{g/mol}$ vs. $16\ \text{g/mol}$) → larger, more polarizable electron cloud → stronger LDFs → greater deviation from ideal behavior.

**Answer: B — $\text{C}_2\text{H}_6$ molecules have a larger, more polarizable electron cloud than $\text{CH}_4$ molecules do.**

Deviations from ideal behavior increase with stronger IMFs. Both gases have only LDFs, but $\text{C}_2\text{H}_6$ has more electrons and a larger, more polarizable electron cloud, producing stronger intermolecular attractions that cause greater deviation from ideal pressure predictions.

> **Trap:** Choosing A (H-bonding). Neither $\text{C}_2\text{H}_6$ nor $\text{CH}_4$ has H bonded to N, O, or F — there is no H-bonding in either molecule. H must be directly bonded to N, O, or F to donate a hydrogen bond.

## Q14. $\text{NH}_3$ can be liquefied at lower pressure than $\text{CO}_2$ at 20°C — why?

$\text{NH}_3$: polar, H bonded to N → H-bonding + dipole–dipole + LDF.
$\text{CO}_2$: linear, nonpolar → LDF only.

Stronger IMFs in $\text{NH}_3$ → less compression (lower pressure) needed to condense it into a liquid.

**Answer: D — $\text{CO}_2$ is a nonpolar molecule that has London dispersion forces that are weaker than the dipole-dipole and London dispersion forces between the polar $\text{NH}_3$ molecules.**

$\text{NH}_3$ has stronger IMFs (H-bonding + dipole–dipole + LDF) than $\text{CO}_2$ (LDF only). Stronger attractive forces between $\text{NH}_3$ molecules mean less work (lower pressure) is needed to force them close enough for the IMFs to condense them into liquid.

> **Trap:** Choosing B ($\text{CO}_2$ has no significant IMF). $\text{CO}_2$ does have LDFs — every molecule does. The error in B is saying "no significant" IMFs. The correct distinction is that $\text{NH}_3$'s IMFs are stronger, not that $\text{CO}_2$ has none.

## Q15. Real gas has higher P than ideal at high pressure — which gas?

Among choices: $\text{SO}_2$ ($M = 64\ \text{g/mol}$) has the largest molecular volume.

**Answer: D — $\text{SO}_2$, because it has the largest molecular volume.**

At high pressure, real gas deviations arise because molecules occupy real volume. The actual free space in the container is less than the ideal gas law assumes, so pressure exceeds the ideal prediction. This deviation is greatest for molecules with the largest physical size.

**Answer: C — 62.5 mL**

Using $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$: moles of solute are conserved during dilution. $V_1 = (0.0250 \times 250.00)/0.100 = 62.5\ \text{mL}$ of 0.100 M stock is needed.

## Q19. Best method to separate a methanol/water mixture (miscible, BP 338 K vs. 373 K)

Both are miscible liquids forming a homogeneous solution. Filtration won't work (no solid). Chromatography is designed for dissolved components by polarity, not bulk liquid–liquid separation. Their boiling points differ by 35 K → distillation separates by BP difference.

**Answer: B — Distillation**

Methanol and water are fully miscible liquids with different boiling points (338 K vs. 373 K). Distillation exploits this difference — methanol (lower BP) vaporizes preferentially, is condensed and collected separately from water.

> **Trap:** Choosing C or D (chromatography). Chromatography separates dissolved solutes by differential affinity for stationary/mobile phases — it does not efficiently separate bulk miscible liquids. Distillation is correct when BP differences are given.

## Q20. How to identify the number of different-colored components in black ink?

Need to separate and count distinct colored compounds. Distillation separates by BP (not helpful for identifying colored components). Elemental analysis gives mass ratios, not number of compounds. Chromatography produces separate bands/spots — each band = one component.

Paper chromatography with solvents of varying polarity reveals the maximum number of distinct colored components.

**Answer: D — Paper chromatography using different solvents with a range of polarities as the mobile phase**

Paper chromatography reveals the number of distinct components as separate spots. Using solvents of varying polarity maximizes separation of components with different polarities, making it the best method to count how many colored compounds are present.

> **Trap:** Choosing B (elemental analysis). Elemental analysis gives mass percentages of elements, not the number of molecular compounds. It cannot distinguish between two different molecules.

## Q21. TLC: which solvent best separates two compounds for column chromatography?

Best separation in column chromatography requires the largest difference in $R_f$ values in TLC, at intermediate positions (not all spots at top, not all at bottom).

- Solvent A: one spot near origin → compounds not separated / barely moving
- Solvent B: two spots at intermediate, well-separated $R_f$ values → best separation
- Solvent C: two spots with less separation
- Solvent D: both spots near solvent front → both compounds elute together rapidly in a column

**Answer: B — Solvent B**

The most effective solvent for column chromatography gives the greatest difference in $R_f$ values at intermediate positions in TLC. Solvent B produces two well-separated spots, indicating the two compounds have sufficiently different affinities to be resolved in a column.

> **Trap:** Choosing D. When both spots are near the solvent front ($R_f \approx 1$), both compounds have high affinity for the mobile phase and will co-elute quickly in a column — no useful separation.

## Q22. Methanol ($\text{CH}_3\text{OH}$) dissolved in water — major attractive force between particles?

$\text{CH}_3\text{OH}$ is polar with an O–H group → H-bond donor and acceptor. Water also has O–H. Solution does not conduct (no ions). The major attractive force between methanol and water molecules is hydrogen bonding (O–H of one molecule ··· O lone pair of the other).

**Answer: C** — Shows the O lone pair of methanol attracting toward an H bonded to O of a water molecule — correct H-bond geometry.

Methanol and water both have O–H groups (donors) and O lone pairs (acceptors). In solution, the dominant attractive force is hydrogen bonding: H bonded to O in one molecule attracted to the lone pair on O of the other.

> **Trap:** Choosing A or B, which show ion-like interactions. Methanol is molecular and non-ionic — there are no full charges, only H-bonds and dipole–dipole forces.

## Q23. Conductivity increases linearly as ionic solid is added to water — what is correct?

Conductivity $> 0$ as soon as any solid is added and increases proportionally → solid dissolves and produces mobile ions. A linear graph in the tested range does not imply infinite solubility — it simply means saturation hasn't been reached within the range tested.

**Answer: A — The increase in conductivity indicates that the unknown is an ionic solid that dissociates into ions when it dissolves in water.**

When an ionic compound dissolves, it releases mobile ions that carry electrical current. More solid → more ions → higher conductivity. The linear increase is consistent with proportional ion concentration.

> **Trap:** Choosing C (infinitely soluble). A linear graph in the observed range does not prove infinite solubility. The graph only shows the tested range — saturation may occur at higher masses outside the graph.

## Q24. KCl solubility in water — which diagram and reason?

$\text{KCl}$ is ionic ($\text{K}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$). It dissolves because water molecules (polar) exert ion–dipole forces on the ions, pulling them away from the crystal lattice. The diagram should show separated, individually solvated ions with water molecules oriented appropriately — that is Diagram 1.

**Answer: A — Diagram 1, because strong ion–dipole interactions between KCl and water help to dissociate the solute.**

Ionic compounds dissolve in polar solvents because ion–dipole attractions between water's permanent dipole and the ions overcome the lattice energy. Diagram 1 shows separately hydrated $\text{K}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions, representing this solvation process.

> **Trap:** Choosing C or D (dipole–dipole or H-bonds). The force between an ion and water is specifically ion–dipole, not dipole–dipole (which is between two polar molecules) or H-bonding (which requires H covalently bonded to N/O/F).

## Q25. What happens when visible light photons are absorbed by dye molecules?

EM spectrum: visible/UV → electronic transitions. IR → vibrational. Microwave → rotational. Visible light causes electrons in the dye to be promoted to higher electronic energy levels.

**Answer: A — Certain electrons in the dye molecule move to a higher energy level, with the difference in energy between the lower and higher energy levels being the same as the energy of the absorbed photons.**

Visible light has enough energy to cause electronic transitions — electrons are promoted from a lower to a higher electronic energy level. The photon energy exactly matches the energy gap between levels ($E = h\nu$).

> **Trap:** Choosing B (vibrational) or C (rotational). Vibrational transitions are caused by IR radiation; rotational transitions by microwave. Visible light corresponds to electronic energy gaps, which are much larger.

## Q26. Infrared spectrum of $\text{CO}_2$ — what occurs at the molecular level?

IR radiation causes molecular bond vibrations (stretching and bending), not electronic transitions (UV/Vis) or rotations (microwave). The absorption dips in the IR spectrum correspond to specific bond vibrational frequencies.

**Answer: A — The atoms in the $\text{CO}_2$ molecules increase their vibration as the bonds between the atoms bend and stretch.**

Infrared radiation has the right energy to excite molecular bond vibrations. When $\text{CO}_2$ absorbs IR photons at specific wavelengths, the C–O bonds vibrate more energetically, corresponding to the absorption bands in the spectrum.

> **Trap:** Choosing C (electronic transitions). Electronic transitions require UV or visible light — much higher energy than IR. IR photons have too little energy to promote electrons to higher levels.

## Q27. Why is IR spectroscopy appropriate to confirm a carbonyl product?

IR detects bond vibrations. The C=O bond in a carbonyl group has a characteristic strong absorption in the IR spectrum (~1700 cm⁻¹) due to its stretching vibration. This is distinct from the O–H stretch in the starting alcohol.

**Answer: B — The absorption of infrared radiation leads to an increase in molecular vibrational level associated with the stretching of the carbonyl.**

IR radiation excites bond stretching and bending vibrations. The C=O bond has a characteristic strong IR absorption due to its stretching vibration, making IR spectroscopy ideal for confirming a carbonyl group in the product.

> **Trap:** Choosing A (electronic transitions from nonbonding electrons). IR does not cause electronic transitions — that requires UV/Vis. Choice A incorrectly applies UV/Vis reasoning to an IR question.

## Q31. Fingerprints on cuvette — effect on absorption curve?

Fingerprints scatter and absorb additional light beyond what the sample absorbs. The detector receives less transmitted light → calculates higher absorbance. This raises the measured absorbance at all wavelengths — the peak appears higher, not shifted in wavelength.

**Answer: A — The peak of the curve would be higher because more light would be absorbed.**

Fingerprints on the transparent sides scatter some incident light, reducing the intensity reaching the detector. Since $A = -\log(I_t/I_0)$, lower transmitted intensity gives higher measured absorbance. The peak shifts up in height; the wavelength position of the peak is unchanged.

> **Trap:** Choosing C or D (wavelength shift). Fingerprints do not change the wavelength at which the dye absorbs. Peak position (wavelength) stays the same; only peak height (absorbance value) increases.

## Q32. Light through frosted cuvette walls (wrong orientation) — effect on measured absorbance?

Calibration was done with light through transparent sides. Sample measured with light through frosted sides. Frosted glass scatters significantly more light than transparent glass → detector sees less transmitted light → measured absorbance is higher than actual.

**Answer: B — The measured absorbance of the $\text{FeSCN}^{2+}$ solution will be higher than the actual absorbance.**

The frosted sides scatter more light than the transparent sides used during calibration. This extra scattering reduces the transmitted intensity detected, making the instrument report a higher absorbance than the sample actually has.

> **Trap:** Choosing C (too low). Students might think "frosted = less light gets in so less is absorbed." The correct reasoning: scattered light never reaches the detector, mimicking additional absorption — the instrument interprets reduced transmitted intensity as higher absorbance regardless of cause.

## Q33. Student forgets to rinse cuvette with sample — water droplets remain — effect?

Residual distilled water dilutes the dye solution → lower concentration → lower absorbance. By Beer's law, $A = \varepsilon bc$; lower $c$ → lower $A$.

**Answer: A — The measured absorbance would be too low, because the distilled water left in the cuvette would slightly dilute the solution.**

The residual water dilutes the dye solution, reducing its concentration below the intended value. Since $A \propto c$ (Beer's law at constant $\varepsilon$ and $b$), a lower concentration produces a lower absorbance reading.

> **Trap:** Choosing D (too high because water absorbs light). Distilled water does not significantly absorb visible light — it is essentially transparent. The error here is exclusively dilution lowering concentration, not water absorbing light.
