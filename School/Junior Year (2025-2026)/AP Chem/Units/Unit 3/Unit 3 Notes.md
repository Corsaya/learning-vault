## Study Hub Quick Reference — Unit 3

> [!check] Q1 — ΔS Sign from Phase Transitions
> Solid → Liquid → Gas: each step **increases** entropy (ΔS > 0). More disordered phase = more accessible microstates. Melting: ΔS > 0 (endothermic, entropy increases). Freezing: ΔS < 0. Vaporization gives the **largest** positive ΔS (molecules fully separated, maximum disorder).

> [!check] Q5 — Ion-Dipole: Water Molecule Orientation Around an Ion
> In a diagram of a hydrated ion: if the **oxygen end** (δ⁻) of water points toward the ion → ion is **positive** (cation attracts δ⁻ oxygen). If the **hydrogen end** (δ⁺) points toward the ion → ion is **negative** (anion attracts δ⁺ hydrogen). The interaction is always **ion–dipole** — never hydrogen bonding.

> [!check] Q17 — Polarity, H-Bonding, and Solubility ("Like Dissolves Like")
> Polar solutes dissolve in polar solvents; nonpolar in nonpolar. For the AP exam, name the **specific IMFs** — not just "like dissolves like." Molecule with O–H or N–H → H-bonds with water → soluble. Nonpolar molecule → only LDFs → not miscible with water but soluble in nonpolar solvents.

> [!check] Q19 — KMT: Same T = Same Average KE; Lighter Molecule = Faster Speed
> At the same temperature, **ALL gases have the same average KE** ($KE_{avg} = \frac{3}{2}RT$). Since $KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$, if KE is equal and mass is smaller, speed must be larger. Lighter molecules move **faster** at the same T — but they do NOT have more kinetic energy.

> [!check] Q24 — Potential Energy vs. Distance: Net Force Direction
> Two atoms approaching: PE decreases as they get closer (attraction → energy released). At **equilibrium bond length**, PE is at minimum. Closer than equilibrium: nuclear repulsion dominates, PE rises sharply. The net force is **attractive** (pulling toward equilibrium) when above the minimum distance, **repulsive** when below.

> [!check] Q26 — London Dispersion Forces and Boiling Point
> Larger molecule = more electrons = larger, more polarizable electron cloud = **stronger LDFs** = **higher boiling point**. For nonpolar molecules: compare total electron count (≈ molar mass). Also: linear/unbranched molecules have greater surface area contact → stronger LDFs → higher BP than branched isomers of the same formula.

> [!check] Q30 — Maxwell-Boltzmann: Same Temperature → Same Distribution
> When two gas samples reach the same equilibrium temperature, their Maxwell-Boltzmann speed distributions become **identical** (same peak position, same shape) if they have the same molar mass. Thermal equilibrium = same average KE = same distribution curve. Heat flows until both temperatures equalize.

> [!check] Q45 — Nonpolar Symmetric Molecules: Only LDF
> Nonpolar symmetric molecules (N₂, O₂, CCl₄, noble gases) have no permanent dipole → **no dipole-dipole forces, no H-bonding**. Their ONLY IMF is **London dispersion force**. Never say they have "no IMFs" — LDFs are always present in every substance.

> [!check] Q48 — cis/trans Isomers: IMF and Vapor Pressure
> **cis** isomers: same substituents on same side → dipoles do NOT cancel → polar → **stronger IMFs** → higher BP → **lower vapor pressure**. **trans** isomers: substituents on opposite sides → dipoles cancel → nonpolar → weaker IMFs → lower BP → **higher vapor pressure** (evaporates more easily at room temperature).

> [!check] FRQ Q1F — H-Bonding Requires H Directly on N, O, or F
> H must be **directly bonded** to N, O, or F to form an H-bond (donor). The acceptor must also be N, O, or F with a lone pair. Ethanol (C₂H₅OH) has H on O → H-bonds → much higher BP than expected. CH₃F has H on C (not F) → **cannot H-bond**. Explicitly state "H is directly bonded to O/N/F" on FRQs.

> [!check] FRQ Q6A — Melting is Endothermic
> Melting requires energy input (ΔH_fus > 0, endothermic) to break intermolecular forces. Temperature stays **constant** during melting — energy goes into breaking IMFs (PE increases), not into increasing kinetic energy. Use $q = n \times \Delta H_{fus}$, not $q = mc\Delta T$, during the phase change.

> [!check] FRQ Q6C — Particle-Level Explanation of Density Change (e.g., Ice vs. Water)
> For water: in solid (ice), molecules are held in a fixed, **open hexagonal H-bonding network** → molecules held farther apart → less dense. When melted, thermal energy disrupts this network → molecules pack **more closely** → liquid water is denser than ice. Particle-level answer must name the H-bonding network and explain how melting disrupts it.

> [!warning] Trap — H-Bonding: H Must Be on N/O/F, Not Just Present in the Molecule
> CH₃F: F is present but H is on C → **NO H-bonding**. NH₄⁺: N is present but has no lone pair (all 4 bonds used) → **cannot be an H-bond acceptor**. Both the donor (H on N/O/F) and acceptor (lone pair on N/O/F) conditions must be satisfied.

> [!warning] Trap — Maxwell-Boltzmann: Higher T Shifts Peak RIGHT
> Higher temperature → peak shifts **RIGHT** (faster speeds), curve gets **LOWER and BROADER**. A left-shifted or taller peak = LOWER temperature. Memory: **"Faster = Right."**

> [!danger] LDFs Are Present in ALL Substances — Never Say "No IMFs"
> London dispersion forces exist in every molecule and atom, polar or nonpolar. Nonpolar molecules have LDFs **only** — but they DO have IMFs. Saying "nonpolar molecules have no intermolecular forces" is always wrong.

## Topic 3.1: Intermolecular Forces (IMFs)

**Essential Knowledge (Farabaugh CED):**
- IMFs are attractions **between** separate molecules or particles. They are weaker than intramolecular bonds (covalent or ionic bonds within a molecule).
- When a substance undergoes a **phase change** (e.g., boiling, melting), **intermolecular** forces are broken — intramolecular bonds stay **intact**.
- FRQ tip (Farabaugh): never say a molecule is nonpolar "because of symmetry." Always say "the bond dipoles cancel due to symmetry."

![[imf_ranking_u3.svg|697]]
*IMF types ranked weakest to strongest with examples, requirements, and the key Coulomb's-law rule for ion–dipole.*

**The Four IMFs (weakest to strongest, generally):**

**1. London Dispersion Forces (LDFs)**
- Present in **ALL** molecules and atoms — polar and nonpolar alike. Never say a substance has "no IMFs."
- Caused by **temporary, fluctuating dipoles** — momentary uneven electron distribution creates an instantaneous dipole that induces a dipole in a neighboring molecule.
- Strength increases with: (a) **more electrons** (larger electron cloud), (b) **higher polarizability**, (c) **greater surface area** between molecules (linear chains > branched, e.g., pentane > 2,2-dimethylpropane).
- Only IMF in noble gases and nonpolar molecules ($\text{N}_2$, $\text{O}_2$, $\text{CCl}_4$, $\text{CH}_4$, hexane).
- Can dominate over dipole–dipole forces when the nonpolar molecule is **much larger** (more electrons) than the polar one. Example: $\text{CCl}_4$ (M = 154) has higher BP than $\text{HCl}$ (M = 36.5) despite $\text{HCl}$ being polar.

**2. Dipole–Dipole Forces**
- Only between **polar molecules** with a permanent net dipole moment.
- The $\delta^-$ end of one molecule attracts the $\delta^+$ end of a neighbor.
- Strength depends on the magnitude of the dipole moment.
- Always act **in addition to** LDFs in polar molecules — polar molecules have both.

**3. Hydrogen Bonding (special, strong dipole–dipole)**
- Requires H covalently bonded to **N, O, or F** (the donor), attracted to a lone pair on **N, O, or F** of another molecule or the same molecule (the acceptor).
- Significantly stronger than ordinary dipole–dipole forces — explains anomalously high boiling points.
- H-bond donor rule: H must be directly bonded to N, O, or F.
- H-bond acceptor rule: the atom must be N, O, or F **and** must have a lone pair available.

| Molecule | H-bond donor? | H-bond acceptor? | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\text{NH}_3$ | Yes | Yes | H on N (donor); N has lone pair (acceptor) |
| $\text{NH}_4^+$ | Yes | **No** | H on N (donor); N has NO lone pair (4 bonds used) |
| $\text{NCl}_3$ | No | Yes | H NOT on N; N has lone pair |
| $\text{CH}_3\text{F}$ | No | No | H on C (not F); cannot donate. F has lone pair but no donor present. |
| $\text{HF}$ | Yes | Yes | H on F (donor); F has lone pairs (acceptor) |
| $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ | Yes | Yes | H on O (donor); O has lone pairs (acceptor) |

**Anomalous boiling points — hydrogen bonding:**
$\text{NH}_3$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, and $\text{HF}$ each have anomalously HIGH boiling points compared to group analogs because they H-bond. All three have H directly bonded to N/O/F.

**4. Ion–Dipole Forces**
- Between an **ion** and a polar molecule. Strongest IMF type.
- Critical for dissolving ionic compounds in polar solvents (e.g., NaCl in water).
- By Coulomb's law: $F \propto |q|/r^2$ — higher charge magnitude and smaller ionic radius → stronger force.
- **Charge-to-size ratio rule (MCQ key):** Compare charge first. A $2+$ ion beats a $1+$ ion almost always. Use radius as a tiebreaker within the same charge. Example: $\text{Mg}^{2+} > \text{Ca}^{2+} > \text{Li}^+ > \text{Na}^+$.

**Diagram-reading rule — ion hydration shell (MCQ key):**
In a particle diagram showing an ion surrounded by water molecules, identify which end of water points inward:
- **O end ($\delta^-$) pointing toward ion → ion is positive** ($\text{M}^+$ or $\text{M}^{2+}$); the negative oxygen is attracted to the positive charge.
- **H end ($\delta^+$) pointing toward ion → ion is negative** ($\text{X}^-$); the positive hydrogen is attracted to the negative charge.
The interaction is always **ion–dipole** — never hydrogen bonding, never dipole–dipole.

**Key Decision Rule (comparing IMF strength):**
- Same electron count: polar > nonpolar (dipole–dipole + LDF > LDF only).
- Small polar vs. large nonpolar: **cannot predict** without experimental data — use given BP or boiling point data.
- H-bonding possible: expect significantly higher BP than similar-mass non-H-bonding molecules.
- Ion present: ion–dipole dominates over all molecular IMFs.

## Topic 3.2: Properties of Solids

**Essential Knowledge (Farabaugh CED):**
- **Vapor pressure:** Pressure exerted by vapor in equilibrium with its liquid at a given temperature. Increases with temperature (more molecules have enough KE to escape the liquid phase).
- Higher IMF strength → **lower** vapor pressure (harder for molecules to escape) → **higher** boiling point.
- **Boiling point:** Temperature at which vapor pressure = external pressure. Normal BP is when VP = 1 atm.

![[solids_types_u3.svg|697]]
*Four solid types with particles, forces, conductivity, MP, and exam examples for rapid identification.*

**Four Types of Solids:**

| Type | Particles | Attractive Force | Conductivity | Melting Point | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ionic | Cations + anions | Electrostatic | Only when melted/dissolved | High | NaCl, MgO, $\text{CaCl}_2$, KBr |
| Covalent network | Atoms (covalently bonded) | Covalent bonds throughout | None (except graphite) | Very high | Diamond, $\text{SiO}_2$, SiC |
| Molecular | Molecules | IMFs (LDF, D-D, H-bond) | None | Low | $\text{I}_2$, $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, glucose |
| Metallic | Metal cations + e⁻ sea | Metallic bonds (delocalized e⁻) | Yes (as solid) | Varies | Na, Cu, Fe, Au |

**Key exam fact — Graphite exception:** Layers held together by LDF between sheets (slides easily → soft, lubricant). Within layers, delocalized π electrons → conducts electricity **along** layers. Graphite is a covalent network solid that IS a conductor.

**Melting a solid breaks INTER-particle forces only:**
- Molecular solid: melting breaks IMFs between molecules. Covalent bonds within the molecule stay intact.
- Covalent network solid: melting requires breaking covalent bonds throughout the lattice → very high energy.
- This is why $\text{P}_4$ (molecular) melts at ~44°C while red phosphorus (network) melts at much higher temperature.

**AP identification procedure (from Farabaugh):** Given unknown solid data:
1. Very high MP + no conductivity (solid or dissolved) → covalent network
2. High MP + conducts when dissolved/melted → ionic
3. Low MP + no conductivity → molecular
4. Conducts as solid → metallic

**Crystalline vs. amorphous solids:**
- Crystalline: regular repeating lattice structure, sharp melting point (e.g., NaCl, diamond, ice).
- Amorphous: no long-range order, gradually soften over a range (e.g., glass, rubber, most plastics).

## Topic 3.3: Solids, Liquids, and Gases

**Essential Knowledge (Farabaugh CED):**
- **Solid:** Particles in fixed positions, vibrational motion only. Definite shape and volume. Essentially incompressible.
- **Liquid:** Particles in contact but mobile, flow and slide. Definite volume, no definite shape. IMF-dependent properties: surface tension (resistance to increasing surface area), viscosity (resistance to flow), and capillary action (rising in narrow tubes — adhesion vs. cohesion).
- **Gas:** Particles far apart, constant rapid random motion. Neither definite shape nor volume. Highly compressible. Essentially no IMFs (ideal model).
- Solid and liquid have similar molar volumes (particles in close contact); gas molar volume is ~1000× larger at STP.

**Comparing phases for a given substance:**
- $\text{H}_2\text{O}$: ice → water → steam all involve the same molecules; only IMFs (H-bonds) are made/broken during phase changes, never the O–H covalent bonds.

## Topic 3.4: Ideal Gas Law

**Essential Knowledge (Krug 3.4a–c, Farabaugh CED):**
- Ideal gas model assumes: (1) particles have negligible volume, (2) no attractive or repulsive forces between particles, (3) all collisions are perfectly elastic, (4) average KE proportional to T(K).
- Ideal behavior is approached at **high T** and **low P** (where particles are far apart and moving fast).

**Key Equations:**

$$PV = nRT \qquad R = 0.08206\ \frac{\text{L·atm}}{\text{mol·K}}$$

$$P_A = X_A \times P_{total} \qquad X_A = \frac{n_A}{n_{total}}$$

$$P_{total} = P_A + P_B + P_C + \cdots \quad \text{(Dalton's Law of Partial Pressures)}$$

$$M = \frac{mRT}{PV} = \frac{dRT}{P} \qquad (d = \text{density in g/L, at given T and P})$$

![[gaslaws_separation_u3.svg|697]]
*Left: gas law equations with variable relationships. Right: separation method decision tree.*

**Variable Relationships (all other variables constant):**

| Law | Variables | Relationship | Equation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boyle's | P and V | Inversely proportional | $P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$ |
| Charles's | V and T | Directly proportional | $V_1/T_1 = V_2/T_2$ |
| Gay-Lussac's | P and T | Directly proportional | $P_1/T_1 = P_2/T_2$ |
| Avogadro's | V and n | Directly proportional | $V_1/n_1 = V_2/n_2$ |

**Temperature in Kelvin is non-negotiable:**
$$T(\text{K}) = T(°\text{C}) + 273$$
Using Celsius in gas law calculations is the single most common error on the AP exam.

**Water displacement (Dalton's Law application):**
Gas collected by displacement of water contains water vapor at that temperature's vapor pressure.
$$P_{\text{gas}} = P_{\text{atm}} - P_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}$$
The vapor pressure of water at each temperature must be given (or looked up from the reference table).

**Molar mass from gas density:**
$$M = \frac{dRT}{P}$$
A gas with density $d = 1.31\ \text{g/L}$ at 1.00 atm, 298 K: $M = (1.31)(0.08206)(298)/1.00 = 32.0\ \text{g/mol}$ → $\text{O}_2$.

## Topic 3.5: Kinetic Molecular Theory (KMT)

**Essential Knowledge (Krug 3.5, Farabaugh CED):**
- KMT is the theoretical model that explains why ideal gas assumptions work:
  - Particles in constant, random, rapid straight-line motion.
  - Collisions between particles and with walls are elastic (no net energy loss).
  - No intermolecular forces between gas particles.
  - Particle volume is negligible compared to container volume.
- Temperature (Kelvin) is directly proportional to the **average kinetic energy** of particles:

$$KE_{\text{avg}} = \frac{3}{2}RT \quad \text{(per mole)}$$

- **At the same temperature, ALL gases have the same average KE** — regardless of molar mass.
- Since $KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$, if KE is the same and mass is smaller, speed must be larger: lighter particles move faster.

**Maxwell–Boltzmann Speed Distribution:**

![[maxwell_boltzmann_u3.svg|697]]
*Left: two gases at same T — lighter gas has broader, right-shifted curve. Right: same gas at two temperatures — higher T is broader, lower-peak, right-shifted.*

- At a given T, particles have a **range** of speeds — not all equal. The curve shows the distribution.
- The peak is the **most probable speed**. The average speed is slightly to the right of the peak.
- **Lighter gas at same T:** broader curve, peak shifted right (faster average speed), lower peak height.
- **Same gas at higher T:** broader curve, peak shifts **RIGHT** (higher average speed), lower peak height. Area under curve = same (same number of molecules).
- **Common mistake:** confusing direction of shift. Higher T → peak moves RIGHT (more molecules at high speeds). A left-shifted or taller peak would represent LOWER temperature.
- **Memory rule:** Higher T = molecules move FASTER → peak moves to the RIGHT side of the speed axis. Think: faster = right.
- At same T: different gases have the same average KE but different average speeds. This is the most-tested KMT MCQ pattern.

**Effusion and Diffusion (Graham's Law):**
Lighter gases effuse (escape through a tiny hole) and diffuse (spread through space) faster.
$$\frac{v_1}{v_2} = \sqrt{\frac{M_2}{M_1}}$$
Not always directly tested but explains why H₂ leaks faster than O₂.

## Topic 3.6: Deviations from Ideal Gas Law

**Essential Knowledge (Krug 3.6, Farabaugh CED):**
- Real gases deviate from ideal behavior for two reasons:
  1. **IMF between particles** → at low T, particles slow down, attractions become significant → actual pressure is **LESS** than ideal (particles don't hit walls as hard; intermolecular attractions "pull them back").
  2. **Finite particle volume** → at high P, particles are compressed close together → actual volume is **GREATER** than ideal (less free space than assumed; the molecules themselves take up space).

| Condition | Dominant Deviation | Effect on P (vs. ideal) | Effect on V (vs. ideal) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low T | IMF | P < ideal | — |
| High P | Particle volume | — | V > ideal |

- Ideal behavior is best approached at: **high temperature** and **low pressure**.
- Deviations are largest at: **low T** (IMF) and **high P** (volume).
- Gases with **stronger IMF** ($\text{NH}_3$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{SO}_2$) deviate more at low T than those with weaker IMF (He, $\text{H}_2$).
- Gases with **larger molecular volume** ($\text{SO}_2 > \text{Xe} > \text{N}_2 > \text{He}$) deviate more at high P.

**High-P graph interpretation (AP MCQ type):** If real gas curve is **above** the ideal gas curve on a P vs. V plot, particle volume dominates (V > ideal → needs more P to compress). The gas with the largest molecules deviates most.

## Topic 3.7: Solutions and Mixtures

**Essential Knowledge (Krug 3.7, Farabaugh CED):**
- A **solution** (homogeneous mixture) has uniform composition throughout. All macroscopic properties are the same at every point.
- A **heterogeneous mixture** has variable composition; macroscopic properties depend on location in the mixture.
- Solutions can be solid, liquid, or gas (e.g., air is a gas-phase solution).

**Molarity:**
$$M = \frac{\text{mol solute}}{\text{L solution}} \qquad n = MV$$

**Preparing solutions from a solid (Krug procedure):**
1. Weigh out the calculated mass of solid.
2. Add to a clean volumetric flask.
3. Dissolve in a **small** amount of distilled water, swirling until fully dissolved.
4. Dilute with distilled water to the calibration mark.
5. Stopper and invert to mix.

**Common lab errors (Farabaugh):**
- Adding water first, then solid → flask already at volume before solid dissolves → concentration too low.
- Dissolving solid, then adding water beyond the calibration mark → volume too high → concentration too low.
- Not rinsing the volumetric flask with the solution before use → dilution error.

**Dilution:**
$$M_1V_1 = M_2V_2 \qquad \text{(moles of solute conserved)}$$
Measure $V_1$ of concentrated stock, transfer to volumetric flask, dilute to the mark.

**Ion concentration from dissolved ionic compounds:**
When an ionic compound dissolves, each formula unit releases its constituent ions. $[\text{Na}^+]$ from $0.0500\ M\ \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4$ is $2 \times 0.0500 = 0.100\ M$ (two $\text{Na}^+$ per formula unit).

## Topic 3.8: Representations of Solutions

**Essential Knowledge (Krug 3.8a–b, Farabaugh CED):**
- Particulate diagrams of solutions show individual ions or molecules with correct relative counts.
- When writing net ionic equations for precipitation reactions, spectator ions are omitted. The filtrate after precipitation contains the spectator ions and still conducts electricity.

**Electrolytes vs. non-electrolytes:**
- **Strong electrolyte:** completely dissociates into ions in solution. Conducts electricity well. Includes strong acids ($\text{HCl}$, $\text{HNO}_3$), strong bases ($\text{NaOH}$, $\text{KOH}$), and all soluble ionic salts.
- **Weak electrolyte:** partially dissociates. Conducts electricity weakly. Includes weak acids ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$) and weak bases ($\text{NH}_3$).
- **Non-electrolyte:** does not dissociate. Does not conduct electricity. Includes molecular compounds like glucose, methanol, urea.

**Conductivity as an identification tool:**
- Solid that dissolves and increases conductivity proportionally to mass added → ionic solid (releases mobile ions).
- A linear conductivity vs. mass graph does NOT imply infinite solubility — it only shows behavior in the tested range.

## Topic 3.9: Separation of Solutions and Mixtures; Chromatography

**Essential Knowledge (Krug 3.9/3.10, Farabaugh CED):**

**Chromatography separates based on differential IMF interactions:**
- **Stationary phase:** stays fixed (e.g., polar paper in TLC/paper chromatography).
- **Mobile phase:** moves through (e.g., solvent).
- Component with stronger attraction to stationary phase → travels **less far** (smaller $R_f$).
- Component with stronger attraction to mobile phase → travels **farther** (larger $R_f$).
- Polar stationary phase: more polar component sticks → smaller $R_f$; less polar component travels farther → larger $R_f$.

$$R_f = \frac{\text{distance traveled by component}}{\text{distance traveled by solvent front}}$$

**TLC → Column chromatography connection:**
- Run TLC first with candidate solvents to find the best mobile phase.
- Best solvent: gives the largest separation between spots at **intermediate** $R_f$ values (0.3–0.7 range).
- Spots all near the top ($R_f \approx 1$) → both compounds sweep through column together → no separation.
- Spots all near the bottom ($R_f \approx 0$) → both compounds stick to column → no elution.
- Transfer the winning TLC solvent to the column for bulk separation.

**Distillation:**
- Separates miscible liquids with **different boiling points**.
- Component with lower BP (weaker IMF) vaporizes first and condenses in the distillate first.
- Example: ethyl acetate (BP 77°C) distills before butyl acetate (BP 126°C) because it has a smaller, weaker-LDF molecule.
- The condenser cools vapor back to liquid; the receiving flask collects it.

**Separation method decision tree:**

| Situation | Method |
|---|---|
| Solid suspended in liquid (insoluble) | Filtration |
| Miscible liquids, different BPs | Distillation |
| Dissolved components — count or identify them | Paper / TLC chromatography |
| Dissolved components — bulk separation | Column chromatography |

## Topic 3.10: Solubility

**Essential Knowledge (Krug 3.9/3.10, Farabaugh CED):**
- **"Like dissolves like":** Substances with similar IMFs tend to dissolve in one another.
  - Polar solutes dissolve in polar solvents (e.g., NaCl in $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ — ion–dipole overcoming lattice energy).
  - Nonpolar solutes dissolve in nonpolar solvents (e.g., $\text{I}_2$ in hexane — LDF matching).
  - Polar solute in nonpolar solvent: IMF mismatch → insoluble.
- **AP exam caution:** "Like dissolves like" is a conceptual shorthand only. On the AP exam, you must explain *which specific IMFs* make the dissolution favorable.

**Miscibility vs. immiscibility:**
- **Miscible:** Liquids that mix in all proportions (ethanol + water: both polar, H-bonding).
- **Immiscible:** Liquids that do not mix ($\text{CCl}_4$ + water: one nonpolar, one polar).

**Why ionic compounds dissolve in water:**
Ion–dipole forces between water molecules and the ions overcome the lattice energy holding the crystal together. Cations are surrounded by water with $\delta^-$ O atoms pointing inward; anions are surrounded by water with $\delta^+$ H atoms pointing inward.

**Polarity rule for molecular solutes:**
- Polar molecular solutes (methanol, ethanol, glucose) dissolve in water because they can H-bond with water or form strong dipole–dipole / H-bond interactions.
- Nonpolar molecular solutes (wax, fats, oils) dissolve in nonpolar solvents (hexane, benzene) because both rely on LDFs.

## Topic 3.11: Spectroscopy and the Electromagnetic Spectrum

**Essential Knowledge (Krug 3.11, Farabaugh CED):**
- Different regions of the EM spectrum interact with different molecular energy levels:
  - **Microwave:** Causes molecular **rotation** (whole molecule spins).
  - **Infrared (IR):** Causes bond **vibration** (stretching and bending of bonds).
  - **Visible / UV:** Causes **electronic transitions** (electrons promoted to higher energy levels).
  - **High-energy UV / X-ray:** Causes **bond breaking** (photodissociation).
- Energy increases: microwave $<$ IR $<$ visible $<$ UV $<$ X-ray.
- Frequency ($\nu$) and energy are directly proportional; wavelength ($\lambda$) and energy are inversely proportional.

![[em_spectrum_u3.svg|697]]
*EM spectrum with frequency ranges, energy ranges, molecular events, and the visible energy bracket trick.*

**Key Equations:**
$$c = \lambda\nu \qquad E = h\nu \qquad E = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$$
$$c = 3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s} \qquad h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J·s}$$

**EM spectrum numerical ranges (from AP exam table):**

| Region | Frequency (Hz) | Energy (J/photon) | Molecular event |
|---|---|---|---|
| Microwave | $3 \times 10^9 - 3 \times 10^{11}$ | very low | Rotation |
| Infrared | $3 \times 10^{11} - 4 \times 10^{14}$ | $2 \times 10^{-22} - 3 \times 10^{-19}$ | Bond vibration |
| Visible | $4 \times 10^{14} - 7.5 \times 10^{14}$ | $3 \times 10^{-19} - 5 \times 10^{-19}$ | Electronic transition |
| Ultraviolet | $7.5 \times 10^{14} - 3 \times 10^{16}$ | $5 \times 10^{-19} - 2 \times 10^{-17}$ | Electronic transition / bond breaking |

**Visible energy bracket MCQ strategy:** On table-based questions, the visible energy range lies between the top of the IR range and the bottom of the UV range. A value of $4 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$ is visible; $9 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$ is in the UV range.

**IR spectroscopy (Krug 3.11, Farabaugh):**
- IR spectra show bond stretching and bending modes as absorption dips at characteristic wavenumbers.
- C=O carbonyl stretch: strong absorption near $1700\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ — confirms presence of aldehyde, ketone, carboxylic acid, or ester.
- O–H stretch: broad absorption $3200–3600\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ — confirms alcohol or carboxylic acid.
- Why IR identifies functional groups: each bond type has a characteristic vibrational frequency. IR can confirm if an oxidation reaction from alcohol to carbonyl was successful by appearance of the C=O peak.

## Topic 3.12: Properties of Photons (Photoelectric Effect)

**Essential Knowledge (Krug 3.12, Farabaugh CED):**
- Light behaves as both a wave and a particle (photon). Each photon has energy $E = h\nu$.
- **Photoelectric effect:** When light hits a metal surface, electrons are ejected only if the photon has energy $\geq$ the binding energy (threshold frequency).
  - Below threshold frequency: no electrons ejected, regardless of intensity.
  - At or above threshold: electrons are ejected. Excess energy becomes kinetic energy of the ejected electron.
  - **Intensity** of light = number of photons per second → affects number of electrons ejected.
  - **Frequency** of light → affects kinetic energy of ejected electrons.
  - Higher frequency ≠ more electrons; higher intensity ≠ more KE per electron.
- This demonstrated that light comes in discrete packets (quanta), not continuous waves.

**Photon energy calculation:**
$$E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$$
If $E_{\text{photon}} \geq E_{\text{binding}}$, electron is ejected. Excess energy: $KE_{\text{electron}} = E_{\text{photon}} - E_{\text{binding}}$.

## Topic 3.13: Beer–Lambert Law

**Essential Knowledge (Krug 3.13, Farabaugh CED):**

$$A = \varepsilon bc$$

- $A$ = absorbance (unitless; measured by spectrophotometer)
- $\varepsilon$ = molar absorptivity $(\text{M}^{-1}\text{cm}^{-1})$ — property of the substance at a given wavelength
- $b$ = path length (cm) — typically 1.00 cm (standard cuvette)
- $c$ = molar concentration (mol/L)

- $A$ is directly proportional to $c$ when $\varepsilon$ and $b$ are constant → **calibration curve is linear**.
- Set spectrophotometer to $\lambda_{\text{max}}$ (wavelength of maximum absorbance) for highest sensitivity and maximum linearity of the calibration curve.
- Read unknown concentration from calibration curve: measure $A$ → read $c$ from graph.

**Calibration curve procedure:**
1. Prepare a series of standard solutions of known concentration.
2. Measure absorbance of each standard at $\lambda_{\text{max}}$.
3. Plot $A$ (y-axis) vs. $c$ (x-axis) → straight line through origin (slope = $\varepsilon b$).
4. Measure $A$ of unknown → read $c$ from the line.

**Cuvette handling errors:**

![[beers_law_errors_u3.svg|697]]
*Complete cuvette error analysis: anything reducing transmitted light → A too high; dilution → A too low.*

| Error | Effect on measured A | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Fingerprints on transparent cuvette sides | Too HIGH | Scatters extra light → less $I_t$ → higher A |
| Light through frosted cuvette walls (wrong orientation) | Too HIGH | Frosted surface scatters more; less $I_t$ to detector |
| Path length longer than standard | Too HIGH | More absorbing species in beam; $A = \varepsilon bc$, $b \uparrow$ |
| Water droplets remaining / cuvette not rinsed with sample | Too LOW | Dilutes solution → lower $c$ → lower A |
| Wavelength not at $\lambda_{\text{max}}$ | Too LOW | $\varepsilon$ is lower at non-peak $\lambda$; peak position on spectrum unchanged |

## AP Answer Templates — Unit 3

> **IMF ranking justification:** "[Molecule A] has a higher boiling point than [molecule B] because [A] experiences [hydrogen bonding / stronger dipole–dipole forces / stronger LDFs due to larger electron cloud], which are stronger intermolecular attractive forces. More energy is required to vaporize [A], resulting in a higher boiling point."

> **Why vapor pressure increases with temperature:** "As temperature increases, a greater fraction of molecules in the liquid phase have sufficient kinetic energy to overcome the intermolecular attractive forces and escape into the gas phase. This increases the rate of evaporation and raises the vapor pressure."

> **Solid type identification:** "Solid [X] is a [type] solid because it has a [high/low] melting point and [conducts/does not conduct] electricity as a solid/when dissolved. This is consistent with [type of particles] held together by [type of force]."

> **Molecular vs. network allotrope:** "Allotrope [II] has a higher melting point because it is a covalent network solid in which covalent bonds extend throughout the entire lattice. Melting requires breaking these covalent bonds, which demands much more energy than overcoming the weak London dispersion forces between discrete [P₄] molecules in allotrope [I]."

> **Why ionic compound dissolves in water:** "When [KCl] dissolves in water, the polar water molecules exert ion–dipole forces on the $\text{K}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions. These ion–dipole attractions are strong enough to overcome the electrostatic (lattice) forces holding the ions in the crystal structure, drawing the ions into solution."

> **Ion–dipole strength (Coulomb's law):** "By Coulomb's law, the force between an ion and a polar molecule is proportional to the magnitude of the ionic charge and inversely proportional to the square of the distance. $[\text{Ion X}]$ has a higher charge-to-size ratio than $[\text{Ion Y}]$ because it has [greater charge / smaller radius], resulting in stronger ion–dipole attractions with water molecules."

> **Chromatography polarity:** "Component [X] has a higher $R_f$ value because it has greater affinity for the [nonpolar] mobile phase than for the [polar] stationary phase, indicating that [X] is less polar than component [Y]."

> **Beer's Law error — smudge or frosted wall:** "The measured absorbance would be too high. The [fingerprints / frosted walls] scatter some of the incident light before it reaches the detector, reducing the transmitted intensity. Since $A = -\log(I_t/I_0)$, a lower transmitted intensity yields a higher measured absorbance than the actual value."

> **Beer's Law error — dilution:** "The measured absorbance would be too low. The residual [water / rinse solution] dilutes the sample, reducing the molar concentration of the dye. Since $A = \varepsilon bc$ and $A \propto c$, a lower concentration produces a lower absorbance reading."

> **KMT — same temperature, different molar masses:** "At the same temperature, all gases have the same average kinetic energy, since $KE_{\text{avg}} = \frac{3}{2}RT$. Since $KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$, a gas with lower molar mass must have a higher average speed to achieve the same kinetic energy."

> **Real gas deviation at high pressure:** "At very high pressure, gas molecules are forced close together and the volume of the molecules themselves becomes a significant fraction of the total volume. This means the actual volume is greater than that predicted by the ideal gas law, causing the measured pressure to be higher than the ideal prediction."

## Critical Reminders — Unit 3

> [!danger] LDFs Exist in ALL Substances — Never Say "No IMFs"
> London dispersion forces are present in every molecule and atom, polar or nonpolar. Nonpolar molecules have LDFs **only** — but they DO have IMFs.

> [!danger] H-Bonding: H Must Be DIRECTLY Bonded to N, O, or F
> H on carbon cannot H-bond. CH₃F: F is present but H is on C → **no H-bonding**. NH₄⁺: N is present but has no lone pair (4 bonds used) → **cannot be H-bond acceptor**.

> [!warning] If H-Bonding Is Possible, It's the Strongest IMF — Don't Stop at Dipole-Dipole
> Ethanol (C₂H₅OH) H-bonds — do NOT say its strongest IMF is dipole-dipole. If O–H or N–H is present, H-bonding wins.

> [!warning] Molecular vs. Covalent Network Solids
> Molecular solids: LOW melting point (IMFs broken). Covalent network solids: VERY HIGH melting point (covalent bonds broken throughout lattice). Never confuse them.

> [!check] Melting Breaks Inter-Particle Forces ONLY
> Intramolecular bonds (covalent bonds within a molecule) are NOT broken during melting or boiling. Only IMFs between particles break.

> [!danger] Ideal Gas Law: ALWAYS Use Kelvin
> T(K) = T(°C) + 273. Using Celsius in PV = nRT is the single most common gas law error. R = 0.08206 L·atm/mol·K.

> [!warning] Same T = Same Average KE (NOT Same Speed)
> At the same temperature, all gases have the same average KE ($KE_{avg} = \frac{3}{2}RT$). Lighter gas moves **faster** but does NOT have more KE.

> [!warning] Maxwell-Boltzmann: Higher T → Peak Shifts RIGHT
> Higher T → peak moves **RIGHT** (faster speeds), gets **LOWER and BROADER**. Left-shifted or taller peak = LOWER temperature. Memory: **"Faster = Right."**

> [!check] Ion Hydration: O End Points Toward Cation, H End Toward Anion
> O end (δ⁻) pointing toward ion → **positive ion**. H end (δ⁺) pointing toward ion → **negative ion**. Always ion-dipole, never hydrogen bonding.

> [!warning] Real Gas Deviations
> Low T: IMF dominant → P < ideal. High P: particle volume dominant → V > ideal. Strongest IMF → most deviation at low T. Largest molecule → most deviation at high P. Ideal behavior: high T and low P.

> [!check] Distillation: Lowest BP Compound Appears First
> The component with the lowest boiling point (weakest IMF) vaporizes first and appears first in the distillate.

> [!warning] Beer's Law Error Direction
> Anything reducing transmitted light (smudge, frosted wall, longer path) → A too **HIGH**. Anything diluting sample (water droplets, wrong rinse) → A too **LOW**.

> [!check] Chromatography: Higher R_f = More Affinity for Mobile Phase
> $R_f$ = distance traveled by component / distance traveled by solvent front. Higher $R_f$ → stronger attraction to mobile phase → weaker attraction to stationary phase.

> [!warning] Charge-to-Size Ratio for Ion-Dipole Strength
> Compare charge first ($2+ > 1+$). Use radius as a tiebreaker within the same charge. Example: Mg²⁺ > Ca²⁺ > Li⁺ > Na⁺ for ion-dipole strength with water.

> [!check] EM Spectrum: Match Region to Molecular Event
> UV/Vis → electronic transitions. IR → bond vibrations (stretching/bending). Microwave → molecular rotation. Energy increases: microwave < IR < visible < UV.
