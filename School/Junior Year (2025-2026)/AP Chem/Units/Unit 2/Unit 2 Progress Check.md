## Q1. Bonding in $\text{Mg(NO}_3)_2(s)$

$\text{Mg(NO}_3)_2$ = magnesium nitrate. Mg is a metal; $\text{NO}_3^-$ is a polyatomic nonmetal anion. The compound-level bond is formed by electron transfer from Mg to $\text{NO}_3^-$ → ionic. The N–O bonds *within* the nitrate ion are covalent, but the question asks about bonding in the compound overall.

**Answer: D — There is ionic bonding between $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ions and $\text{NO}_3^-$ ions.**

The large $\Delta\text{EN}$ between the metal Mg and the nonmetal-based polyatomic anion drives full electron transfer, producing ions held by electrostatic attraction. The question targets the bond between formula units, not the internal N–O covalent bonds inside nitrate.

> **Trap:** (B) — Students see Mg and O in the formula and assume a direct polar covalent Mg–O bond. Mg fully transfers its electrons to become $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ and never forms a covalent bond; the ionic interaction is between $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ and the intact $\text{NO}_3^-$ unit.

## Q2. Valid claim about ionic binary compounds

Ionic bonding = large $\Delta\text{EN}$ → electron transfer, not equal sharing, not both metals.

**Answer: D — The electronegativity difference between the elements is relatively large.**

A large $\Delta\text{EN}$ (generally $> 1.7$) drives complete electron transfer rather than sharing, producing the ions whose electrostatic attraction defines ionic bonding.

> **Trap:** (A) — Two metals form metallic bonds, not ionic bonds. Ionic bonding requires a metal + nonmetal pairing.

## Q3. Binary compound with identical electronegativities

$\Delta\text{EN} = 0$ → no unequal sharing, no electron transfer → pure nonpolar covalent bond. No ionic character, no lattice, no high boiling point.

**Answer: B — The bonding in the compound is nonpolar covalent.**

Equal electronegativities mean electrons are shared equally with no partial charges, no ions, and no lattice energy.

> **Trap:** (D) — Ionic bonds require a *large* $\Delta\text{EN}$. Zero $\Delta\text{EN}$ is the exact opposite condition for ionic bonding.

## Q4. Does molten NaCl conduct electricity?

Diagram shows $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions freely dispersed — crystal lattice has broken down, ions are mobile.

**Answer: B — It conducts electricity because ions are free to move.**

When NaCl melts, the lattice breaks down and ions become mobile. Mobile charged particles carry current. The mechanism is ion flow, not electron flow.

> **Trap:** (A) — Na is a metal in elemental form, but in molten NaCl it exists as $\text{Na}^+$ with no free electrons. Conductivity in an ionic melt is ionic, not metallic.

## Q5. Particulate diagram: layers shifting + like-charge repulsion

Diagram shows ionic layers displaced by force until like charges align → repulsion → crystal fractures.

**Answer: B — Brittleness**

When an external force displaces one ionic layer relative to another, ions of the same sign become adjacent. The resulting electrostatic repulsion fractures the crystal along that plane. This is the atomic-level explanation for brittleness in ionic solids.

> **Trap:** (D) Conductivity — that requires mobile charge carriers, not layer geometry. The diagram shows mechanical fracture, not charge transport.

## Q6. Structure of solid KI maximizing Coulombic attractions

$\text{K}^+$ and $\text{I}^-$ form an ionic solid. Maximum stability requires opposite charges to alternate throughout the lattice.

**Answer: A** — alternating $+/-$ checkerboard pattern.

Every cation is surrounded only by anions and vice versa, maximizing Coulombic attractions (opposite charges adjacent) and minimizing repulsions (like charges are never adjacent).

> **Trap:** (B)/(C) — Both show same charges grouped in rows. That arrangement maximizes repulsion and is unstable; ions of the same sign would repel each other strongly.

## Q7. Particulate diagram: positive ions + mobile electrons — does it conduct?

Diagram shows large positive ions in a lattice with small electrons freely scattered around them = classic metallic bonding model.

**Answer: B — It conducts electricity because electrons are free to move through the substance.**

The delocalized "sea" of electrons is mobile throughout the metallic lattice. When a potential difference is applied, electrons flow and carry charge. This is metallic conductivity driven by mobile electrons, not ions.

> **Trap:** (A) — "Made of positive and negative particles" is also true of ionic solids, which do NOT conduct as solids. The key distinction is whether charge carriers are *free to move* — here it is the electrons that are mobile, not the positive ions.

## Q8. Which particulate diagram represents an interstitial alloy?

Interstitial alloy = smaller atoms occupy the *gaps* between larger atoms without replacing them. Must show two visibly different atom sizes with small atoms nestled in interstitial spaces.

**Answer: A** — large atoms arranged in a regular lattice with clearly smaller atoms in the gaps between them.

In an interstitial alloy (like steel = Fe + C), small atoms fill the spaces between large metal atoms, making the lattice more rigid and the material harder and less malleable.

> **Trap:** (B) — Shows two different-sized atoms appearing to substitute for each other in lattice positions (substitutional pattern), not filling gaps between host atoms.

## Q9. Diagram: positive ion layers sliding with electron sea present

Arrows show two layers of $+$ ions sliding relative to each other while remaining in contact with the surrounding electron sea.

**Answer: B — Malleability, because it shows how adjacent layers of positive ions can move relative to one another while remaining in full contact with the electron sea.**

The electron sea has no directional bonds, so layers of metal cations can slide without disrupting the metallic bonding. No fracture occurs because the sea continuously surrounds cations regardless of their relative positions. This is the atomic-level explanation of malleability and ductility.

> **Trap:** (A) Electrical conductivity — that requires electron *flow* (current), not layer sliding. The arrows here show mechanical deformation, not charge transport. Conductivity and malleability both involve the electron sea but in different ways.

## Q10. Which Lewis diagram correctly represents $\text{CH}_2\text{O}$ (formaldehyde)?

$\text{CH}_2\text{O}$: C is central, 12 total valence electrons. C needs 4 bonds.

**Diagram 2** (C=O double bond + 2 C–H bonds): check formal charges:
$$\text{FC}(\text{C}) = 4 - 0 - \frac{8}{2} = 0$$
$$\text{FC}(\text{O}) = 6 - 4 - \frac{4}{2} = 0$$
$$\text{FC}(\text{H}) = 1 - 0 - \frac{2}{2} = 0 \checkmark$$

All formal charges = 0 → best Lewis structure.

**Diagram 1** (C single bond to O, C only has 3 bonds): $\text{FC}(\text{C}) = 4 - 0 - 3 = +1$ — not minimized.

**Answer: D — Diagram 2, because all atoms have a formal charge of 0.**

The structure that minimizes formal charges (all = 0) is preferred. Diagram 2's C=O double bond satisfies C's need for 4 bonds and gives all atoms FC = 0.

> **Trap:** (B) — Claims Diagram 1 also has all FC = 0, but this is false. Diagram 1's carbon has only 3 bonds and 0 lone pairs: $\text{FC}(\text{C}) = 4 - 0 - 3 = +1$. Always calculate FC rather than trusting a distractor's claim.

## Q11. Best Lewis structure for $\text{H}_3\text{NO}$ based on formal charges

**Answered B (wrong) — Correct: A**

**What was answered — B:** $\text{H}_3\text{N} \text{–O}:$ (N central with 3 N–H bonds and 1 N–O single bond; O terminal with 3 lone pairs).

**Why B is wrong (AP explanation):**
$$\text{FC}(\text{N}) = 5 - 0 - \tfrac{1}{2}(8) = +1 \quad \text{(4 bonds, 0 lone pairs)}$$
$$\text{FC}(\text{O}) = 6 - 6 - \tfrac{1}{2}(2) = -1 \quad \text{(3 lone pairs, 1 bond)}$$
Formal charges of $+1$ and $-1$ are present — not the best structure. The AP explanation states: "In this diagram, there are formal charges of $+1$ on the N atom and $-1$ on the O atom. The best Lewis diagram for $\text{H}_3\text{NO}$ has formal charges of zero for all atoms."

**Correct answer: A — $:\!\text{N}(\text{H})(\text{H})\text{–O–H}$ (N with 1 lone pair, 2 N–H bonds, 1 N–O bond; O with 2 lone pairs, 1 O–H bond).**

Total valence electrons: $5 + 6 + 3(1) = 14$. Connectivity: N central (least electronegative non-H atom), bonded to 2 H's and 1 O; O bonded to N and 1 H. Formal charge check:

$$\text{FC}(\text{N}) = 5 - 2 - \tfrac{1}{2}(6) = 0 \quad \text{(1 lone pair, 3 bonds)}$$
$$\text{FC}(\text{O}) = 6 - 4 - \tfrac{1}{2}(4) = 0 \quad \text{(2 lone pairs, 2 bonds)}$$
$$\text{FC}(\text{H}) = 1 - 0 - \tfrac{1}{2}(2) = 0$$

All formal charges $= 0$ → this is the best Lewis structure.

> **Mistake pattern:** Assuming N must have 4 bonds (because it's central) without checking whether giving O a bonding hydrogen achieves all-zero formal charges. When evaluating Lewis structures, always compute formal charges for every atom in every candidate structure — "N is central" does not automatically mean all 3 H's bond to N.

## Q12. Best Lewis structure for $\text{CO}_2$: Diagram 1 (O=C=O) vs. Diagram 2 (:O–C≡O:)

**Diagram 1** (two C=O double bonds):
$$\text{FC}(\text{C}) = 4 - 0 - 4 = 0$$
$$\text{FC}(\text{O}) = 6 - 4 - 2 = 0 \checkmark$$

**Diagram 2** (one C–O single + one C≡O triple):
$$\text{FC}(\text{C}) = 4 - 0 - 4 = 0$$
$$\text{FC}(\text{O, single bond}) = 6 - 6 - 1 = -1$$
$$\text{FC}(\text{O, triple bond}) = 6 - 2 - 3 = +1$$

Diagram 2 has $+1$ on an oxygen — a highly electronegative atom — which is penalized twice: nonzero FC and wrong sign.

**Answer: A — Diagram 1, because all the atoms have a formal charge of 0.**

The criterion for the best Lewis structure is minimized formal charges, not bond strength. Diagram 1 gives all FC = 0; Diagram 2 places a positive FC on the more electronegative oxygen.

> **Trap:** (D) — "Triple bonds are stronger than double bonds" is true as a fact about bond energy, but bond strength does not determine which Lewis structure is *best*. Formal charge minimization is the selection rule.

## Q13. Which Lewis diagram represents a polar molecule?

- (A) $\text{BF}_3$: 3 bonding pairs, 0 LP on B → trigonal planar → symmetric → dipoles cancel → **nonpolar**
- (B) $\text{CO}_2$: 2 double bonds → linear → symmetric → dipoles cancel → **nonpolar**
- (C) $\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2$: 4 electron groups, tetrahedral, but 2 Cl + 2 H → **asymmetric** → dipoles do NOT cancel → **polar** ✓
- (D) $\text{N}_2$: homonuclear diatomic, $\Delta\text{EN} = 0$ → **nonpolar**

**Answer: C — $\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2$**

$\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2$ has polar C–Cl and C–H bonds arranged asymmetrically around the central C atom (Cl is much more electronegative than H), so the bond dipoles do not cancel and the molecule has a net dipole moment.

> **Trap:** (A) $\text{BF}_3$ — has very polar B–F bonds, but trigonal planar geometry is perfectly symmetric, causing all three bond dipoles to cancel. Polar bonds are necessary but not sufficient for a polar molecule — geometry must also be asymmetric.

## Q14. Comparing C=C vs. C–C bond strength in $\text{H}_2\text{C=CH–CH}_3$

Left bond: C=C double bond → bond order 2. Right bond: C–C single bond → bond order 1.

**Answer: A — The carbon-to-carbon bond on the left is stronger because it is a double bond.**

A double bond (bond order 2) has greater electron density between the nuclei than a single bond (bond order 1). Greater electron density means stronger electrostatic attraction between the shared electrons and both nuclei → higher bond dissociation energy and shorter bond length.

> **Trap:** (C) — "Both are bonds between carbon atoms" ignores bond order entirely. The type of bond (single vs. double vs. triple) is the dominant factor in bond strength, not the identity of the bonded atoms alone.

## Q15. H–N–H bond angle in $\text{NH}_3$

$\text{NH}_3$: N has 3 bonding pairs + 1 lone pair = 4 electron groups → tetrahedral electron geometry → trigonal pyramidal molecular geometry. Ideal tetrahedral angle = 109.5°. Lone pair compresses actual angle to ~107°. Closest available choice: 109.5°.

**Answer: C — 109.5°**

$\text{NH}_3$ has 4 electron groups around N (sp³ hybridized), giving a tetrahedral electron geometry with an ideal bond angle of 109.5°. The lone pair compresses the actual H–N–H angle slightly below this, but 109.5° is the reference geometry and the closest answer.

> **Trap:** (D) 120° — trigonal planar geometry (3 electron groups, sp²). $\text{NH}_3$ has 4 electron groups, not 3. Count *all* electron groups including lone pairs when determining geometry.

## Summary of Key Patterns — Unit 2 MCQ

| Pattern | Rule | Questions |
|---|---|---|
| Ionic vs. covalent in polyatomic ion compounds | Metal↔polyatomic ion bond is ionic; internal bonds within the polyatomic ion are covalent | Q1 |
| $\Delta\text{EN}$ = 0 → nonpolar covalent | Equal electronegativities mean equal sharing; no partial charges, no ionic character | Q3 |
| Ionic solid conductivity | Conducts only when melted or dissolved; solid state ions are fixed | Q4 |
| Brittleness explained by layer shift | Like charges align after displacement → repulsion → fracture | Q5 |
| Ionic lattice structure | Opposite charges must alternate to maximize attraction and minimize repulsion | Q6 |
| Metallic vs. ionic conductivity (solid) | Metallic = mobile electrons conduct; ionic solid = no mobile carriers → no conduction | Q7 |
| Interstitial alloy particulate diagram | Small atoms in gaps between large atoms; substitutional = same-size atom replacement | Q8 |
| Malleability diagram: layers sliding in electron sea | No directional bonds → layers slide freely → malleability, not conductivity | Q9 |
| Best Lewis structure = minimize formal charge | All FC = 0 is ideal; negative FC must be on more electronegative atom | Q10, Q11, Q12 |
| Formal charge calculation must be verified | Do not trust distractor text; calculate FC directly using $\text{FC} = V - LP - \frac{1}{2}B$ | Q10 |
| Polar molecule: polar bonds AND asymmetric geometry both required | $\text{BF}_3$, $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{CCl}_4$: polar bonds but symmetric → nonpolar | Q13 |
| Bond order determines strength, not atom identity | Double > single regardless of which atoms; C=C > C–C | Q14 |
| VSEPR: count all electron groups including lone pairs | 4 groups → tetrahedral base (109.5°); LP compresses but doesn't change the reference geometry | Q15 |
