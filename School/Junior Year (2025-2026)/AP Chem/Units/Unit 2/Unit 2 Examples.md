## Topic 2.1: Types of Chemical Bonds

**Example 1 — Classify the bond:**

| Bond | $\Delta\text{EN}$ | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Na–Cl | $3.16 - 0.93 = 2.23$ | Ionic |
| H–Cl | $3.16 - 2.20 = 0.96$ | Polar covalent |
| H–H | $2.20 - 2.20 = 0$ | Nonpolar covalent |
| C–H | $2.55 - 2.20 = 0.35$ | Nonpolar covalent (just below 0.4) |
| O–H | $3.44 - 2.20 = 1.24$ | Polar covalent |

**Example 2 — AP MCQ style:**
Which bond is most polar?
(A) C–H (B) N–H (C) O–H (D) F–H

F has the highest electronegativity (3.98) and H has 2.20 → $\Delta\text{EN} = 1.78$ → **(D) F–H is most polar.**

**Example 3 — Dual bonding in a polyatomic ion compound (Progress Check Q1):**

Identify all types of bonding present in $\text{Mg(NO}_3)_2$.

- Between $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ and $\text{NO}_3^-$: Mg is a metal, $\text{NO}_3^-$ is a nonmetal-based polyatomic anion. Large $\Delta\text{EN}$ → complete electron transfer → **ionic bond**.
- Within $\text{NO}_3^-$: N and O are both nonmetals with moderate $\Delta\text{EN}$ → electron sharing → **polar covalent bonds**.

One compound, two bond types. MCQ will ask about the compound-level bond (ionic), not the internal ion bonds.

*Key move: Identify the formula unit boundaries first. The metal↔polyatomic anion bond is always ionic; look inside the polyatomic ion for covalent bonds.*

## Topic 2.2: Intramolecular Force and Potential Energy

![[bond_order_strength_u2.svg|658]]
*Bond order determines both strength and length: triple > double > single in strength; triple < double < single in length.*

**Example 1 — Bond order and length:**
Rank C–C, C=C, and C≡C by increasing bond length:

Triple < Double < Single: **C≡C < C=C < C–C**

Bond energies in the same order: C≡C (839 kJ/mol) > C=C (614 kJ/mol) > C–C (347 kJ/mol).

**Example 2 — PE curve interpretation:**
On a potential energy vs. internuclear distance graph:
- The minimum of the curve = equilibrium bond length = bond dissociation energy.
- Left of minimum: repulsion dominates (PE rises steeply).
- Right of minimum: atoms are being pulled apart (PE rises gradually toward zero = dissociated atoms).

**Example 3 — Bond order determines strength, not atom identity (Progress Check Q14):**

The molecule $\text{H}_2\text{C=CH–CH}_3$ has two C–C bonds. Compare their strengths.

- Left C–C bond: double bond, bond order = 2
- Right C–C bond: single bond, bond order = 1

Bond order 2 > bond order 1 → left bond is stronger. Bond dissociation energy is higher and bond length is shorter for the double bond.

Common wrong answer: "Both are between carbon atoms so they're equal." This ignores bond order entirely — the same atom pair can form bonds of vastly different strengths depending on order.

*Key move: Identify the bond order first. Higher order = stronger bond, always. Atom identity is secondary.*

## Topic 2.3: Structure of Ionic Solids

![[ionic_lattice_structure_u2.svg|662]]
*Alternating charges (left) = stable; grouped like charges (right) = unstable. Every ion must be surrounded by oppositely charged neighbors.*

![[ionic_brittleness_u2.svg|656]]
*Force → layer slides → like charges align → repulsion → fracture. This is the atomic-level mechanism of brittleness.*

**Example 1 — Lattice energy ranking:**
Rank by increasing lattice energy magnitude: KCl, MgO, CaO.

- KCl: +1/−1 charges, large ions. Lattice energy ≈ −701 kJ/mol.
- CaO: +2/−2 charges, smaller than K/Cl. Lattice energy ≈ −3414 kJ/mol.
- MgO: +2/−2 charges, smallest ions. Lattice energy ≈ −3795 kJ/mol.

Order (least to most): **KCl < CaO < MgO**

**Example 2 — Conductivity:**
- NaCl(s): does NOT conduct — ions are fixed in lattice.
- NaCl(aq): DOES conduct — $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions are mobile.
- NaCl(l): DOES conduct — ions are mobile in the melt.

**Example 3 — Identifying a stable ionic lattice from a particulate diagram (Progress Check Q6):**

Which arrangement of $\text{K}^+$ and $\text{I}^-$ ions represents a stable solid?
- Arrangement A: $+/-/+$ top row, $-/+/-$ bottom row (alternating checkerboard)
- Arrangement B: $+/+/+$ top row, $-/-/-$ bottom row (grouped by charge)

Arrangement A is correct. In a stable ionic lattice, every cation must be surrounded only by anions and vice versa. This maximizes Coulombic attractions ($+$ attracts $-$) and minimizes repulsions ($+$ repels $+$). Arrangement B places like charges adjacent → destabilizing.

*Key move: Check that no two ions of the same sign are adjacent. Any touching like-charge pair = wrong answer.*

**Example 4 — Brittleness from particulate diagram (Progress Check Q5):**

A diagram shows ionic layers in a regular arrangement, then a force applied, then a shifted arrangement where $+$ ions are now adjacent to $+$ ions with the label "Layers Repel."

This illustrates brittleness. When one layer slides relative to another, like charges align. The resulting repulsion fractures the crystal rather than allowing it to deform. Contrast with metals, where layer sliding preserves the electron sea and causes no fracture.

*Key move: Brittleness = layer shift → like-charge alignment → repulsion → fracture. The diagram is showing a mechanical property, not conductivity.*

## Topic 2.4: Structure of Metals and Alloys

![[metallic_bonding_properties_u2.svg|691]]
*Malleability = layer sliding (cations move, sea adjusts — no fracture). Conductivity = electron flow (cations fixed, electrons carry charge). Not the same phenomenon.*

![[alloy_types_u2.svg|692]]
*Interstitial (steel): small atoms in gaps → rigid, hard, less malleable. Substitutional (brass): same-size atoms swap in → retains ductility.*

**Example 1 — Substitutional vs. interstitial:**
- Brass (Cu + Zn): Zn atoms (radius 134 pm) replace Cu atoms (radius 128 pm) — similar size → **substitutional alloy**. Still malleable and conductive.
- Steel (Fe + C): C atoms (radius 77 pm) are much smaller than Fe (radius 126 pm) → fit in gaps → **interstitial alloy**. Harder and less malleable than pure iron.

**Example 2 — AP MCQ style:**
Why is a sample of copper metal a good conductor, but a sample of $\text{CuSO}_4$(s) is not?

In copper metal, valence electrons are delocalized and mobile throughout the metallic lattice. In solid $\text{CuSO}_4$, all electrons are localized in ionic/covalent bonds and there are no mobile charge carriers.

**Example 3 — Malleability from particulate diagram (Progress Check Q9):**

A diagram shows a lattice of positive ions labeled "Electron Sea" with two large arrows indicating adjacent layers sliding horizontally past each other.

This illustrates malleability. Because metallic bonding has no directional bonds, layers of positive ions can slide relative to one another while remaining continuously surrounded by the delocalized electron sea. No bonds are broken during the sliding — the sea adjusts. This is why metals bend and deform rather than fracture under stress.

This is NOT illustrating conductivity. Conductivity = electron flow (current). Malleability = layer sliding (deformation). Both involve the electron sea, but the phenomena are distinct.

*Key move: Arrows showing layers moving = malleability. Electrons flowing in one direction = conductivity. Distinguish by what is moving.*

**Example 4 — Identifying interstitial alloy from particulate diagram (Progress Check Q8):**

Four diagrams show different arrangements of atoms. Which is an interstitial alloy?

Look for: a regular lattice of large atoms with noticeably smaller atoms occupying the spaces *between* the large atoms (not replacing them). The large atoms maintain their lattice positions; small atoms are tucked into gaps.

Eliminate:
- Diagrams showing two atom sizes where smaller atoms occupy lattice positions = substitutional
- Diagrams showing only one size = pure metal
- Diagrams showing $+$ and $-$ labeled ions = ionic compound, not an alloy

*Key move: Interstitial = small in the gaps, large atoms keep their positions. The two atom sizes must be clearly different, and small atoms must be nestled between large ones.*

## Topic 2.5: Lewis Diagrams

**Example 1 — $\text{SO}_2$ (18 total valence electrons: S has 6 + 2×O has 6 = 18):**

Skeleton: O–S–O (S is central; O is never central). Place 3 lone pairs on each O = 12 electrons used. Remaining: $18 - 12 = 6$ → 3 lone pairs on S. But this gives S only 2 single bonds (4 electrons from bonds + 6 lone pair = only 2-bond structure with no octets satisfied on S). Convert one lone pair on a terminal O to a double bond → one S=O double bond, one S–O single bond, 1 lone pair on S.

Now verify with formal charges to confirm this double-bond structure is the best:

**Structure A — two S–O single bonds, 3 lone pairs on S:**
S: $\text{FC} = 6 - 6 - \tfrac{1}{2}(4) = 6 - 6 - 2 = -2$
Each O (3 lone pairs, 1 bond): $\text{FC} = 6 - 6 - \tfrac{1}{2}(2) = -1$
Net charge: $-2 + (-1) + (-1) = -4 \neq 0$ ✗ (too many lone pairs; violates electron count)

**Structure B — one S=O double bond, one S–O single bond, 1 lone pair on S:**
S (1 lone pair, 1 double bond, 1 single bond — 6 bonding electrons + 2 lone pair):
$$\text{FC(S)} = 6 - 2 - \tfrac{1}{2}(6) = 6 - 2 - 3 = +1$$
Doubly bonded O (2 lone pairs, 1 double bond):
$$\text{FC(O=)} = 6 - 4 - \tfrac{1}{2}(4) = 6 - 4 - 2 = 0$$
Singly bonded O (3 lone pairs, 1 single bond):
$$\text{FC(O–)} = 6 - 6 - \tfrac{1}{2}(2) = 6 - 6 - 1 = -1$$
Net charge: $+1 + 0 + (-1) = 0$ ✓

**Structure C — two S=O double bonds, 0 lone pairs on S:**
S (0 lone pairs, 2 double bonds — 8 bonding electrons):
$$\text{FC(S)} = 6 - 0 - \tfrac{1}{2}(8) = 6 - 0 - 4 = +2$$
Each doubly bonded O (2 lone pairs):
$$\text{FC(O)} = 6 - 4 - \tfrac{1}{2}(4) = 0$$
Net charge: $+2 + 0 + 0 = +2 \neq 0$ ✗

**Comparison:** Structure B has the smallest formal charge magnitudes (+1 and −1, net 0) among valid structures. The negative FC is on O, which is more electronegative than S — correct placement. **Structure B is the best Lewis structure.** Two equivalent resonance structures exist (the double bond can be on either O), so the actual S–O bond length is intermediate (bond order = 1.5).

*Key move: Always calculate formal charges on all atoms in each candidate structure. The double-bond structure wins here not because "double bonds are better" but because it minimizes formal charge magnitudes.*

**Example 2 — $\text{NH}_3$ (8 total valence electrons: N has 5 + 3×H has 1 = 8):**

Skeleton: H–N–H with third H. Place 3 N–H bonds (6 electrons used). Remaining 2 electrons → lone pair on N. Lewis structure: N with 3 bonds and 1 lone pair. Formal charges: all zero ✓.

**Example 3 — $\text{NO}_3^-$ (24 valence electrons including the extra from charge):**

$\text{N}(5) + 3\times\text{O}(6) + 1\text{(charge)} = 24$ electrons.

Best structure: One N=O double bond, two N–O single bonds. Formal charges: N = +1 (has 4 bonds, 0 lone pairs: $5 - 0 - 4 = +1$), doubly bonded O = 0, singly bonded O = −1 each. Resonance distributes the negative formal charge over all three oxygens — this is why all three N–O bonds are equivalent.

**Example 4 — $\text{PCl}_5$ (expanded octet: P in period 3):**

$\text{P}(5) + 5\times\text{Cl}(7) = 40$ electrons. P forms 5 bonds = 10 electrons around P → expanded octet allowed for period 3.

**Example 5 — Formal Charge for $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$: comparing two candidate structures (Krug 2.6, Farabaugh):**

Total valence electrons: S(6) + 4×O(6) + 2(charge) = **32**.

**Structure A — 4 S=O double bonds:**
S forms 4 double bonds = 8 bonding pairs; no lone pairs on S.
$$\text{FC}(\text{S}) = 6 - 0 - \tfrac{1}{2}(16) = 6 - 0 - 8 = -2$$
Each O in S=O: 2 lone pairs (4 electrons) + 1 bond pair shared.
$$\text{FC}(\text{O}) = 6 - 4 - \tfrac{1}{2}(4) = 6 - 4 - 2 = 0$$
Net charge check: $-2 + 4(0) = -2$ ✓. But FC(S) = −2 is a large magnitude formal charge.

**Structure B — 2 S=O double bonds + 2 S–O single bonds:**
S has 2 double bonds + 2 single bonds = 6 bonding pairs; no lone pairs on S.
$$\text{FC}(\text{S}) = 6 - 0 - \tfrac{1}{2}(12) = 6 - 6 = 0 \checkmark$$
Doubly bonded O (2 lone pairs): $\text{FC} = 6 - 4 - 2 = 0$
Singly bonded O (3 lone pairs): $\text{FC} = 6 - 6 - \tfrac{1}{2}(2) = 6 - 6 - 1 = -1$
Net charge: $0 + 2(0) + 2(-1) = -2$ ✓

**Comparison:** Structure B has FC of 0 on S and −1 on two oxygens — smaller magnitude than Structure A's FC of −2 on S. **Structure B is the better Lewis structure** because it minimizes formal charges.

Both structures are valid resonance contributors; they differ only in which two oxygens carry the negative FC. The actual $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ is a resonance hybrid with all four S–O bonds equivalent at intermediate length.

*Key move: When comparing Lewis structures for the same species, calculate FC on EVERY atom in each structure. The winner is the one with the smallest magnitudes — not the one with more double bonds.*

**Example 6 — Selecting best structure by formal charge when distractors make false FC claims (Progress Check Q10):**

Two Lewis diagrams for $\text{CH}_2\text{O}$ (formaldehyde):
- Diagram 1: C has single bond to O, double bond to... (only 3 bonds on C)
- Diagram 2: C has double bond to O ($\text{C=O}$) plus two C–H bonds (4 bonds total)

An answer choice claims "Diagram 1, because all atoms have FC = 0." Verify directly:

$$\text{Diagram 1: FC(C)} = 4 - 0 - 3 = +1 \quad \text{(NOT zero — claim is false)}$$

$$\text{Diagram 2: FC(C)} = 4 - 0 - 4 = 0 \checkmark$$
$$\text{Diagram 2: FC(O)} = 6 - 4 - 2 = 0 \checkmark$$
$$\text{Diagram 2: FC(H)} = 1 - 0 - 1 = 0 \checkmark$$

Diagram 2 is correct. Never accept a distractor's claim that FC = 0 — always calculate.

*Key move: When a question says "based on formal charges," calculate every atom in every candidate structure. Distractors routinely lie about FC values.*

**Example 7 — Best structure for $\text{H}_3\text{NO}$ (Progress Check Q11):**

Total valence electrons: $5 + 6 + 3(1) = 14$.

Central atom: N (less electronegative than O; H is never central).

Structure: 3 N–H bonds + 1 N–O single bond + 2 lone pairs on O.

$$\text{FC(N)} = 5 - 0 - 4 = +1$$
$$\text{FC(O)} = 6 - 4 - 1 = -1$$

FC is minimized (+1 and −1, net = 0), and the negative FC is on O (more electronegative than N). This is the best structure.

Wrong answer trap: placing O as the central atom increases formal charges and violates the central-atom rule (least electronegative non-H atom is central).

*Key move: (1) Identify central atom by electronegativity — N before O. (2) Calculate FC. (3) Confirm negative FC is on the more electronegative atom.*

## Topic 2.6: Resonance and Formal Charge

**Example 1 — Ozone ($\text{O}_3$):**

Total valence electrons: $3\times6 = 18$. Central O is between two terminal O atoms.

Structure 1: O=O–O (double on left, single on right, lone pairs).
Structure 2: O–O=O (single on left, double on right, lone pairs).

Actual $\text{O}_3$ is a hybrid → both O–O bonds are equivalent (bond length intermediate between single and double ≈ bond order 1.5). Molecule is bent.

**Example 2 — Benzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_6$):**

Two resonance structures with alternating single and double C–C bonds. The hybrid has all C–C bond lengths equal (139 pm — between single bond 154 pm and double bond 134 pm). All bonds have bond order 1.5. This delocalization gives benzene extra stability (resonance energy).

**Example 3 — Best resonance structure for $\text{CO}_2$ (Progress Check Q12):**

**Diagram 1:** O=C=O (two double bonds)
$$\text{FC(C)} = 4 - 0 - 4 = 0$$
$$\text{FC(O)} = 6 - 4 - 2 = 0 \checkmark \text{ All zero.}$$

**Diagram 2:** :O–C≡O: (one single + one triple bond)
$$\text{FC(C)} = 4 - 0 - 4 = 0$$
$$\text{FC(O, single)} = 6 - 6 - 1 = -1$$
$$\text{FC(O, triple)} = 6 - 2 - 3 = +1$$

Diagram 2 places a positive FC on oxygen — the most electronegative element in the structure — doubly penalized: nonzero FC and wrong sign on wrong atom. Diagram 1 wins.

Wrong answer trap: "Diagram 2 because triple bonds are stronger." Bond strength does not determine the best Lewis structure. Formal charge minimization does.

*Key move: The best-Lewis-structure criterion is always formal charge minimization, not bond energy. These are different concepts.*

**Example 3 — Nitrite ion ($\text{NO}_2^-$): drawing resonance structures and explaining bond length:**

Total valence electrons: $\text{N}(5) + 2\times\text{O}(6) + 1\text{(charge)} = 18$ electrons.

Skeleton: O–N–O (N is central; O is never central).

**Resonance Structure 1:** N=O on left, N–O on right, 1 lone pair on N.
$$\text{FC(N)} = 5 - 2 - \tfrac{1}{2}(6) = 5 - 2 - 3 = 0$$
$$\text{FC(O, double)} = 6 - 4 - \tfrac{1}{2}(4) = 0$$
$$\text{FC(O, single)} = 6 - 6 - \tfrac{1}{2}(2) = -1$$
Net charge: $0 + 0 + (-1) = -1$ ✓

**Resonance Structure 2:** N–O on left, N=O on right, 1 lone pair on N. (Mirror image of Structure 1.)
By symmetry: $\text{FC(N)} = 0$, $\text{FC(O, double)} = 0$, $\text{FC(O, single)} = -1$. Net: $-1$ ✓

**Are both structures equivalent contributors?** Yes. Both resonance structures have identical formal charge distributions (FC = 0 on N, 0 on one O, −1 on the other O). Neither structure is "better" — they contribute equally to the resonance hybrid.

**Actual $\text{NO}_2^-$ structure (resonance hybrid):**
- Both N–O bonds are equivalent in length: intermediate between a single bond (N–O ≈ 140 pm) and a double bond (N=O ≈ 120 pm).
- Bond order = $\frac{1 + 2}{2} = 1.5$ (average of one single and one double bond across two equivalent structures).
- The negative charge is delocalized equally over both oxygen atoms — neither oxygen permanently bears the −1 formal charge.

*Key move: When two resonance structures are equivalent (identical formal charge patterns), both contribute equally. State that the actual bond length is intermediate and calculate the bond order as the average bond order across all resonance structures.*

## Topic 2.7: VSEPR and Hybridization

![[molecular_polarity_u2.svg|697]]
*Symmetric geometry → dipoles cancel → nonpolar (BF₃, CO₂, CCl₄). Asymmetric geometry or mixed substituents → dipoles don't cancel → polar (CH₂Cl₂, H₂O, NH₃).*

**Example 1 — Determine geometry and hybridization:**

| Molecule | Bonding groups | Lone pairs on central | Electron geometry | Molecular geometry | Hybridization | Polar? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\text{BeCl}_2$ | 2 | 0 | Linear | Linear | sp | No |
| $\text{BF}_3$ | 3 | 0 | Trigonal planar | Trigonal planar | $\text{sp}^2$ | No (symmetric) |
| $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ | 2 | 2 | Tetrahedral | Bent | $\text{sp}^3$ | Yes |
| $\text{NH}_3$ | 3 | 1 | Tetrahedral | Trigonal pyramidal | $\text{sp}^3$ | Yes |
| $\text{CH}_4$ | 4 | 0 | Tetrahedral | Tetrahedral | $\text{sp}^3$ | No |
| $\text{SO}_2$ | 2 bonds + 1 LP | 1 | Trigonal planar | Bent | $\text{sp}^2$ | Yes |
| $\text{PCl}_5$ | 5 | 0 | Trigonal bipyramidal | Trigonal bipyramidal | (no AP hybridization) | No |

**Example 2 — Bond angle compression:**

Ideal tetrahedral = 109.5°.
- $\text{CH}_4$: 109.5° (no lone pairs).
- $\text{NH}_3$: ~107° (1 lone pair compresses).
- $\text{H}_2\text{O}$: ~104.5° (2 lone pairs compress further).

**Example 3 — Polarity (AP 2023 style):**
Is $\text{SF}_4$ polar?

$\text{SF}_4$: 4 bonding pairs + 1 lone pair = 5 electron groups → see-saw geometry. The 4 S–F bonds are all polar, and the see-saw shape is asymmetric → bond dipoles do NOT cancel → **$\text{SF}_4$ is polar.**

Contrast with $\text{SF}_6$: 6 bonding pairs, octahedral → all S–F bond dipoles cancel → nonpolar.

**Example 4 — $\sigma$ and $\pi$ bond count:**
How many $\sigma$ and $\pi$ bonds are in $\text{HC≡N}$?

H–C: 1 $\sigma$. C≡N: 1 $\sigma$ + 2 $\pi$. Total: **2 $\sigma$ and 2 $\pi$ bonds**.

**Example 5 — AP FRQ chain (Lewis → VSEPR → polarity → IMF):**
For $\text{CH}_3\text{OH}$ (methanol):
1. Lewis: C has 4 bonds (3 C–H, 1 C–O); O has 2 bonds (1 O–C, 1 O–H) and 2 lone pairs.
2. Geometry around C: tetrahedral ($\text{sp}^3$); geometry around O: bent ($\text{sp}^3$).
3. Polar bonds: C–O ($\Delta\text{EN} = 1.04$) and O–H ($\Delta\text{EN} = 1.24$).
4. Asymmetric geometry → net dipole → **polar molecule**.
5. IMF: London dispersion + dipole-dipole + **hydrogen bonding** (H bonded to O). High boiling point for its molar mass.

**Example 6 — Polarity of $\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2$ vs. $\text{CCl}_4$ (Progress Check Q13):**

Both molecules have a tetrahedral central carbon with 4 single bonds. Why is one polar and the other not?

$\text{CCl}_4$: all 4 substituents identical (all Cl, all same $\Delta\text{EN}$) → 4 bond dipoles point symmetrically outward and cancel completely → **nonpolar**.

$\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2$: 2 Cl substituents + 2 H substituents → different bond dipoles (C–Cl vs. C–H have very different $\Delta\text{EN}$) → the four dipoles do NOT cancel → **net dipole moment → polar**.

The lesson: geometry alone does not determine polarity. When different substituents are attached to a symmetric geometry, the dipoles no longer cancel.

*Key move: For polarity with tetrahedral geometry, check whether ALL substituents are identical. If yes → nonpolar. If mixed → likely polar.*

**Example 7 — VSEPR bond angle for $\text{NH}_3$ (Progress Check Q15):**

$\text{NH}_3$: count all electron groups on N.
- 3 bonding pairs (N–H)
- 1 lone pair
- Total: 4 electron groups → tetrahedral electron geometry

Reference bond angle for 4 electron groups = 109.5°. Lone pair compresses actual angle to ~107°, but the closest answer choice and the reference geometry is **109.5°**.

Do not confuse:
- 3 electron groups (no LP) → trigonal planar → 120°
- 4 electron groups (1 LP) → trigonal pyramidal → ~107° (reference: 109.5°)

$\text{NH}_3$ has 4 groups, not 3. Counting only the bonds (3) and ignoring the lone pair is the most common error on this question type.

*Key move: Always count lone pairs as electron groups for VSEPR. Use the reference geometry angle (109.5° for 4 groups) when choosing from answer options.*

**Example 8 — Full VSEPR analysis for $\text{ClF}_3$ (T-shaped geometry):**

**Step 1 — Count total valence electrons:**
$\text{Cl}(7) + 3\times\text{F}(7) = 28$ electrons.

**Step 2 — Draw the Lewis structure:**
Skeleton: F–Cl–F with one additional F. Place 3 lone pairs on each F = 18 electrons used. Remaining: $28 - 18 = 10$ electrons = 5 lone pairs. But 3 bonds to F already account for 6 bonding electrons, so $28 - 6\text{(bonds)} - 18\text{(F lone pairs)} = 4$ electrons = **2 lone pairs on Cl**.
Verify: 3 Cl–F bonds (6 e⁻) + 6 lone pairs on F (18 e⁻) + 2 lone pairs on Cl (4 e⁻) = 28 ✓

**Step 3 — Count electron groups on Cl:**
3 bonding pairs (Cl–F bonds) + 2 lone pairs = **5 electron groups total**.

**Step 4 — Determine electron geometry:**
5 electron groups → **trigonal bipyramidal** electron geometry (the five groups arrange themselves at the vertices of a trigonal bipyramid: 3 equatorial positions at 120° from each other in a plane, 2 axial positions at 90° above and below that plane).

**Step 5 — Place lone pairs in equatorial positions:**
In a trigonal bipyramidal arrangement, lone pairs prefer **equatorial positions** over axial positions. Why? Equatorial positions have two 90° neighbors (the two axial groups) plus two 120° neighbors (the other equatorial groups). Axial positions have three 90° neighbors (the three equatorial groups). Since lone pair–bonding pair repulsion at 90° is more severe than at 120°, placing lone pairs in equatorial positions (where they have only two 90° interactions) minimizes repulsion compared to axial placement (three 90° interactions). With 2 lone pairs occupying the 2 equatorial positions (along with 1 bonding F in the third equatorial spot), the remaining 2 F atoms go to the axial positions.

**Step 6 — Determine molecular geometry:**
Ignoring lone pairs, the 3 F atoms form a **T-shape**: 1 equatorial F flanked by 2 lone pairs in the equatorial plane, with 2 axial F atoms above and below. The molecular geometry is **T-shaped**.

**Step 7 — Predict bond angles:**
- Axial Cl–F bonds (above and below): ideally 90° from the equatorial plane. Compressed to **< 90°** by lone-pair–bonding-pair repulsion from the 2 equatorial lone pairs.
- Equatorial Cl–F bond: lies in the plane between the two equatorial lone pairs; the angle between this F and each axial F is **< 90°** (compressed from ideal 90°).

**Summary:**

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Total electron groups on Cl | 5 |
| Lone pairs on Cl | 2 |
| Electron geometry | Trigonal bipyramidal |
| Molecular geometry | T-shaped |
| Bond angles | <90° (compressed by equatorial lone pairs) |

*Key move: For 5-electron-group molecules with lone pairs, always place lone pairs in equatorial positions first (minimizes 90° lone-pair repulsions). The molecular geometry name describes only the positions of atoms — in ClF₃ this gives a T-shape, not trigonal bipyramidal.*
