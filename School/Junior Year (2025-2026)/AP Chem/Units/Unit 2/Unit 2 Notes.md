## Study Hub Quick Reference — Unit 2

> [!check] Q9 — Ionic Lattice: Alternating Charges Required
> A stable ionic lattice has **alternating cations and anions** — every cation surrounded only by anions and vice versa. This arrangement maximizes Coulombic attractions and minimizes repulsions. Diagrams showing like charges grouped together do **NOT** represent a stable ionic solid.

> [!check] Q42 — H–O–C Bond Angle (~105°)
> Oxygen in water has 4 electron groups (2 bonding + 2 lone pairs) → tetrahedral **electron** geometry → **bent** molecular geometry. Lone pairs compress bond angle below 109.5° to ~105°. Same logic: any sp³ oxygen with 2 lone pairs (like the O in an alcohol or water) has ~105° bond angles.

> [!check] Q43 — sp² Carbon is Trigonal Planar (120°)
> 3 electron groups around carbon → sp² hybridization → **trigonal planar** geometry → **120° bond angles**. No lone pairs on sp² carbon → ideal angles. Examples: C in CO₃²⁻, benzene ring, or any C=C double bond. The carbon and the three atoms bonded to it are all in the same plane.

> [!check] Q44 — Properties of Ionic Solids
> (1) **High melting point** — strong electrostatic forces between ions. (2) **Brittle** — like charges align when layers shift → electrostatic repulsion fractures crystal. (3) **Do NOT conduct as solid** — ions fixed in lattice. (4) **DO conduct when melted or dissolved** — ions become mobile. (5) Dissolve in polar solvents via ion-dipole forces.

> [!check] FRQ Q3A — Lewis Structure Procedure
> 1. Count total valence e⁻ (add 1 per negative charge; subtract 1 per positive charge)
> 2. Draw skeleton — least EN atom is central; **H and F are NEVER central**
> 3. Fill terminal atoms to octets first (3 lone pairs each if 1 bond present)
> 4. Give remaining e⁻ to central atom
> 5. If central atom is short, convert terminal lone pairs → double/triple bonds
> 6. Calculate formal charge on every atom: FC = V − LP − ½B; prefer structure where all FC = 0 or minimized

> [!check] FRQ Q3B — VSEPR: 3 Bonding Domains + 0 Lone Pairs = Trigonal Planar
> 3 bonding groups + 0 lone pairs on central atom → **trigonal planar** molecular geometry → 120° bond angles → sp² hybridization. If 3 bonding + 1 lone pair → bent (<120°). Always count ALL electron groups (bonding + lone pairs) first to get electron geometry, then determine molecular geometry.

> [!warning] Trap — Formal Charge Determines Best Lewis Structure, NOT Bond Strength
> The BEST Lewis structure has **minimized formal charges**, not the most double bonds. A structure with all FC = 0 beats a triple-bonded structure with FC = ±2. Always calculate FC = V − LP − ½B for every atom in every candidate.

> [!warning] Trap — Electron Geometry ≠ Molecular Geometry
> NH₃: 4 electron groups → tetrahedral **electron** geometry, but 3 bonding groups → **trigonal pyramidal molecular** geometry. Always identify which geometry the question asks for. "Geometry of NH₃" = trigonal pyramidal (molecular), not tetrahedral.

> [!danger] Critical — H and F Are Never Central Atoms
> H can only form 1 bond — **never central**. F is the most electronegative — **always terminal**. The least electronegative non-H atom is the central atom.

## Topic 2.1: Types of Chemical Bonds

**Essential Knowledge (Farabaugh CED):**
- All chemical bonds arise from Coulombic (electrostatic) attractions between charged particles.
- **Ionic bond:** Electron transfer from metal to nonmetal. Large electronegativity difference (generally > 1.7).
- **Covalent bond:** Electron sharing between nonmetals. Similar electronegativities.
- **Polar covalent:** Unequal sharing. Electronegativity difference between ~0.4 and ~1.7. Creates partial charges ($\delta+$ and $\delta-$).
- **Nonpolar covalent:** Equal or near-equal sharing. $\Delta\text{EN} < 0.4$. Examples: $\text{H}_2$, $\text{Cl}_2$, C–H bonds.
- **Metallic bond:** Delocalized "sea" of electrons surrounding positive metal cations.

**Electronegativity trend:** Increases across a period and up a group. F is highest (3.98), Cs/Fr is lowest.

**Bond Polarity:** Partial charge $\delta+$ is on the less electronegative atom; $\delta-$ is on the more electronegative atom.

**Bond Type and $\Delta\text{EN}$:**

| $\Delta\text{EN}$ Range | Bond Type |
|---|---|
| 0 | Nonpolar covalent |
| $< 0.4$ | Nonpolar covalent |
| $0.4 – 1.7$ | Polar covalent |
| $> 1.7$ | Ionic |

**Compounds containing polyatomic ions (e.g., $\text{Mg(NO}_3)_2$):**
A single compound can contain *both* ionic and covalent bonding. The bond between the metal ion and the polyatomic anion is ionic (electron transfer). The bonds *within* the polyatomic ion are covalent (electron sharing between nonmetals). On MCQs: the question "what type of bonding exists in [ionic compound with polyatomic ion]?" targets the ionic bond between formula units, not the internal covalent bonds.

## Topic 2.2: Intramolecular Force and Potential Energy

**Essential Knowledge (Farabaugh CED):**
- When atoms bond, potential energy decreases as they approach (attraction dominates) until they reach the equilibrium bond length, which is the energy minimum (bond dissociation energy).
- If atoms get too close, repulsion between nuclei causes PE to sharply increase.
- **Bond length** and **bond energy** (bond dissociation energy) are inversely related: shorter bonds are stronger.
- Bond order: single < double < triple. Bond strength and bond energy increase with bond order; bond length decreases with bond order.

**Bond Order vs. Bond Properties:**
![[bond_order_strength_u2.svg|697]]
*Single (weakest, longest) → double (medium) → triple (strongest, shortest). Bond order is the dominant factor; atom identity is secondary.*

| Bond Type | Bond Order | Length (relative) | Energy (relative) |
|---|---|---|---|
| C–C single | 1 | Longest | Weakest |
| C=C double | 2 | Medium | Medium |
| C≡C triple | 3 | Shortest | Strongest |

**Bond strength is determined by bond order, not atom identity alone.** A C=C double bond is always stronger than a C–C single bond regardless of what else is attached to the carbons. Bond order is the dominant factor when comparing bonds between the same pair of atom types.

**Coulomb's Law Connection:** Bond strength increases with higher charges on bonded atoms and shorter bond lengths, exactly as Coulomb's law predicts for intramolecular attractions.

## Topic 2.3: Structure of Ionic Solids

**Essential Knowledge (Farabaugh CED):**
- Ionic solids consist of a regular lattice of alternating cations and anions held together by electrostatic forces.
- **Lattice energy:** Energy released when gaseous ions form 1 mol of solid ionic compound. Always negative (exothermic). More negative = more stable lattice.
- Lattice energy magnitude increases with: (1) higher ionic charges, (2) smaller ionic radii (shorter distance).

**Properties of Ionic Solids:**
- High melting/boiling point (strong electrostatic forces).
- Brittle (like charges align and repel when layers shift).
- Conduct electricity only when melted or dissolved (ions must be mobile to carry charge).
- Do NOT conduct as a solid (ions are fixed in lattice).

**Brittleness — Atomic-Level Explanation:**
![[ionic_brittleness_u2.svg|697]]
*Three-stage sequence: stable lattice → force applied → layer shifts until like charges align → electrostatic repulsion fractures the crystal.*

When an external force displaces one ionic layer relative to an adjacent layer, ions of the same sign come into alignment. The resulting electrostatic repulsion between like charges causes the crystal to fracture along that plane. This is why ionic solids are brittle but metals (with a non-directional electron sea) are not.

**Ionic Lattice Structure:**
![[ionic_lattice_structure_u2.svg|697]]
*Stable ionic lattices require alternating charges — every cation surrounded only by anions and vice versa. Like-charge neighbors are always wrong.*

In a stable ionic lattice, opposite charges must alternate. Every cation must be surrounded by anions and vice versa. This arrangement maximizes Coulombic attractions and minimizes repulsions. Diagrams showing like charges grouped together do NOT represent a stable ionic solid.

## Topic 2.4: Structure of Metals and Alloys

**Essential Knowledge (Farabaugh CED):**
- Metallic bonding: positive metal cations surrounded by a delocalized "sea" of mobile valence electrons.
- Properties explained by metallic bonding:
  - **Electrical conductivity:** Mobile electrons carry charge.
  - **Thermal conductivity:** Mobile electrons transfer kinetic energy.
  - **Malleability/ductility:** Layers can slide without breaking the "sea" — no directional bond.
- **Alloys:** Mixtures of metals (or metals + nonmetals).
  - **Substitutional alloy:** Atoms of similar size replace each other (e.g., brass = Cu + Zn). Retains conductivity.
  - **Interstitial alloy:** Smaller atoms fit into gaps between larger metal atoms (e.g., steel = Fe + C). Carbon makes the lattice more rigid → harder, less malleable.

**Distinguishing metallic conductivity from ionic conductivity:**
In a metallic solid, mobile *electrons* carry charge — the positive ions stay fixed. In a molten or dissolved ionic compound, mobile *ions* carry charge — there are no free electrons. A solid ionic compound conducts by neither mechanism (ions fixed, no free electrons) → does not conduct.

**Malleability vs. Conductivity (MCQ trap):**
![[metallic_bonding_properties_u2.svg|697]]
*Left: layer sliding (malleability) — cations move, sea adjusts. Right: electron flow (conductivity) — cations fixed, electrons carry current. Different mechanisms, same electron sea.*

Both properties involve the electron sea, but they are mechanically different. Malleability = layers of positive ions *sliding* past each other while remaining surrounded by the sea. Conductivity = electrons *flowing* through the lattice in response to a potential difference. A particulate diagram showing layer sliding illustrates malleability, not conductivity.

**Identifying alloy type from a particulate diagram:**
![[alloy_types_u2.svg|697]]
*Interstitial (left, steel): small C atoms in gaps between large Fe atoms. Substitutional (right, brass): same-size Zn atoms replace Cu atoms in lattice positions.*

- Interstitial alloy: two clearly different atom sizes; smaller atoms occupy the *gaps between* larger atoms. The large atoms still form a regular lattice.
- Substitutional alloy: two atoms of similar size; some host-lattice positions are occupied by the guest atom instead.

## Topic 2.5: Lewis Diagrams

**Essential Knowledge (Farabaugh CED):**
- Lewis structures show all valence electrons as bonds (lines) or lone pairs (dots).
- The octet rule states most atoms (except H, which fills to 2) seek 8 valence electrons.
- **Exceptions to octet rule:**
  - H and He: duet (2 electrons).
  - Be: often 4 electrons (electron-deficient).
  - B: often 6 electrons (electron-deficient).
  - Elements in Period 3+ (P, S, Cl, etc.): can have expanded octets (10 or 12 electrons).

**Lewis Structure Procedure (Krug 2.5a, Farabaugh):**
1. **Count total valence electrons:** Sum valence electrons from every atom. Add 1 e⁻ per unit of negative charge; subtract 1 e⁻ per unit of positive charge.
2. **Draw the skeleton:** Least electronegative non-hydrogen atom is central. H is **never** central; F is **never** central.
3. **Distribute to terminal atoms first:** Place lone pairs on each terminal atom to complete its octet (6 electrons if it already has 1 bond = 3 lone pairs).
4. **Give remaining electrons to the central atom.**
5. **Check central atom octet:** If short, convert lone pairs from terminal atoms to double or triple bonds (one lone pair → one bond per conversion).
6. **Verify:** Total electrons drawn = total valence electrons counted in step 1. Total bonds + lone pairs must match exactly.

**Counting electrons for charged species (MCQ trap):**
- $\text{NO}_3^-$: N(5) + 3×O(6) + 1 (charge) = **24** total valence electrons.
- $\text{NH}_4^+$: N(5) + 4×H(1) − 1 (charge) = **8** total valence electrons.
- **Off-by-one errors on charges are the single most common Lewis structure mistake.** Always write out the full count.

**Exceptions to the Octet Rule (Krug 2.5b):**
- **H and He:** duet (2 electrons maximum).
- **Be:** often 4 electrons (electron-deficient; $\text{BeCl}_2$).
- **B:** often 6 electrons (electron-deficient; $\text{BF}_3$, $\text{BCl}_3$). B accepts a lone pair as a Lewis acid but does NOT automatically get 8.
- **Period 3+ central atoms (P, S, Cl, etc.):** can have expanded octets (10 or 12 electrons). Examples: $\text{PCl}_5$ (10e⁻ on P), $\text{SF}_6$ (12e⁻ on S), $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ (can be drawn with S having 12e⁻).

**Formal Charge:**
$$\text{FC} = (\text{valence electrons of atom}) - (\text{lone pair electrons on atom}) - \frac{1}{2}(\text{bonding electrons on atom})$$

The best Lewis structure:
1. Minimizes the magnitude of all formal charges (all zero is ideal).
2. Places any negative FC on the more electronegative atom.
3. Avoids placing a positive FC on a highly electronegative atom (F, O) unless unavoidable.

**Formal charge must be calculated — never assumed from a distractor.** MCQ answer choices frequently claim "all atoms have FC = 0" for incorrect structures. Always apply the formula to every atom in every candidate structure.

**Common Mistake:** Choosing the structure with more double/triple bonds because "stronger bonds are better." Bond strength does NOT determine the best Lewis structure — formal charge minimization does. A structure with all single bonds and formal charges of zero beats a structure with a triple bond and formal charges of ±2.

## Topic 2.6: Resonance and Formal Charge

**Essential Knowledge (Farabaugh CED):**
- **Resonance** occurs when more than one valid Lewis structure can be drawn with the same atom connectivity but different electron arrangement. The actual structure is a hybrid (blend) of all resonance structures.
- Resonance structures must have the same skeleton (same connectivity); only electrons move.
- Delocalized electrons in resonance structures are spread over multiple bonds → bond lengths are intermediate between single and double.
- Use formal charge to identify the most stable resonance structure: the one with the lowest formal charges (all zero is ideal), with negative FC on the most electronegative atom.

**Common Resonance Examples:**
- $\text{SO}_3$: 3 equivalent resonance structures (S=O double bond rotates among three positions).
- $\text{NO}_3^-$: 3 equivalent resonance structures.
- $\text{O}_3$: 2 resonance structures (bent molecule).
- Benzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_6$): 2 equivalent resonance structures; all C–C bonds are equivalent (1.5 bond order).

**Selecting the best Lewis structure — formal charge rules (MCQ-critical):**
1. Prefer the structure where all formal charges = 0.
2. If charges cannot all be zero, prefer the structure with the smallest magnitude formal charges.
3. Negative formal charge must sit on the more electronegative atom.
4. Bond strength (single vs. double vs. triple) is NOT the criterion for selecting the best Lewis structure — formal charge minimization is.

## Topic 2.7: VSEPR and Hybridization

**Essential Knowledge (Farabaugh CED):**
- **VSEPR (Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion):** Electron groups around a central atom arrange themselves to minimize repulsion. Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs and compress bond angles.
- **Electron geometry** = arrangement of all electron groups (bonding + lone pairs).
- **Molecular geometry** = arrangement of atoms only (ignoring lone pairs).
- Lone pairs compress bond angles by ~2–2.5° each below the ideal.

**VSEPR Geometry Table (must memorize):**

| Electron Groups | Lone Pairs | Electron Geometry | Molecular Geometry | Bond Angles |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0 | Linear | Linear | 180° |
| 3 | 0 | Trigonal planar | Trigonal planar | 120° |
| 3 | 1 | Trigonal planar | Bent | <120° |
| 4 | 0 | Tetrahedral | Tetrahedral | 109.5° |
| 4 | 1 | Tetrahedral | Trigonal pyramidal | <109.5° |
| 4 | 2 | Tetrahedral | Bent | <109.5° |
| 5 | 0 | Trigonal bipyramidal | Trigonal bipyramidal | 90°, 120° |
| 6 | 0 | Octahedral | Octahedral | 90° |

**Hybridization:**
- 2 electron groups → sp (180°, linear)
- 3 electron groups → $\text{sp}^2$ (120°, trigonal planar)
- 4 electron groups → $\text{sp}^3$ (109.5°, tetrahedral)
- **AP exam does NOT test d-orbital hybridization ($\text{sp}^3\text{d}$, $\text{sp}^3\text{d}^2$).** Use VSEPR to predict geometry for 5- and 6-group molecules, but do NOT assign hybridization.

**Sigma ($\sigma$) and Pi ($\pi$) Bonds:**
- Every single bond = 1 $\sigma$ bond.
- Double bond = 1 $\sigma$ + 1 $\pi$.
- Triple bond = 1 $\sigma$ + 2 $\pi$.
- $\sigma$ bonds: head-on overlap, allow free rotation.
- $\pi$ bonds: side-by-side overlap of unhybridized p orbitals, restrict rotation.

**Molecular Polarity — Two conditions both required:**
![[molecular_polarity_u2.svg|697]]
*Left: BF₃, CO₂, CCl₄ have polar bonds but symmetric geometry — dipoles cancel → nonpolar. Right: CH₂Cl₂, H₂O, NH₃ have asymmetric geometry — dipoles do not cancel → polar.*

1. The molecule contains polar bonds ($\Delta\text{EN} \neq 0$).
2. The bond dipoles do NOT cancel (asymmetric geometry).

**Nonpolar despite polar bonds:** $\text{CO}_2$ (linear — dipoles cancel), $\text{BF}_3$ (trigonal planar), $\text{CCl}_4$ (tetrahedral), $\text{SF}_6$ (octahedral).

**Polarity of $\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2$ vs. $\text{CCl}_4$ (MCQ contrast):**
$\text{CCl}_4$ is tetrahedral with 4 identical C–Cl bonds → perfectly symmetric → dipoles cancel → nonpolar.
$\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2$ is also tetrahedral, but has 2 C–Cl bonds and 2 C–H bonds (different electronegativities) → asymmetric → dipoles do NOT cancel → polar. Same geometry; different substituents → different polarity outcome.

> **Lattice energy comparison:** "MgO has a higher lattice energy than NaCl because the ions $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ and $\text{O}^{2-}$ have greater charges and smaller ionic radii. According to Coulomb's law, the electrostatic force of attraction increases with increasing charge magnitude and decreasing distance between the ions."

A substance is only classified as amphoteric if it passes both tests. [Substance Y] is NOT amphoteric because it reacts with [acid / base] only and does not respond to the other."

## Critical Reminders — Unit 2

> [!danger] H and F Are Never Central Atoms
> H can only form 1 bond — **never central**. F is the most electronegative — **always terminal**. Central atom = least electronegative non-H atom in the formula.

> [!danger] Formal Charge Determines the Best Lewis Structure — NOT Bond Strength
> **Bond strength does NOT determine the best Lewis structure.** "Triple bonds are stronger" is irrelevant. Apply FC = V − LP − ½B to every atom in every candidate. Best structure = all FC near zero, negative FC on the more electronegative atom.

> [!warning] Always Calculate Formal Charge — Never Trust Distractors
> Never accept an answer choice claiming "FC = 0 for all atoms" without verifying. Apply $FC = V - LP - \frac{1}{2}B$ directly to each atom.

> [!warning] Formal Charge ≠ Oxidation State
> They are calculated differently. FC = V − LP − ½(bonding e⁻). Oxidation state = V − (lone pairs) − (all bonding e⁻). Never confuse them.

> [!warning] Resonance: Only Electrons Move, Never Atoms
> Resonance structures have **identical atom connectivity**. Only electrons move between resonance structures. Bond lengths in the resonance hybrid are intermediate — between single and double bond values.

> [!warning] Lone Pairs Compress Bond Angles Below Ideal
> Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs. Every lone pair on the central atom compresses bond angles ~2–2.5° below ideal. H₂O (2 LP): ~105°. NH₃ (1 LP): ~107°.

> [!check] AP Does NOT Test sp³d or sp³d² Hybridization
> For molecules with 5 or 6 electron groups (PCl₅, SF₆, XeF₄), predict geometry using VSEPR but do NOT assign hybridization. The AP exam does not test d-orbital hybridization.

> [!warning] Molecular Polarity Requires BOTH Conditions
> (1) Polar bonds (ΔEN ≠ 0) AND (2) asymmetric geometry so dipoles don't cancel. CO₂ is linear → dipoles cancel → **nonpolar** despite polar C=O bonds. BF₃, CCl₄, SF₆ are all nonpolar despite polar bonds.

> [!warning] CH₂Cl₂ is Polar; CCl₄ is Nonpolar
> Same tetrahedral geometry, different substituents. CCl₄: 4 identical C–Cl bonds → symmetric → dipoles cancel → nonpolar. CH₂Cl₂: 2 C–Cl + 2 C–H → asymmetric → polar. Same geometry, different polarity outcome.

> [!check] σ Bonds Allow Rotation; π Bonds Restrict It
> Single bond = 1σ (free rotation). Double bond = 1σ + 1π (rigid). π bonds prevent rotation because side-by-side p-orbital overlap breaks if the bond rotates. This is the structural basis for cis/trans isomers.

> [!warning] Electron Geometry ≠ Molecular Geometry With Lone Pairs
> NH₃: 4 electron groups → tetrahedral **electron** geometry, but 3 bonding groups → **trigonal pyramidal molecular** geometry. "Geometry of NH₃" on an MCQ = trigonal pyramidal (molecular). Always identify which geometry is being asked.

> [!warning] Ionic Solid Conductivity: Only When Melted or Dissolved
> Solid ionic compound = does NOT conduct (ions fixed). Melted or dissolved = DOES conduct (mobile ions). Metallic solid = DOES conduct (mobile electrons — different mechanism).

> [!check] Malleability and Conductivity Are Different Properties
> Both involve the electron sea, but differently. Malleability = ion layers slide while surrounded by the sea. Conductivity = electrons flow in response to a potential. A particulate diagram showing layer sliding = malleability, NOT conductivity.

> [!check] Compounds With Polyatomic Ions Contain BOTH Bond Types
> Mg(NO₃)₂: the Mg²⁺ ↔ NO₃⁻ interaction is **ionic**; the N–O bonds within NO₃⁻ are **covalent**. Both types are present in the same compound.
