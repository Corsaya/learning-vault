## Study Hub Quick Reference — Unit 8

> [!check] Q21 — Buffers: Conjugate Base Neutralizes Added Strong Acid
> A buffer contains a weak acid (HA) and its conjugate base (A⁻). When strong acid (H⁺) is added: **A⁻ + H⁺ → HA**. The conjugate base consumes the added acid, preventing large pH drops. When strong base (OH⁻) is added: **HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H₂O**. The weak acid consumes the added base.

> [!check] Q27 — Acid Strength: Percent Ionization and pKa
> **Stronger acid** = lower pKa = larger Ka = higher percent ionization = more H⁺ at equilibrium. At equal concentrations, stronger acid → lower pH. Percent ionization = $([A^-]_{eq}/[HA]_{initial}) \times 100\%$. Diluting a weak acid **decreases** [H⁺] but **increases** % ionization.

> [!check] Q28 — Titration Stoichiometry: Finding Equivalence Volume
> At equivalence: moles of H⁺ = moles of OH⁻ (1:1 stoichiometry). $n_{acid} = M_{acid} \times V_{acid}$; $n_{base} = M_{base} \times V_{base}$. Set equal and solve for the unknown volume. For polyprotic acids, multiply moles by the number of ionizable protons.

> [!check] Q29 — Kw and Neutral pH at Non-Standard Temperature
> At 25°C: $K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$ → neutral pH = 7.00. At **higher temperatures**, $K_w$ increases (autoionization is endothermic) → neutral pH **< 7** (but the solution is still neutral — $[H^+] = [OH^-]$). **Neutral means $[H^+] = [OH^-]$, NOT pH = 7.**

> [!check] Q32 — Strong Acid Dilution: C₁V₁ = C₂V₂
> Diluting a strong acid: $C_1V_1 = C_2V_2$ → find new $[H^+]$ (= new [HCl] since fully dissociated) → $pH = -\log[H^+]$. Diluting 10× means $[H^+]$ decreases 10× → pH increases by exactly 1 unit (for strong acid).

> [!check] Q39 — Salt Hydrolysis: Conjugate Acid of Weak Base → Acidic Solution
> When a salt containing the conjugate acid of a weak base dissolves (e.g., NH₄Cl), the cation hydrolyzes: **NH₄⁺ + H₂O → NH₃ + H₃O⁺** → solution is **acidic** (pH < 7). Conversely, the conjugate base of a weak acid (e.g., CH₃COO⁻) makes a **basic** solution.

> [!check] Q41 — Weak Acid Titration: At Equivalence Point, All HA → A⁻
> At the equivalence point of weak acid + strong base: all HA has been converted to A⁻ (conjugate base). Solution contains **only A⁻**, which hydrolyzes: $A^- + H_2O \rightleftharpoons HA + OH^-$ → solution is **basic** (pH > 7 at equivalence). Unlike strong acid/base (pH = 7).

> [!check] FRQ Q4A — Brønsted-Lowry Conjugate Pairs
> A conjugate acid-base pair differs by exactly **one proton (H⁺)**. Acid donates H⁺ → becomes conjugate base. Base accepts H⁺ → becomes conjugate acid. **Trap:** the acid and base on the reactant side are NOT a conjugate pair — each reactant pairs with its own product across the arrow.

> [!check] FRQ Q4B — Titration Curves: Strong vs. Weak Acid
> **Strong acid:** starts at lower pH (fully dissociated → more H⁺); equivalence point pH = 7. **Weak acid:** starts at higher pH; equivalence point pH > 7 (conjugate base hydrolyzes). **Half-equivalence point** of weak acid: pH = pKa (Henderson-Hasselbalch with [HA] = [A⁻]).

> [!check] FRQ Q4C — Buffer Requirements
> A buffer requires: (1) a **weak acid** AND (2) its **conjugate base** in significant amounts (or weak base + conjugate acid). A strong acid + its salt is NOT a buffer. Added H⁺ → A⁻ + H⁺ → HA; Added OH⁻ → HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H₂O.

> [!check] FRQ Q7A — pH Calculation: [H⁺] = 10^(−pH)
> $pH = -\log[H^+]$ → $[H^+] = 10^{-pH}$. For strong acid: $[H^+]$ = [acid] (fully dissociated, no ICE table). For weak acid: ICE table with Ka. At 25°C: $pOH = 14 - pH$ and $[OH^-] = 10^{-pOH}$.

> [!warning] Trap — Equivalence Point pH Depends on Acid/Base Strength
> Strong + strong → pH = 7. Weak acid + strong base → pH **> 7** (A⁻ hydrolyzes). Strong acid + weak base → pH **< 7** (BH⁺ hydrolyzes). Never assume equivalence point = pH 7 unless both are strong.

> [!warning] Trap — Neutral ≠ pH 7 Above 25°C
> At temperatures above 25°C, $K_w > 10^{-14}$ → neutral pH < 7. A solution with pH = 6.5 at 50°C can be perfectly neutral if $[H^+] = [OH^-]$.

> [!danger] Buffer ≠ Weak Acid Alone
> A weak acid alone is NOT a buffer. A buffer requires **both** the weak acid AND its conjugate base in comparable amounts. Adding a small amount of strong base to a large amount of weak acid creates a buffer only if the base doesn't consume all the acid.

## Topic 8.1: Introduction to Acids and Bases

**Essential Knowledge (Farabaugh CED):**
- **Brønsted-Lowry definition**: An acid is a **proton donor** ($\text{H}^+$ donor); a base is a **proton acceptor**.
- **Conjugate acid-base pairs**: differ by one proton ($\text{H}^+$). When an acid donates $\text{H}^+$, the resulting species is its conjugate base. When a base accepts $\text{H}^+$, the result is its conjugate acid.
- **Autoionization of water**: $\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq)$; $K_w = [\text{H}^+][\text{OH}^-] = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$ at 25°C.
- $K_w$ increases with temperature (endothermic process). At $T > 25°\text{C}$, neutral pH $< 7$ (but solution remains neutral because $[\text{H}^+] = [\text{OH}^-]$).
- **pH and pOH**: $\text{pH} = -\log[\text{H}^+]$; $\text{pOH} = -\log[\text{OH}^-]$; $\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14.00$ at 25°C. At other temperatures: $\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = \text{p}K_w = -\log K_w$.
- **Neutral solution**: $[\text{H}^+] = [\text{OH}^-]$, always. The numerical value of the neutral pH changes with temperature.

**Conjugate Pair Identification:**

| Acid | Conjugate Base |
|---|---|
| $\text{HCl}$ | $\text{Cl}^-$ |
| $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$ | $\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-$ |
| $\text{NH}_4^+$ | $\text{NH}_3$ |
| $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ | $\text{OH}^-$ |
| $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ | $\text{HSO}_4^-$ |

**Amphoteric species**: can act as both acid and base. $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and $\text{HCO}_3^-$ are classic examples.

**$K_w$ and Neutral pH at Different Temperatures:**

| Temperature | $K_w$ | Neutral pH |
|---|---|---|
| 25°C | $1.0 \times 10^{-14}$ | 7.00 |
| 37°C | $\approx 2.4 \times 10^{-14}$ | $\approx 6.81$ |
| 55°C | $7.0 \times 10^{-14}$ | $\approx 6.58$ |

## Topic 8.2: pH and pOH of Strong Acids and Bases

**Essential Knowledge (Farabaugh CED):**
- **Strong acids** dissociate **completely** in aqueous solution: $[\text{H}^+]$ = initial acid concentration exactly. No $K_a$ equilibrium calculation needed.
- Seven strong acids: **HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, HClO₄, HClO₃** (memorize these).
- **Strong bases** dissociate completely: $[\text{OH}^-]$ = (initial base concentration) × (OH⁻ per formula unit).
  - Diprotic strong bases ($\text{Ca(OH)}_2$, $\text{Sr(OH)}_2$, $\text{Ba(OH)}_2$): multiply concentration by 2.

**pH Calculation Roadmap:**

$$\text{Strong acid:} \quad [\text{H}^+] = C_{\text{acid}} \implies \text{pH} = -\log C_{\text{acid}}$$

$$\text{Strong base:} \quad [\text{OH}^-] = n \cdot C_{\text{base}} \implies \text{pOH} = -\log[\text{OH}^-] \implies \text{pH} = 14 - \text{pOH}$$

$$\text{Diprotic base} \text{ (e.g., Ba(OH)}_2): \quad [\text{OH}^-] = 2 \times C_{\text{base}}$$

**pH of Dilute Strong Acids (below ~$10^{-6}$ M):**
Water's contribution ($10^{-7}\ M\ \text{H}^+$) becomes non-negligible only if acid concentration $< 10^{-6}\ M$. For concentrations $\geq 10^{-5}\ M$, ignore water's contribution.

## Topic 8.3: Weak Acid and Base Equilibria

**Essential Knowledge (Farabaugh CED):**
- **Weak acids** partially ionize: $K_a$ is small but nonzero. Set up an ICE table.
$$\text{HA}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_3\text{O}^+(aq) + \text{A}^-(aq)$$
$$K_a = \frac{[\text{H}_3\text{O}^+][\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]}$$
- **Weak bases** partially accept protons: $K_b$ expression analogous.
$$\text{B}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \rightleftharpoons \text{BH}^+(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq)$$
$$K_b = \frac{[\text{BH}^+][\text{OH}^-]}{[\text{B}]}$$
- **Relationship**: $K_a \times K_b = K_w$ for a conjugate acid-base pair. $\text{p}K_a + \text{p}K_b = 14$ at 25°C.
- Stronger acid → larger $K_a$ → smaller $\text{p}K_a$ → weaker conjugate base (smaller $K_b$).

**ICE Table for Weak Acid:**

| | $\text{HA}$ | $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$ | $\text{A}^-$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | $C_a$ | 0 | 0 |
| **C** | $-x$ | $+x$ | $+x$ |
| **E** | $C_a - x$ | $x$ | $x$ |

$$K_a = \frac{x^2}{C_a - x} \approx \frac{x^2}{C_a} \quad \text{(if } x \ll C_a\text{)}$$

$$x = [\text{H}^+] = \sqrt{K_a \cdot C_a}$$

**Percent Ionization:**
$$\% \text{ ionization} = \frac{[\text{H}^+]_{\text{eq}}}{C_a} \times 100\%$$

Diluting a weak acid **increases % ionization** but **decreases** $[\text{H}^+]$ (fewer moles total, but a larger fraction ionizes).

![[weak_acid_eq_u8.svg|697]]
*ICE table for weak acid HA: at equilibrium, $[\text{H}^+] = \sqrt{K_a C}$ (approximation). Doubling concentration increases $[\text{H}^+]$ by $\sqrt{2}$, not a factor of 2 — only strong acids scale linearly.*

## Topic 8.4: Acid-Base Reactions and Buffers

**Essential Knowledge (Farabaugh CED):**
- **Neutralization**: strong acid + strong base → water + salt. pH at equivalence = 7.00 (25°C).
- **Buffer systems**: a weak acid and its conjugate base (or weak base and conjugate acid) resist pH changes.
  - When strong acid is added: the **conjugate base** component neutralizes it: $\text{A}^-(aq) + \text{H}^+(aq) \to \text{HA}(aq)$.
  - When strong base is added: the **weak acid** component neutralizes it: $\text{HA}(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq) \to \text{A}^-(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$.
- Buffers only resist pH changes — they do not prevent them entirely. A small pH change occurs with each addition.

**Acid-Base Stoichiometry Before Buffer Calculations:**
When strong acid/base is added to a weak acid or buffer:
1. Complete the stoichiometry (moles, not concentrations — volume cancels in H-H).
2. Calculate remaining moles of each buffer component.
3. Then apply Henderson-Hasselbalch.

## Topic 8.5: Acid-Base Titrations

**Essential Knowledge (Farabaugh CED):**
- **Titration**: adding a known concentration of titrant (from a burette) to an analyte until the equivalence point is reached.
- At the **equivalence point**: moles of titrant = moles of analyte (adjusted for stoichiometry). The pH depends on the type of titration.
- **Half-equivalence point**: moles of titrant = half the moles of analyte. At this point, $[\text{HA}] = [\text{A}^-]$, so by Henderson-Hasselbalch: $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a$.

**pH at Different Points in a Weak Acid / Strong Base Titration:**

| Region | pH Calculation |
|---|---|
| Before any titrant added | Weak acid ICE table; $[\text{H}^+] = \sqrt{K_a C}$ |
| Buffer region (before equivalence) | Henderson-Hasselbalch; stoichiometry first |
| Half-equivalence point | $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a$ |
| Equivalence point | Only conjugate base present; $[\text{OH}^-] = \sqrt{K_b C}$; $\text{pH} > 7$ |
| After equivalence point | Excess strong base dominates; $\text{pH} = 14 + \log[\text{OH}^-]$ |

![[titration_curve_u8.svg|697]]
*Weak acid/strong base titration curve. Key points: (1) initial pH from weak acid ICE, (2) buffer region — gradual slope, (3) half-equivalence point: pH = pKa, (4) equivalence point: pH > 7 (conjugate base hydrolyzes), (5) post-equivalence: sharp rise.*

**Reading pKa from a Titration Curve:**
- Find the equivalence volume (large jump in pH).
- Divide by 2 → half-equivalence volume.
- Read the pH at the half-equivalence volume from the curve.
- That pH value = $\text{p}K_a$.

**Finding Unknown Concentration from Titration:**
At equivalence: $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$ (for 1:1 stoichiometry). Divide moles of titrant added by volume of analyte.

## Topic 8.6: Molecular Structure of Acids and Bases

**Essential Knowledge (Farabaugh CED):**
- Acid strength is determined by the **stability of the conjugate base** (how well it can accommodate the negative charge after $\text{H}^+$ is released).
- **Inductive effect**: electronegative substituents withdraw electron density through bonds, stabilizing the conjugate base → stronger acid.
  - More electronegative substituents → stronger acid.
  - More substituents → stronger acid ($\text{CCl}_3\text{COOH} > \text{CHCl}_2\text{COOH} > \text{CH}_2\text{Cl·COOH} > \text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$).
- **Oxyacids ($\text{H}_x\text{EO}_y$)**: same central element → more oxygens → stronger acid (more $\text{O}$ atoms pull electron density, better stabilizing conjugate base). Example: $\text{HClO}_4 > \text{HClO}_3 > \text{HClO}_2 > \text{HClO}$.
- **Binary acids (H-X)**: going down a group, bond strength decreases → easier to ionize → stronger acid ($\text{HI} > \text{HBr} > \text{HCl} > \text{HF}$). The bond strength factor dominates over electronegativity going down the group.
- **Base strength**: availability of the lone pair on the base nitrogen/oxygen.
  - Electron-withdrawing groups (e.g., F in $\text{NF}_3$) reduce lone pair availability → weaker base.
  - Electron-donating groups → stronger base.

**Comparing Oxyacid Strength:**

| Factor | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Same central element, more O | More O atoms = stronger acid | $\text{H}_2\text{SeO}_4 > \text{H}_2\text{SeO}_3$ |
| Same structure, more electronegative center | More electronegativity = stronger acid | $\text{H}_2\text{SeO}_4 > \text{H}_2\text{TeO}_4$ |
| Haloacids going down group | More polarizable bond = stronger acid | $\text{HI} > \text{HBr} > \text{HCl}$ |

## Topic 8.7: pH and pKa

**Essential Knowledge (Farabaugh CED):**
- **Henderson-Hasselbalch equation:**
$$\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]}$$
- The ratio $[\text{A}^-]/[\text{HA}]$ determines whether the weak acid or conjugate base dominates at a given pH.
- **pH vs. $\text{p}K_a$ comparison:**

| Condition | Dominant species | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| $\text{pH} > \text{p}K_a$ | $[\text{A}^-] > [\text{HA}]$ | Log term positive → conjugate base dominates |
| $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a$ | $[\text{A}^-] = [\text{HA}]$ | Log term = 0 |
| $\text{pH} < \text{p}K_a$ | $[\text{HA}] > [\text{A}^-]$ | Log term negative → weak acid dominates |

![[ph_pka_u8.svg|697]]
*pH vs. pKa diagram: below pKa, the acid form HA dominates; above pKa, the base form A⁻ dominates; at pKa, equal concentrations. Each unit above/below pKa represents a 10-fold change in the ratio.*

**Quantitative relationship:**
$$\frac{[\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]} = 10^{(\text{pH} - \text{p}K_a)}$$

If $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + 1$: $[\text{A}^-] = 10 \times [\text{HA}]$.
If $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a - 1$: $[\text{HA}] = 10 \times [\text{A}^-]$.

## Topic 8.8–8.10: Properties of Buffers, Henderson-Hasselbalch, Buffer Capacity

**Essential Knowledge (Farabaugh CED):**
- A **buffer** is a solution containing a weak acid and its conjugate base (or weak base and its conjugate acid) in comparable amounts.
- **Buffer pH** depends only on the **ratio** $[\text{A}^-]/[\text{HA}]$ and $\text{p}K_a$ — not on absolute concentrations. Two buffers with the same ratio have identical pH even if concentrations differ.
- **Buffer capacity**: the ability to resist pH change. Capacity is highest when $[\text{A}^-] \approx [\text{HA}]$ (near the half-equivalence point, $\text{pH} \approx \text{p}K_a$). Higher total concentration → more buffer capacity.
- Adding strong acid to a buffer: conjugate base is consumed; weak acid is produced; $[\text{A}^-]/[\text{HA}]$ ratio decreases; pH decreases slightly.
- Adding strong base to a buffer: weak acid is consumed; conjugate base is produced; ratio increases; pH increases slightly.
- When a buffer is **overwhelmed** (excess strong acid/base exceeds the amount of buffer component that can neutralize it), the buffering action fails and pH changes dramatically.

**Buffer Composition and Capacity Table:**

| Condition | pH effect | Capacity for acid | Capacity for base |
|---|---|---|---|
| $[\text{A}^-] \gg [\text{HA}]$ | pH $\gg$ p$K_a$ | High | Low |
| $[\text{A}^-] = [\text{HA}]$ | pH = p$K_a$ | Balanced | Balanced |
| $[\text{HA}] \gg [\text{A}^-]$ | pH $\ll$ p$K_a$ | Low | High |

![[buffer_action_u8.svg|697]]
*Buffer action: added acid is absorbed by A⁻ → A⁻ decreases, HA increases, pH drops slightly. Added base is absorbed by HA → HA decreases, A⁻ increases, pH rises slightly. Once either component is exhausted, buffering fails.*

## Topic 8.11: pH and Solubility

**Essential Knowledge (Farabaugh CED):**
- **Salts of weak acids** are more soluble at lower pH. The anion of the salt (conjugate base of the weak acid) is protonated by $\text{H}^+$, removing it from equilibrium and driving further dissolution.
$$\text{M}^{n+}(aq) + n\text{A}^-(aq) \rightleftharpoons \text{MA}_n(s)$$
$$\text{H}^+(aq) + \text{A}^-(aq) \to \text{HA}(aq) \quad \text{(acid removes } \text{A}^-\text{, shifts dissolution right)}$$
- **Salts of strong acids** (e.g., $\text{AgCl}$, $\text{BaSO}_4$) are pH-independent because $\text{Cl}^-$ and $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ do not react with $\text{H}^+$ (they are conjugate bases of strong acids — too weak to be protonated).
- Adding **base (OH⁻)** to a metal hydroxide equilibrium ($\text{M(OH)}_n$) suppresses dissolution (common-ion effect): more solid precipitates.
- **Beer's Law connection**: if the colored ion is the dissolved species (e.g., $\text{Cu}^{2+}$), lower pH → more dissolution → higher $[\text{Cu}^{2+}]$ → higher absorbance ($A = \varepsilon lc$).

**pH Effect on Solubility Summary:**

| Salt type | Effect of lower pH | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Salt of weak acid (e.g., $\text{CaF}_2$, $\text{CaCO}_3$) | **Solubility increases** | $\text{H}^+$ protonates $\text{F}^-$ or $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$, removing product |
| Salt of strong acid (e.g., $\text{AgCl}$) | No change | $\text{Cl}^-$ is not protonated by $\text{H}^+$ |
| Metal hydroxide (e.g., $\text{Mg(OH)}_2$) | Solubility increases | $\text{H}^+$ neutralizes $\text{OH}^-$, pulling equilibrium right |
| Metal hydroxide + added base | Solubility decreases | Common-ion ($\text{OH}^-$) shifts equilibrium left |

## AP Answer Templates — Unit 8

> **Strong acid/base pH:** "HCl / HBr / $\text{HNO}_3$ / HClO₄ is a strong acid that dissociates completely. Therefore $[\text{H}^+] = [C]\ \text{M}$ and $\text{pH} = -\log([C]) = [value]$. No $K_a$ equilibrium calculation is needed."

> **Weak acid pH (ICE):** "Setting up an ICE table with initial $[\text{HA}] = C$, $[\text{H}^+] = 0$: at equilibrium, $[\text{H}^+] = x$. $K_a = x^2/(C - x) \approx x^2/C$ (approximation valid if $x/C < 0.05$). $x = \sqrt{K_a \cdot C} = [value]$. $\text{pH} = -\log(x) = [value]$."

> **Dilution effect on weak acid:** "Diluting the weak acid decreases the absolute $[\text{H}^+]$ (fewer moles of acid total), but the fraction that ionizes (percent ionization) increases. This is because dilution shifts the weak acid equilibrium right (Le Chatelier), but the increase in fraction ionized does not compensate for the decrease in total acid present."

> **Acid-base stoichiometry before H-H:** "First perform the neutralization stoichiometry: $[\text{mol acid}] - [\text{mol base added}] = [\text{mol acid remaining}]$; $[\text{mol base added}] = [\text{mol conjugate base formed}]$. Then apply Henderson-Hasselbalch: $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log([n_{\text{base}}]/[n_{\text{acid}}])$. Note: moles (not concentrations) may be used directly since both species share the same volume."

> **Half-equivalence pKa reading:** "At the half-equivalence point, exactly half the weak acid has been converted to conjugate base, so $[\text{HA}] = [\text{A}^-]$ and the log term in Henderson-Hasselbalch equals zero. Therefore, $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a$ at the half-equivalence volume. Reading the pH at the half-equivalence volume from the titration curve gives $\text{p}K_a \approx [value]$."

> **Buffer neutralization direction:** "When strong [acid/base] is added to the buffer, it is neutralized by the [conjugate base / weak acid] component: $[\text{A}^-] + \text{H}^+ \to \text{HA}$ [or vice versa]. This consumes the strong [acid/base] and converts it to the weak [acid/base], preventing a large pH change."

> **Acid strength from structure:** "The [greater number of electronegative substituents / higher electronegativity of the central atom] stabilizes the conjugate base by withdrawing electron density from the O–H bond through induction. A more stable conjugate base means the acid is stronger (lower $\text{p}K_a$)."

> **pH and solubility (acid effect):** "At lower pH, $\text{H}^+$ reacts with $[\text{anion}]$ to form $[\text{weak acid product}]$. This removes the anion (a product of dissolution) from equilibrium, shifting the dissolution equilibrium to the right. Therefore, $[\text{salt}]$ is more soluble at lower pH. This applies to salts of weak acids, not strong acids."

> **Buffer capacity error:** "Less $[\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}]$ than intended means $[\text{NH}_4^+] < [\text{NH}_3]$. By Henderson-Hasselbalch, this gives a pH higher than intended ($\log[\text{NH}_3]/[\text{NH}_4^+] > 0$). The buffer has greater capacity to neutralize added acid (more $\text{NH}_3$ available) but less capacity for added base (less $\text{NH}_4^+$ available)."

## Critical Reminders — Unit 8

> [!danger] Strong Acids: No ICE Table
> $[\text{H}^+]$ = initial concentration exactly. There is no $K_a$ to use — skip the ICE table entirely. Applying ICE to a strong acid is a guaranteed error.

> [!danger] Diprotic Strong Bases: Factor of 2
> $\text{Ca(OH)}_2$, $\text{Sr(OH)}_2$, $\text{Ba(OH)}_2$: $[\text{OH}^-] = 2 \times C_{\text{base}}$. Forgetting the factor of 2 gives a full pH unit of error.

> [!danger] Neutral ≠ pH 7
> Neutral means $[\text{H}^+] = [\text{OH}^-]$. At 55°C with $K_w = 7 \times 10^{-14}$, neutral pH ≈ 6.58. Pure water at **any** temperature is always neutral — "acidic" and "pH < 7" are not the same thing.

> [!danger] Henderson-Hasselbalch: Conjugate Base in the Numerator
> $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log[\text{A}^-]/[\text{HA}]$ — the numerator is always the **conjugate base** ($\text{A}^-$), not the weak acid. Inverting the fraction gives the wrong pH.

> [!warning] Diluting a Weak Acid: Opposite Trends
> $[\text{H}^+]$ decreases (fewer moles total); % ionization **increases** (Le Chatelier shifts right). These trend in opposite directions — do not confuse them.

> [!warning] Stoichiometry Before Henderson-Hasselbalch
> When NaOH is added to a buffer, first calculate moles of acid consumed and conjugate base produced. Then plug those **moles** into H-H (volume cancels — moles work directly).

> [!warning] Always Use Total Final Volume
> When calculating excess acid/base concentration after mixing two solutions, divide by **total** final volume. Never divide by only one of the original volumes.

> [!warning] Common-Ion with OH⁻ Suppresses Dissolution
> Adding KOH to a saturated $\text{Mg(OH)}_2$ solution adds $\text{OH}^-$ (common ion) → equilibrium shifts left → more precipitate → cloudier solution.

> [!check] pH vs. pKa: Which Species Dominates?
> pH > $\text{p}K_a$ → conjugate base dominates ($[\text{A}^-] > [\text{HA}]$); pH < $\text{p}K_a$ → weak acid dominates. Each full unit of difference = 10-fold ratio between the two species.

> [!check] Half-Equivalence Point: pH = pKa
> At half the equivalence volume, $[\text{HA}] = [\text{A}^-]$ → log term = 0 → pH = $\text{p}K_a$ exactly. Most testable landmark on any titration curve.

> [!check] Equivalence Point (Weak Acid + Strong Base): pH > 7
> All HA has been converted to $\text{A}^-$. The conjugate base hydrolyzes: $\text{A}^- + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{HA} + \text{OH}^-$. The solution is basic, not neutral.

> [!check] Acid Strength from Structure
> **Inductive effect**: more electronegative substituents → more stable conjugate base → stronger acid. **Oxyacids**: more oxygens on the same central element → stronger acid ($\text{HClO}_4 > \text{HClO}_3 > \text{HClO}_2 > \text{HClO}$). **Binary acids**: down a group, bond strength decreases → stronger acid ($\text{HI} > \text{HBr} > \text{HCl}$) — electronegativity alone predicts the opposite.

> [!check] Salts and pH Dependence
> **Salts of weak acids** (e.g., $\text{CaF}_2$, $\text{CaCO}_3$): dissolve more at low pH — $\text{H}^+$ protonates the anion, pulling equilibrium right. **Salts of strong acids** (e.g., AgCl): pH-independent — $\text{Cl}^-$ and $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ are not protonated.

> [!check] Buffer Capacity and Failure
> Capacity is **maximized** when $[\text{A}^-] = [\text{HA}]$ (pH = $\text{p}K_a$). Unequal amounts skew capacity toward one direction. **Buffer failure**: excess strong acid/base exhausts one component — pH then changes sharply.
