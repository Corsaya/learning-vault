## Topic 1.1: Moles and Molar Mass

**Example 1 — Mole-to-mass conversion:**
How many grams are in 2.50 mol of $\text{Ca(NO}_3)_2$?

$$M = 40.08 + 2(14.01) + 6(16.00) = 164.10 \text{ g/mol}$$

$$m = nM = (2.50)(164.10) = \mathbf{410 \text{ g}}$$

**Example 2 — Particles to moles:**
How many molecules are in 4.60 g of ethanol ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$)?

$$M = 2(12.01) + 6(1.008) + 16.00 = 46.07 \text{ g/mol}$$

$$n = \frac{4.60}{46.07} = 0.0998 \text{ mol}$$

$$N = (0.0998)(6.022 \times 10^{23}) = \mathbf{6.01 \times 10^{22} \text{ molecules}}$$

**Example 3 — MCQ-style (no calculator):**
Which sample contains the greatest number of moles?

- (A) 28 g $\text{N}_2$ → $M = 28$ g/mol → 1.0 mol
- (B) 32 g $\text{O}_2$ → $M = 32$ g/mol → 1.0 mol
- (C) 18 g $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ → $M = 18$ g/mol → 1.0 mol (all equal — this is the trap)
- (D) 44 g $\text{CO}_2$ → $M = 44$ g/mol → 1.0 mol

Answer: All equal. If the question asked 28 g $\text{CO}_2$ vs 28 g $\text{N}_2$: $\text{CO}_2$ ($M = 44$) gives 0.64 mol vs $\text{N}_2$ ($M = 28$) gives 1.0 mol. More mass per mole = fewer moles.

**Example 4 — Greatest mass for 1.0 mol (Progress Check Q3):**
Which 1.0 mol sample has the greatest mass? NO, $\text{NO}_2$, $\text{N}_2\text{O}$, $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$?

- NO: $M = 14 + 16 = 30$ g/mol → 30 g
- $\text{NO}_2$: $M = 14 + 32 = 46$ g/mol → 46 g
- $\text{N}_2\text{O}$: $M = 28 + 16 = 44$ g/mol → 44 g
- $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$: $M = 28 + 80 = \mathbf{108}$ g/mol → **108 g** ✓

**Answer: $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$** — for equal moles, greatest molar mass = greatest mass.

*Key move: 1.0 mol of any substance has mass equal to its molar mass in grams. Compare molar masses directly.*

## Topic 1.2: Mass Spectra of Elements

![[mass_spectrum_weighted_average.svg|697]]

**Example 1 — Weighted average from spectrum:**
Element X has two peaks: m/z = 79 at 50.7% abundance and m/z = 81 at 49.3% abundance.

$$\bar{m} = (79)(0.507) + (81)(0.493) = 40.05 + 39.93 = \mathbf{79.99 \approx 80.0 \text{ amu}}$$

This is bromine (Br). Note the average (80.0) lies between the two peaks and is closer to the more abundant one.

**Example 2 — Reading peak height:**
A spectrum shows 3 peaks for element Y at m/z = 28, 29, 30 with relative heights 100, 5, 3. The most abundant isotope has mass 28. Average atomic mass will be only slightly above 28. This is silicon (Si).

**Example 3 — AP Test Style (2022 MCQ):**
A mass spectrum shows two peaks: m/z = 63 (69.2% abundant) and m/z = 65 (30.8% abundant).

$$\bar{m} = (63)(0.692) + (65)(0.308) = 43.60 + 20.02 = \mathbf{63.6 \text{ amu}}$$

This is copper (Cu). Average is closer to 63 because that isotope is more abundant.

**Example 4 — Two-peak spectrum calculation (Progress Check Q6):**
Mass spectrum shows peaks at m/z = 185 (~35%) and m/z = 187 (~65%).

$$\bar{m} = (185)(0.35) + (187)(0.65) = 64.75 + 121.55 = \mathbf{186.3 \text{ amu}}$$

Average falls closer to 187 (the more abundant isotope). This is rhenium (Re).

*Key move: always convert % → decimal before multiplying. The average is pulled toward the taller peak but never equals it exactly when a second isotope exists.*

**Example 5 — Identify element from number of peaks (Progress Check Q5):**
A spectrum shows ~7 peaks spread across m/z 154–160. Strategy: count peaks = number of stable isotopes. Seven isotopes in the 154–160 range with average ~157 → Gadolinium (Gd, $Z = 64$, actual = 157.25 amu). Eliminate: Eu (2 isotopes), Tb (1 isotope), Dy (7 isotopes but centered higher ~162.5 amu).

*Key move: the number of peaks uniquely constrains the element before you even calculate the average. Use it as the first filter.*

**Example 6 — Element with a single stable isotope (AP MCQ distinction):**
A mass spectrum shows a single peak at m/z = 127 with 100% relative abundance.

What can be concluded?
- This element has exactly ONE naturally occurring stable isotope (single peak)
- The atomic mass = 127 amu (since there is no other isotope to average with)
- Identity: Iodine (I, $Z = 53$, atomic mass = 126.90 amu) ✓

Why no weighted average? With only one isotope, 100% of the element is that mass. There are no other masses to weight. The "average" equals the only isotope's mass.

Other elements with single stable isotopes: F (m/z = 19), P (m/z = 31), Mn (m/z = 55), Co (m/z = 59), As (m/z = 75), Nb (m/z = 93), Rh (m/z = 103).

*Key move: Count peaks before calculating anything. One peak = one stable isotope = atomic mass equals the peak position exactly. No calculation needed. Multi-peak elements require weighted average.*

## Topic 1.3: Elemental Composition

**Example 1 — Empirical formula from % composition:**
A compound is 40.0% C, 6.71% H, 53.3% O.

Step 1 (assume 100 g): 40.0 g C, 6.71 g H, 53.3 g O

Step 2 (convert to moles):
$$n_C = \frac{40.0}{12.01} = 3.33 \quad n_H = \frac{6.71}{1.008} = 6.66 \quad n_O = \frac{53.3}{16.00} = 3.33$$

Step 3 (divide by smallest = 3.33): C = 1, H = 2, O = 1

Empirical formula: $\mathbf{\text{CH}_2\text{O}}$ (formaldehyde, or glucose if $n = 6$)

**Example 2 — Molecular formula:**
The compound above has a molar mass of 180 g/mol. Empirical formula mass = 12 + 2 + 16 = 30 g/mol.

$$n = \frac{180}{30} = 6$$

Molecular formula: $\mathbf{\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6}$ (glucose)

**Example 3 — Combustion analysis (AP FRQ crossover):**
A 0.500 g hydrocarbon burns completely to give 1.571 g $\text{CO}_2$ and 0.643 g $\text{H}_2\text{O}$.

$$n_C = n_{\text{CO}_2} = \frac{1.571}{44.01} = 0.0357 \text{ mol} \quad \rightarrow \quad m_C = 0.0357 \times 12.01 = 0.429 \text{ g}$$

$$n_H = 2 \times n_{\text{H}_2\text{O}} = 2 \times \frac{0.643}{18.02} = 0.0714 \text{ mol} \quad \rightarrow \quad m_H = 0.0714 \times 1.008 = 0.072 \text{ g}$$

Check: $0.429 + 0.072 = 0.501 \approx 0.500$ g ✓ (pure hydrocarbon, no O)

Ratio C:H = 0.0357 : 0.0714 = 1 : 2 → **$\text{CH}_2$ empirical formula**

**Example 6 — Combustion analysis with oxygen in the compound (AP FRQ crossover, Farabaugh):**
A 0.750 g sample of a compound containing only C, H, and O is burned completely. 1.320 g CO₂ and 0.540 g H₂O are collected.

Step 1 — Find moles of C and H:

$$n_C = n(\text{CO}_2) = \frac{1.320}{44.01} = 0.02999 \text{ mol} \quad \rightarrow \quad m_C = 0.02999 \times 12.01 = 0.360 \text{ g}$$

$$n_H = 2 \times n(\text{H}_2\text{O}) = 2 \times \frac{0.540}{18.02} = 0.05993 \text{ mol} \quad \rightarrow \quad m_H = 0.05993 \times 1.008 = 0.0604 \text{ g}$$

Step 2 — Find mass and moles of O by subtraction:

$$m_O = 0.750 - 0.360 - 0.060 = 0.330 \text{ g}$$

$$n_O = \frac{0.330}{16.00} = 0.02063 \text{ mol}$$

Step 3 — Find ratios (divide by smallest = 0.02063):

$$\text{C: } \frac{0.02999}{0.02063} = 1.45 \approx 1.5 \qquad \text{H: } \frac{0.05993}{0.02063} = 2.90 \approx 3 \qquad \text{O: } 1.00$$

Multiply by 2: C:H:O = 3:6:2 → **Empirical formula: $\text{C}_3\text{H}_6\text{O}_2$** (e.g., propionic acid)

*Key move: When a compound may contain O (any compound with C, H, and O), you CANNOT assume the sample mass is all C and H. Always calculate $m_C$ and $m_H$ first, then subtract from total sample mass to find $m_O$. Never use CO₂ or H₂O masses to find oxygen in the original compound — those oxygens came from the O₂ burned, not the compound.*

## Topic 1.4: Composition of Mixtures

**Example 1 — Mole fraction from mass data (Progress Check Q12):**
A vessel contains 4.0 g $\text{H}_2$ and 32.0 g $\text{O}_2$. Find the mole fraction of each gas.

Step 1 (mass → moles, requires molar mass):
$$n_{\text{H}_2} = \frac{4.0}{2.02} = 1.98 \text{ mol} \qquad n_{\text{O}_2} = \frac{32.0}{32.00} = 1.00 \text{ mol}$$

Step 2 (total moles):
$$n_{\text{total}} = 1.98 + 1.00 = 2.98 \text{ mol}$$

Step 3 (mole fractions):
$$\chi_{\text{H}_2} = \frac{1.98}{2.98} = \mathbf{0.664} \qquad \chi_{\text{O}_2} = \frac{1.00}{2.98} = \mathbf{0.336}$$

Check: $0.664 + 0.336 = 1.000$ ✓

*Key move: you cannot convert mass to mole fraction without molar mass. If a question asks "what additional info is needed to find mole fraction?", the answer is always molar mass.*

**Example 2 — Purity determination from elemental mass % (Progress Check Q10):**
A jar labeled NaCl measures 35.2% Na by mass. Is it pure?

Theoretical % Na in NaCl:
$$\frac{22.99}{58.44} \times 100 = \mathbf{39.3\%}$$

Measured 35.2% ≠ 39.3% → **sample is not pure NaCl.** The deviation indicates the presence of an impurity that dilutes the Na content.

*Key move: a pure substance has a fixed, characteristic elemental composition. Any deviation from the theoretical value is diagnostic of impurity.*

**Example 3 — Purity from precipitation experiment (Progress Check Q7):**
10.0 g of a powder labeled $\text{BaCl}_2$ is dissolved and treated with excess $\text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4$. The filtered, dried $\text{BaSO}_4$ precipitate weighs 9.32 g.

$$n_{\text{BaSO}_4} = \frac{9.32}{233.39} = 0.03995 \text{ mol}$$

Stoichiometry (1:1 ratio $\text{BaCl}_2 : \text{BaSO}_4$): $n_{\text{BaCl}_2} = 0.03995$ mol

$$m_{\text{BaCl}_2} = 0.03995 \times 208.23 = 8.32 \text{ g}$$

$$\% \text{ purity} = \frac{8.32}{10.0} \times 100 = \mathbf{83.2\%}$$

*Key move: this experiment directly answers "Is the $\text{BaCl}_2$ pure?" It cannot answer questions about $\text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4$ purity (excess was added) or $\text{BaSO}_4$ solubility (insoluble by design).*

**Example 4 — Molar mass from mass-before/mass-after experiment (Progress Check Q8):**
A student heats 5.40 g of metal M in air until it completely converts to MO. The product weighs 6.60 g.

$$\Delta m = 6.60 - 5.40 = 1.20 \text{ g O gained}$$

$$n_O = \frac{1.20}{16.00} = 0.0750 \text{ mol}$$

Since formula is MO (1:1 ratio): $n_M = 0.0750$ mol

$$M_M = \frac{5.40}{0.0750} = \mathbf{72.0 \text{ g/mol}} \rightarrow \text{Germanium (Ge)}$$

*Key move: this experiment answers "what is the molar mass of M?" It cannot answer questions about density or melting point — those require completely different measurements.*

**Example 5 — What data answers what question (Progress Check Q9):**
A 42.0 g sample containing only C and H has 36.0 g C and 6.0 g H.

$$n_C = \frac{36.0}{12.01} = 3.00 \text{ mol} \qquad n_H = \frac{6.0}{1.008} = 5.95 \approx 6.00 \text{ mol}$$

Ratio = 3 : 6 = 1 : 2 → **Empirical formula = $\text{CH}_2$**

Can this data answer other questions?
- Volume? **No** — need density.
- Molar mass? **No** — empirical formula only gives the ratio, not the actual molecular mass.
- Chemical stability? **No** — requires different experiments.
- Empirical formula? **Yes** — this is exactly what mass % data gives you.

## Topic 1.5: Electron Configuration

![[aufbau_orbital_filling.svg|697]]

**Example 1 — Full configurations:**
- Na ($Z = 11$): $1s^2\ 2s^2\ 2p^6\ 3s^1$ → $\mathbf{[\text{Ne}]\ 3s^1}$
- Fe ($Z = 26$): $[\text{Ar}]\ 3d^6\ 4s^2$
- Cr ($Z = 24$): $\mathbf{[\text{Ar}]\ 3d^5\ 4s^1}$ (exception — half-filled d)
- Cu ($Z = 29$): $\mathbf{[\text{Ar}]\ 3d^{10}\ 4s^1}$ (exception — full d)

**Example 2 — Ion configurations:**
- $\text{Fe}^{2+}$: Remove 2 from $4s$ first → $[\text{Ar}]\ \mathbf{3d^6}$ (NOT $[\text{Ar}]\ 3d^4\ 4s^2$)
- $\text{Fe}^{3+}$: Remove 2 from $4s$, then 1 from $3d$ → $[\text{Ar}]\ \mathbf{3d^5}$
- $\text{Cl}^-$: Add 1 to $3p$ → $[\text{Ne}]\ 3s^2\ 3p^6$ = $\mathbf{[\text{Ar}]}$

**Example 3 — Hund's Rule orbital diagram:**
Nitrogen ($1s^2\ 2s^2\ 2p^3$):

$2p$: ↑ | ↑ | ↑ (correct — one electron per orbital, parallel spins)

NOT: ↑↓ | ↑ | \_\_ (incorrect — violates Hund's rule)

**Example 4 — Counting unpaired electrons (Progress Check Q13):**
Configuration: $1s^2\ 2s^2\ 2p^6\ 3s^2\ 3p^6$ (Argon, $Z = 18$)

Every subshell is completely filled: $s^2$ = paired, $p^6$ = all three orbitals paired. → **0 unpaired electrons**

*Key move: Hund's rule only applies when filling an incomplete subshell. Do not redistribute electrons from a filled subshell. A completely filled noble-gas configuration always has 0 unpaired electrons.*

**Example 5 — Orbital diagram for oxygen (Progress Check Q14):**
O ($Z = 8$): $1s^2\ 2s^2\ 2p^4$

$2p$ has 4 electrons across 3 orbitals. By Hund's rule: fill each singly first, then pair.

$2p$: ↑↓ | ↑ | ↑ → one paired orbital + two singly occupied → **2 unpaired electrons**

Wrong: ↑↓ | ↑↓ | \_\_ (pairs first before filling third orbital = violates Hund's rule)

*Key move: for oxygen's $2p^4$, place 1 electron in each of the 3 orbitals first, then add the 4th to make the first one paired. The result is ↑↓ | ↑ | ↑ — two unpaired electrons.*

## Topic 1.6: Photoelectron Spectroscopy

![[pes_spectrum_nitrogen.svg|697]]

**Example 1 — Identify the element:**
PES spectrum shows peaks with relative heights: 2 (highest binding energy), 2, 3 (lowest binding energy).

Total electrons = 2 + 2 + 3 = 7 → **Nitrogen (N)**

Assignment: peak 1 (height 2) = $1s^2$, peak 2 (height 2) = $2s^2$, peak 3 (height 3) = $2p^3$

**Example 2 — Compare two elements:**
Mg and Al both show a $1s$ peak and $2s$ peak. Al's peaks are at slightly higher binding energies because Al has one more proton ($Z_{eff}$ is higher), pulling all electrons more tightly.

**Example 3 — AP 2019 MCQ style:**
An element's PES spectrum shows four peaks with heights 2 : 2 : 6 : 2.

Total electrons = 12 → **Magnesium (Mg)**: $1s^2\ 2s^2\ 2p^6\ 3s^2$

The valence electrons ($3s^2$) are the rightmost peak at lowest binding energy.

**Example 4 — "Consistent with the electron shell model" (Progress Check Q17 — Boron):**
Boron ($Z = 5$): $1s^2\ 2s^2\ 2p^1$. PES shows three peaks (left to right): height 2, height 2, height 1.

- Peak 1 (leftmost, ~19 MJ/mol, height 2) = $1s^2$ ✓
- Peak 2 (~1.4 MJ/mol, height 2) = $2s^2$ ✓
- Peak 3 (rightmost, ~0.8 MJ/mol, height 1) = $2p^1$ ✓

The statement "the spectrum shows a single electron in the $2p$ subshell" is consistent — the rightmost peak has height 1, directly showing one electron in $2p$. This distinguishes B from C (which would show height = 2 for $2p^2$).

*Key move: map left → right to $1s$, $2s$, $2p$, etc., then check that each peak's height matches the electron count in that subshell.*

**Example 5 — Carbon PES (Progress Check Q18):**
Carbon ($Z = 6$): $1s^2\ 2s^2\ 2p^2$. Three peaks, all with height 2 (2 electrons each in $1s$, $2s$, $2p$).

Correct statement: "The spectrum shows equal numbers of electrons in the three occupied subshells." (2 each)

Wrong statements to eliminate:
- "4 electrons in the inner shell" → inner shell = $n=1$ = only $1s^2$ = 2 electrons, not 4.
- "All valence electrons have the same binding energy" → $2s$ and $2p$ are at different binding energies (separate peaks).
- "More electrons in inner shell than outer" → inner ($n=1$) = 2 electrons; outer ($n=2$) = 4 electrons. Outer > inner for C.

## Topic 1.7: Periodic Trends

![[periodic_trends.svg|697]]

**Example 1 — Ranking atomic radii:**
Rank in order of increasing atomic radius: Na, Mg, K, Ca.

Same period (Na, Mg): $Z_{eff}$ increases left to right → Na > Mg in radius.
Same group (Na vs K, Mg vs Ca): more shells down → K > Na, Ca > Mg.

Order (smallest → largest): $\mathbf{\text{Mg} < \text{Na} < \text{Ca} < \text{K}}$

**Example 2 — IE exception (AP MCQ classic):**
Why is the first ionization energy of oxygen lower than that of nitrogen?

- Nitrogen: $2p^3$ — each $2p$ orbital has one electron (no repulsion).
- Oxygen: $2p^4$ — one $2p$ orbital has a paired electron. The electron–electron repulsion in that orbital makes one electron easier to remove.

IE order: **N > O** despite O being further right on the periodic table.

**Example 3 — Isoelectronic series:**
Rank $\text{Na}^+$, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$, $\text{Al}^{3+}$, Ne in order of increasing radius (all have 10 electrons).

Higher nuclear charge → stronger pull → smaller radius. $Z$: Ne = 10, $\text{Na}^+$ = 11, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ = 12, $\text{Al}^{3+}$ = 13.

Order (smallest → largest): $\mathbf{\text{Al}^{3+} < \text{Mg}^{2+} < \text{Na}^+ < \text{Ne}}$

**Example 4 — Coulomb's law applied to lattice energy:**
Why does MgO have a much higher melting point than NaCl?

- MgO: $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ (charge $+2$, radius 72 pm) and $\text{O}^{2-}$ (charge $-2$, radius 140 pm).
- NaCl: $\text{Na}^+$ (charge $+1$, radius 102 pm) and $\text{Cl}^-$ (charge $-1$, radius 181 pm).

By Coulomb's law: MgO has $4\times$ the charge product ($2 \times 2$ vs $1 \times 1$) and a shorter interionic distance → much stronger attraction → much higher melting point (2852°C vs 801°C).

**Example 5 — Cross-period and cross-group comparison (Progress Check Q20):**
Why is the atomic radius of K greater than Br?

K ($Z = 19$): $[\text{Ar}]\ 4s^1$; Br ($Z = 35$): $[\text{Ar}]\ 3d^{10}\ 4s^2\ 4p^5$.

Both have valence electrons in $n = 4$, so principal quantum number is NOT the deciding factor here. The key is $Z_{eff}$: Br has 35 protons vs K's 19, with similar core electron shielding → Br's higher $Z_{eff}$ pulls valence electrons in much more tightly → smaller radius.

*Key move: when two elements share the same valence shell ($n$), $Z_{eff}$ is always the deciding factor for radius comparisons.*

**Example 6 — Down-a-group IE comparison (Progress Check Q22):**
Why does Rb lose an electron more easily than Li?

Li: $2s^1$ ($n = 2$); Rb: $5s^1$ ($n = 5$). Rb's valence electron is in the 5th principal energy level — vastly farther from the nucleus. By Coulomb's law ($F \propto 1/r^2$), the much greater distance in Rb makes its electron far easier to remove despite Rb's higher nuclear charge.

*Key move: do not be fooled by "more protons = harder to remove." The distance effect ($r^2$ in the denominator) dominates over charge ($Z$) when going down a group.*

## Topic 1.8: Valence Electrons and Ionic Compounds

![[coulombs_law_ionic.svg|697]]

**Example 1 — Predict ionic charges:**
- Na (Group 1): loses $1e^-$ → $\text{Na}^+$
- Ca (Group 2): loses $2e^-$ → $\text{Ca}^{2+}$
- Al (Group 13): loses $3e^-$ → $\text{Al}^{3+}$
- O (Group 16): gains $2e^-$ → $\text{O}^{2-}$
- Cl (Group 17): gains $1e^-$ → $\text{Cl}^-$

**Example 2 — Write ionic formula from charges:**
$\text{Al}^{3+}$ and $\text{O}^{2-}$: need to balance charges → $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ (total $6+$ and $6-$ balance).

**Example 3 — Lattice energy trend:**
LiF vs CsI: LiF has much smaller ions ($\text{Li}^+$ and $\text{F}^-$) with the same charges ($+1$ and $-1$). Smaller distance → larger $F$ by Coulomb's law → LiF has a much higher lattice energy and higher melting point.

**Example 4 — Same-group stoichiometry prediction (Progress Check Q23):**
Rb reacts with O in a 2:1 ratio forming $\text{Rb}_2\text{O}$. Which element also reacts with O in a 2:1 ratio?

Rb is Group 1 → $+1$ charge. $\text{O}^{2-}$ is $-2$. Formula: $2(+1) + 1(-2) = 0$ → $\text{Rb}_2\text{O}$ (2:1 ratio).

Any Group 1 element forms a $+1$ ion → same 2:1 ratio. Cs is in Group 1 (same group as Rb) → $\text{Cs}_2\text{O}$.

Eliminated:
- Sr (Group 2): $+2$ → SrO (1:1 ratio) ✗
- S (Group 16): nonmetal, forms anion ✗
- Br (Group 17): nonmetal ✗

**Answer: Cs** — same group = same valence electrons = same ionic charge = same stoichiometric ratio.

*Key move: same group → same ionic charge → same formula type. Period number is irrelevant; group number is everything.*

**Example 5 — Ionic charge from configuration (Progress Check Q24):**
Al ($Z = 13$): $[\text{Ne}]\ 3s^2\ 3p^1$ → 3 valence electrons → loses all 3 → $\text{Al}^{3+}$ → $[\text{Ne}]$ noble gas configuration.

The charge equals the number of valence electrons for Group 1–3 metals. "Three valence electrons" is the correct and complete justification.

*Key move: the ionic charge for main-group metals = number of valence electrons = group number. Do not confuse unpaired electrons (1 for Al's $3p^1$) with valence electrons (3 total for Al).*
