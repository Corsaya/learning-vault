## Q1. HCl + NH₃ reaction: mixture temperature rises 24°C → 28°C — exo or endothermic?

The temperature of the reaction mixture increased from $24\ °\text{C}$ to $28\ °\text{C}$. When the surroundings (the mixture) gain thermal energy, the reaction itself must have released that energy.

**Answer: C — It is an exothermic process, because energy is released by the reaction and is gained by the reaction mixture.**

In an exothermic reaction, the chemical system releases energy to its surroundings. The rise in mixture temperature from $24\ °\text{C}$ to $28\ °\text{C}$ is direct evidence that the surroundings gained thermal energy — meaning the system (the reaction) released it. Energy flows: system → surroundings → measurable temperature increase.

> **Trap:** Choosing A (endothermic, energy released by reaction). The word "released" is correct, but pairing it with "endothermic" is contradictory — endothermic means the reaction absorbs energy from surroundings. A temperature increase always signals exothermic; a temperature decrease always signals endothermic.

## Q2. NaOH(s) dissolved in water — temperature of water increases — what is accurate?

Temperature of water increases → water absorbs heat energy → the dissolution process released that heat to the water. A process that releases heat to surroundings is exothermic.

**Answer: C — It is an exothermic process because heat energy is absorbed by the water as the NaOH(s) dissolves in it.**

The dissolution of $\text{NaOH}(s)$ releases energy to the surrounding water (the surroundings), causing the water temperature to rise. This identifies the process as exothermic. From the water's perspective, it absorbs the heat released by the dissolving solid.

> **Trap:** Choosing A (endothermic, heat absorbed by water). The wording trips students: "absorbed by water" is true, but "endothermic" is wrong. Endothermic means the reaction absorbs heat — here the reaction releases heat that the water then absorbs. The classification (endo/exo) is from the reaction's perspective, not the surroundings'.

## Q3. Hand warmer ad claims it "draws out body's own heat" — accurate claim?

The ad claims the reaction is endothermic (absorbs heat from hands). If heat flowed from hands into the hand warmer via an endothermic reaction, the hands would lose thermal energy → hands feel colder, not warmer. The warming sensation actually requires an exothermic reaction releasing heat to the hands.

**Answer: A — The advertisement's claim is inaccurate because heat flowing from the hands to the warmer would only happen if the chemical reaction was endothermic, which would cause the hands to feel colder.**

Heat spontaneously flows from high temperature to low temperature. For a hand warmer to draw heat from the hands, it would need to be cooler than the hands — consistent with an endothermic reaction that absorbs heat from the surroundings. However, an endothermic process would cool the hands, not warm them. A warming hand warmer must be exothermic, releasing heat to the hands. The advertisement's mechanistic claim is backward.

> **Trap:** Choosing D (claim accurate, exothermic, hands feel warmer). D gets the final outcome right (warming) but the mechanism wrong — if the warmer is exothermic, heat flows from the warmer to the hands, not from the hands to the warmer as the advertisement states.

## Q4. Hot Zn pellet (50.0°C) dropped into cooler water (27.8°C) — why does water temperature increase?

The Zn pellet is hotter than the water. Heat flows from hot to cold via molecular collisions at the Zn surface. Collisions between water molecules and the Zn surface transfer kinetic energy from the faster-moving Zn atoms to the slower water molecules, raising water temperature.

**Answer: B — Collisions between the water molecules and the surface of the Zn pellet will result in the transfer of energy, increasing the average kinetic energy of the water molecules.**

Thermal energy transfer occurs at the contact boundary through collisions. The higher-energy Zn atoms (at $50.0\ °\text{C}$) transfer kinetic energy to the lower-energy water molecules (at $27.8\ °\text{C}$) through repeated collisions at the solid–liquid interface. This increases the average KE (and thus temperature) of the water.

> **Trap:** Choosing A (metallic bonds break, releasing energy). Metallic bonds in Zn are not broken by simply placing the pellet in room-temperature water — that would require a chemical reaction (and Zn does not react with pure water under these conditions). The energy transfer here is purely physical (thermal conduction via collisions), not chemical bond-breaking.

## Q5. Two NaCl solutions at different temperatures combined — which prediction is correct?

When a cooler solution is mixed with a warmer solution, collisions transfer energy from higher-KE particles (warm) to lower-KE particles (cool). The cooler particles gain KE; the warmer particles lose KE. They reach thermal equilibrium at a temperature between the two initial temperatures.

**Answer: B — The average kinetic energy of the particles in the cooler solution will increase as they collide with the particles from the warmer solution.**

Energy transfers from the warmer, higher-KE particles to the cooler, lower-KE particles through molecular collisions until thermal equilibrium is reached. At equilibrium, all particles share the same average KE — which is intermediate between the two initial values.

> **Trap:** Choosing C or D (final KE lower than cooler solution, or higher than warmer solution). Neither extreme is physically possible in a simple mixing scenario with no external heat source or sink. The equilibrium KE must fall between the two initial values by conservation of energy.

## Q6. Two CH₃OH samples: Sample 1 (125.0 mL, 36.0°C) mixed with Sample 2 (250.0 mL, 18.0°C) — what happens?

Higher temperature = higher average kinetic energy. Sample 1 ($36.0\ °\text{C}$) has greater average KE than Sample 2 ($18.0\ °\text{C}$). Thermal energy transfers from higher-KE to lower-KE particles through molecular collisions — direction: Sample 1 → Sample 2.

**Answer: D — The $\text{CH}_3\text{OH}$ molecules from sample 1 transfer thermal energy to the $\text{CH}_3\text{OH}$ molecules from sample 2 through collisions because the average kinetic energy of the molecules in sample 1 is greater.**

Temperature is a measure of average kinetic energy. Sample 1's molecules have higher average KE ($36.0\ °\text{C}$) and transfer energy to Sample 2's molecules ($18.0\ °\text{C}$) through collisions until equilibrium is reached.

> **Trap:** Choosing B (Sample 2 transfers to Sample 1 because Sample 2 has more moles). The direction of heat transfer is always from higher KE (higher temperature) to lower KE (lower temperature) — it is never governed by the number of moles. More molecules does not mean more energy per molecule.

## Q7. Combustion of 1.00 g benzoic acid in 2.00 L water — estimate heat released from graph

From the graph: initial water temperature $\approx 22.2\ °\text{C}$, final temperature $\approx 25.0\ °\text{C}$.

$$\Delta T = 25.0 - 22.2 = 2.8\ °\text{C}$$

Mass of water: density $= 1.00\ \text{g/mL}$, volume $= 2.00\ \text{L} = 2000\ \text{mL}$:

$$m = 2000\ \text{g}$$

$$q = mc\Delta T = (2000\ \text{g})(4.2\ \text{J/g·}°\text{C})(2.8\ °\text{C}) = 23{,}520\ \text{J} \approx 24\ \text{kJ} \approx 25\ \text{kJ}$$

**Answer: B — 25 kJ**

Using $q = mc\Delta T$: $m = 2000\ \text{g}$, $c = 4.2\ \text{J/g·}°\text{C}$, $\Delta T \approx 2.8\ °\text{C}$ (read from graph). $q \approx 23{,}500\ \text{J} = 23.5\ \text{kJ} \approx 25\ \text{kJ}$.

> **Trap:** Choosing C (180 kJ) or D (210 kJ). These result from using the wrong $\Delta T$ (perhaps reading the final temperature as the full temperature rather than the change) or forgetting to read the baseline from the graph. Always read $\Delta T$ as final minus initial from the plateau regions, not the peak reading.

## Q8. Al (20.0 g, $\Delta T = -57°\text{C}$, $c = 0.9$) in H₂O (40.0 g, $\Delta T = +6°\text{C}$, $c = 4.2$) — equation for heat transfer

In an insulated system, heat lost by Al equals heat gained by water:

$$-q_{\text{Al}} = q_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}$$

Check the ratio:
$$q_{\text{Al}} = (20.0)(0.9)(-57) = -1{,}026\ \text{J}$$
$$q_{\text{H}_2\text{O}} = (40.0)(4.2)(+6) = +1{,}008\ \text{J}$$

$$-q_{\text{Al}} \approx q_{\text{H}_2\text{O}} \quad (\text{ratio} \approx 1{,}026/1{,}008 \approx 1)$$

**Answer: D — $-q_{\text{Al}} = q_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}$**

By conservation of energy in an insulated calorimeter, heat released by the hotter object equals heat absorbed by the cooler object. Numerically: $|q_{\text{Al}}| = 1{,}026\ \text{J}$ and $|q_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}| = 1{,}008\ \text{J}$ — essentially equal (small difference due to container absorption), confirming the $1:1$ ratio.

> **Trap:** Choosing C ($-q_{\text{Al}} = 2\,q_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}$). Doubling the water mass while keeping $\Delta T$ small does not double the energy. Always compute both $q$ values and compare; do not assume the ratio equals the mass ratio.

## Q9. NH₄NO₃ dissolved in water — $\Delta T$ of water to correct significant figures

From the thermometer diagrams: initial reading $= 22.0\ °\text{C}$, final reading $= 16.5\ °\text{C}$.

$$\Delta T = 16.5 - 22.0 = -5.5\ °\text{C}$$

Significant figures for subtraction: both readings have one decimal place → result has one decimal place → $-5.5\ °\text{C}$ (2 significant figures).

**Answer: C — $-5.5\ °\text{C}$**

Reading the thermometer to $0.5\ °\text{C}$ precision: $T_i = 22.0\ °\text{C}$, $T_f = 16.5\ °\text{C}$. By the subtraction rule, the result is reported to one decimal place: $\Delta T = -5.5\ °\text{C}$.

> **Trap:** Choosing A ($-5.500\ °\text{C}$, 4 sig figs) or B ($-5.50\ °\text{C}$, 3 sig figs). Thermometer readings from a diagram cannot support four or even three decimal places of precision. The subtraction rule limits the result to one decimal place — reporting extra digits implies false precision.

## Q10. Evaporation vs. condensation — how do energy changes compare per mole?

Evaporation and condensation are exact reverse processes. The energy absorbed when 1 mol of liquid water escapes to vapor (evaporation) equals the energy released when 1 mol of water vapor condenses to liquid — because $\Delta H_{\text{vap}} = -\Delta H_{\text{cond}}$ in magnitude.

**Answer: C — The amount of energy absorbed by one mole of water molecules as they escape the liquid is equal to the amount of energy released by one mole of water molecules as they come together to form a liquid.**

Evaporation is endothermic ($\Delta H_{\text{vap}} > 0$); condensation is exothermic ($\Delta H_{\text{cond}} < 0$). Since they are the exact reverse of each other, the magnitudes are equal: $|\Delta H_{\text{vap}}| = |\Delta H_{\text{cond}}|$ for the same substance at the same temperature.

> **Trap:** Choosing A (absorbed > released) or B (absorbed < released). Students sometimes confuse the direction of the process with the magnitude. The magnitude of the enthalpy change is identical for forward and reverse processes; only the sign (energy in vs. out) differs.

## Q11. How many moles of H₂S must condense to release the same heat as 1 mol H₂O condensing?

$$\Delta H_{\text{vap}}(\text{H}_2\text{S}) = 20\ \text{kJ/mol}, \quad \Delta H_{\text{vap}}(\text{H}_2\text{O}) = 40\ \text{kJ/mol}$$

Condensation releases the same energy magnitude as vaporization. 1 mol $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ condensing releases 40 kJ.

$$n(\text{H}_2\text{S}) = \frac{40\ \text{kJ}}{20\ \text{kJ/mol}} = 2\ \text{mol}$$

**Answer: C — 2 moles of $\text{H}_2\text{S}$**

Since $\Delta H_{\text{cond}}$ is the reverse of $\Delta H_{\text{vap}}$, condensing 1 mol $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ releases $40\ \text{kJ}$. Each mole of $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ condensing releases only $20\ \text{kJ}$, so twice as many moles are needed: $40\ \text{kJ} \div 20\ \text{kJ/mol} = 2\ \text{mol}$.

> **Trap:** Choosing B (1 mol) — equal moles would only work if the two substances had the same $\Delta H_{\text{vap}}$. $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ has stronger hydrogen bonding than $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ (which has only weak LDFs), so it has a higher $\Delta H_{\text{vap}}$ and releases more heat per mole upon condensation.

## Q12. Na heating curve: 0.50 mol Na undergoes solid → gas transition — heat absorbed?

The diagram shows closely packed Na atoms spreading to widely separated particles → vaporization. From the heating curve for 1.0 mol Na, the vaporization plateau width $\approx 97\ \text{kJ}$ (Na $\Delta H_{\text{vap}} \approx 97\ \text{kJ/mol}$).

For 0.50 mol:

$$q = 0.50\ \text{mol} \times 97\ \text{kJ/mol} \approx 48\ \text{kJ} \approx 49\ \text{kJ}$$

Vaporization is endothermic (heat absorbed) and increases potential energy between Na atoms.

**Answer: B — Approximately 49 kJ of heat are absorbed to overcome the attractive forces acting between Na atoms.**

Vaporization requires breaking the metallic attractive forces between Na atoms. Reading the vaporization plateau from the 1.0 mol heating curve gives $\approx 97\ \text{kJ}$; for half as many moles, half the energy is needed: $\approx 49\ \text{kJ}$. The process is endothermic — heat flows into the system.

> **Trap:** Choosing C or D (heat released). Vaporization always absorbs heat (endothermic). Only the reverse process — condensation — releases heat. Any phase transition from lower energy state (solid/liquid) to higher energy state (gas) is endothermic.

## Q13. $\text{Na}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_3$ + 4 NaOCl + 2 NaOH → 2 $\text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4$ + 4 NaCl + $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\Delta H° = -1236\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$ — heat when 2.00 mol NaOCl reacts

The balanced equation shows 4 mol NaOCl per mole of reaction. 2.00 mol NaOCl represents:

$$\text{mol rxn} = \frac{2.00\ \text{mol NaOCl}}{4\ \text{mol NaOCl/mol rxn}} = 0.500\ \text{mol rxn}$$

$$q = 0.500\ \text{mol rxn} \times (-1236\ \text{kJ/mol rxn}) = -618\ \text{kJ}$$

Negative $q$ → heat released.

**Answer: C — 618 kJ of heat is released.**

Scaling by the mole ratio: $2.00\ \text{mol NaOCl} \div 4 = 0.500\ \text{mol rxn}$. Multiplying by $|\Delta H°| = 1236\ \text{kJ/mol rxn}$ gives $618\ \text{kJ}$. The negative sign of $\Delta H°$ confirms heat is released (exothermic).

> **Trap:** Choosing D (2470 kJ released) — from multiplying $2.00 \times 1236$ directly, ignoring that 4 mol NaOCl are consumed per mole of reaction. Always divide the given moles by the stoichiometric coefficient from the balanced equation before multiplying by $\Delta H°$.

## Q14. Thermite: 2 Al + $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ → 2 Fe + $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$, $\Delta H = -850\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$ — heat from 108 g Al

$$n_{\text{Al}} = \frac{108\ \text{g}}{27.0\ \text{g/mol}} = 4.00\ \text{mol Al}$$

Stoichiometry: 2 mol Al per mol rxn:

$$\text{mol rxn} = \frac{4.00\ \text{mol}}{2} = 2.00\ \text{mol rxn}$$

$$q = 2.00 \times 850\ \text{kJ} = 1700\ \text{kJ released}$$

**Answer: D — 1700 kJ**

$108\ \text{g Al} \div 27.0\ \text{g/mol} = 4.00\ \text{mol Al}$. Since the equation consumes 2 mol Al per mol rxn, 4.00 mol Al corresponds to 2.00 mol rxn. Heat $= 2.00 \times 850 = 1700\ \text{kJ}$.

> **Trap:** Choosing C (850 kJ) — treating $\Delta H$ as the answer for any amount of Al rather than scaling. $\Delta H°$ is always per mole of reaction as written; you must scale by the actual moles of reaction that occur.

## Q15. $2\ \text{CO}(g) + \text{C}(g) \to \text{C}_3\text{O}_2(g)$, $\Delta H° = 127.3\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$ — heat when 1.00 mol CO reacts

Stoichiometry: 2 mol CO per mol rxn:

$$\text{mol rxn} = \frac{1.00\ \text{mol CO}}{2} = 0.500\ \text{mol rxn}$$

$$q = 0.500 \times 127.3\ \text{kJ} = 63.65\ \text{kJ} \approx 63.7\ \text{kJ absorbed}$$

**Answer: B — 63.7 kJ**

Endothermic reaction ($\Delta H° > 0$) → heat is absorbed. 1.00 mol CO is half the stoichiometric coefficient (2), so only half the enthalpy change applies: $0.500 \times 127.3 = 63.7\ \text{kJ}$.

> **Trap:** Choosing C (127.3 kJ) — using the full $\Delta H°$ value without adjusting for the actual moles of CO reacting. The reaction enthalpy applies to the equation as written (2 mol CO); using 1 mol CO requires halving it.

## Q16. $2\ \text{O}_3(g) \to 3\ \text{O}_2(g)$, $\Delta H°_{\text{rxn}} = -300\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$ — find O–O bond enthalpy in ozone

Each $\text{O}_3$ molecule has 2 oxygen-to-oxygen bonds. For 2 mol $\text{O}_3$: 4 bonds broken. Let $x$ = average bond enthalpy per O–O bond in ozone.

$$\Delta H_{\text{rxn}} = \Sigma(\text{bonds broken}) - \Sigma(\text{bonds formed})$$

$$-300 = 4x - 3(500)$$

$$4x = -300 + 1500 = 1200$$

$$x = 300\ \text{kJ/mol}$$

**Answer: C — 300 kJ/mol**

Breaking 4 O–O bonds in 2 mol $\text{O}_3$ costs $4x\ \text{kJ}$; forming 3 O=O bonds in 3 mol $\text{O}_2$ releases $3 \times 500 = 1500\ \text{kJ}$. Setting $4x - 1500 = -300$ gives $x = 300\ \text{kJ/mol}$.

> **Trap:** Choosing B (150 kJ/mol) — confusing the O–O single bond enthalpy (150 kJ/mol, given in the table) with the O–O bond in ozone. The bonds in ozone are intermediate between single and double (resonance), so the answer lies between 150 and 500 kJ/mol. The calculation yields 300 kJ/mol, not 150.

## Q17. $\text{CH}_4 + 2\ \text{O}_2 \to \text{CO}_2 + 2\ \text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\Delta H_{\text{rxn}} = -802\ \text{kJ/mol}$ — which expression gives O=O bond enthalpy?

$$\Delta H_{\text{rxn}} = \Sigma(\text{bonds broken}) - \Sigma(\text{bonds formed})$$

Bonds broken (reactants): 4 C–H in $\text{CH}_4$ + 2 O=O in $\text{O}_2$ (each $= x$)
Bonds formed (products): 2 C=O in $\text{CO}_2$ + 4 O–H in 2$\text{H}_2\text{O}$

$$-802 = [4(414) + 2x] - [2(799) + 4(464)]$$

Solving for $x$ (bond enthalpy per mol $\text{O}_2$):

$$2x = -802 + 2(799) + 4(464) - 4(414)$$

$$x = \frac{1}{2}\left[-802 + 2(799) + 4(464) - 4(414)\right]\ \text{kJ/mol}$$

**Answer: A — $\dfrac{1}{2}\left[-802 + 2(799) + 4(464) - 4(414)\right]\ \text{kJ/mol}$**

The expression isolates the O=O bond enthalpy by rearranging the bond enthalpy equation. The $\frac{1}{2}$ factor appears because 2 mol $\text{O}_2$ are consumed per mole of reaction, but we want the value per single mole of $\text{O}_2$.

> **Trap:** Choosing B or C (incorrect signs or coefficients on O–H/C–O terms). Remember: bonds broken are positive (energy in) and bonds formed are negative (energy out) in the expression $\Delta H = \Sigma(\text{broken}) - \Sigma(\text{formed})$. Confusing which terms are added vs. subtracted is the most common error.

## Q18. $\text{H}_2(g) + \text{Cl}_2(g) \to 2\ \text{HCl}(g)$ — $\Delta H_{\text{rxn}}$ from bond enthalpies

$$\Delta H_{\text{rxn}} = \Sigma(\text{bonds broken}) - \Sigma(\text{bonds formed})$$

Bonds broken: 1 H–H ($436$) + 1 Cl–Cl ($243$) $= 679\ \text{kJ}$

Bonds formed: 2 H–Cl ($2 \times 432$) $= 864\ \text{kJ}$

$$\Delta H_{\text{rxn}} = 679 - 864 = -185\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$$

**Answer: B — $-185\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$**

Breaking H–H and Cl–Cl bonds costs $679\ \text{kJ}$; forming 2 H–Cl bonds releases $864\ \text{kJ}$. Net: $679 - 864 = -185\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$.

> **Trap:** Choosing A ($-247\ \text{kJ/mol}$) — from incorrectly subtracting only one H–Cl bond ($436 + 243 - 432 = 247$) instead of two. The balanced equation produces 2 mol HCl, so 2 mol H–Cl bonds are formed and both must be included.

## Q19. $\text{CaCO}_3(s) \to \text{CaO}(s) + \text{CO}_2(g)$ — $\Delta H°_{\text{rxn}}$ from standard enthalpies of formation

$$\Delta H°_{\text{rxn}} = \Sigma \Delta H°_f(\text{products}) - \Sigma \Delta H°_f(\text{reactants})$$

$$= \left[\Delta H°_f(\text{CaO}) + \Delta H°_f(\text{CO}_2)\right] - \Delta H°_f(\text{CaCO}_3)$$

$$= \left[(-640) + (-390)\right] - (-1210)$$

$$= -1030 + 1210 = +180\ \text{kJ}$$

**Answer: B — $+180\ \text{kJ}$**

$\Delta H°_{\text{rxn}} = [(-640) + (-390)] - (-1210) = -1030 + 1210 = +180\ \text{kJ}$. The positive value confirms the decomposition of $\text{CaCO}_3$ is endothermic, consistent with limestone requiring high heat to decompose.

> **Trap:** Choosing A ($-180\ \text{kJ}$) — reversing the sign. This is the most common arithmetic error: students subtract correctly but then flip the final sign. A positive $\Delta H°_f(\text{reactants})$ subtracted from a less-negative products sum gives a positive $\Delta H°_{\text{rxn}}$.

## Q20. $\text{SO}_2(g) + \frac{1}{2}\ \text{O}_2(g) \to \text{SO}_3(g)$ — $\Delta H°$ from enthalpies of formation

$$\Delta H° = \Delta H°_f(\text{SO}_3) - \left[\Delta H°_f(\text{SO}_2) + \frac{1}{2}\Delta H°_f(\text{O}_2)\right]$$

$$= (-395.2) - \left[(-296.1) + \frac{1}{2}(0.0)\right]$$

$$= -395.2 - (-296.1) = -395.2 + 296.1 = -99.1\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$$

**Answer: B — $-99.1\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$**

$\Delta H° = (-395.2) - (-296.1) = -99.1\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$. The reaction is exothermic. Note $\Delta H°_f(\text{O}_2) = 0$ by definition (element in standard state), so it contributes nothing.

> **Trap:** Choosing A ($-591.3\ \text{kJ/mol}$) — adding the $\Delta H°_f$ values of SO₂ and SO₃ rather than subtracting. Always use $\Delta H° = \Sigma\Delta H°_f(\text{products}) - \Sigma\Delta H°_f(\text{reactants})$; the reactant values are subtracted, not added.

## Q21. $\text{CH}_4(g) + 2\ \text{O}_2(g) \to \text{CO}_2(g) + 2\ \text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$ — which expression gives $\Delta H°_{\text{rxn}}$?

$$\Delta H°_{\text{rxn}} = \Sigma\Delta H°_f(\text{products}) - \Sigma\Delta H°_f(\text{reactants})$$

$$= \left[\Delta H°_f(\text{CO}_2) + 2\,\Delta H°_f(\text{H}_2\text{O})\right] - \left[\Delta H°_f(\text{CH}_4) + 2\,\Delta H°_f(\text{O}_2)\right]$$

$$= \left[(-390) + 2(-240)\right] - \left[(-75) + 2(0)\right]$$

$$= (-390 - 480) - (-75) = -870 + 75 = -795\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$$

**Answer: C — $\Delta H°_{\text{rxn}} = \left[(-390\ \text{kJ/mol}) + 2(-240\ \text{kJ/mol})\right] - (-75\ \text{kJ/mol})$**

The expression correctly applies $\Sigma\Delta H°_f(\text{products}) - \Sigma\Delta H°_f(\text{reactants})$: products $\text{CO}_2$ and $2\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$ with appropriate coefficients, minus reactant $\text{CH}_4$ (O₂ contributes 0).

> **Trap:** Choosing A or B (reactants minus products, or wrong coefficients on $\text{H}_2\text{O}$). The direction matters: products minus reactants, never reactants minus products. Also, the coefficient $2$ on $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ must appear inside the expression because $\Delta H°_f$ is per mole of substance, not per mole of reaction.

## Q22. Hess's law: what additional data is needed to find $\Delta H°$ for $2\ \text{C(graphite)} + 3\ \text{H}_2(g) \to \text{C}_2\text{H}_6(g)$?

Given:
$$\text{Rxn 1: } \text{C}_2\text{H}_6(g) + \tfrac{7}{2}\,\text{O}_2(g) \to 2\,\text{CO}_2(g) + 3\,\text{H}_2\text{O}(l),\quad \Delta H° = -1560\ \text{kJ}$$
$$\text{Rxn 2 (given): } \text{C(graphite)} + \text{O}_2(g) \to \text{CO}_2(g),\quad \Delta H° = -394\ \text{kJ}$$

Strategy: reverse Rxn 1 to put $\text{C}_2\text{H}_6$ as a product; use $2\times$ Rxn 2 for the carbon. Still need $3\,\text{H}_2(g) + \tfrac{3}{2}\,\text{O}_2(g) \to 3\,\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$, which requires $\Delta H°_f(\text{H}_2\text{O}(l))$ — the combustion of $\text{H}_2$.

**Answer: C — $\Delta H°$ for $\text{H}_2(g) + \frac{1}{2}\,\text{O}_2(g) \to \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$**

Applying Hess's law:
$$[\text{Rxn 1 reversed}] + [2 \times \text{combustion of C}] + [3 \times \text{combustion of H}_2]$$

The combustion of $\text{H}_2$ is the missing piece. Once known, $\text{H}_2$ atoms in the target equation can be accounted for.

> **Trap:** Choosing A ($\Delta H°_f$ for $\text{N}_2(l)$) or D (irrelevant species). Hess's law requires that all atoms in the target equation be traceable through the provided reactions. Nitrogen appears nowhere in the target reaction; the missing link is always $\text{H}_2$ combustion (to form $\text{H}_2\text{O}$).

## Q23. Hess's law: find Reaction 2 such that Rxn 1 + Rxn 2 = Overall

$$\text{Rxn 1: } 2\,\text{O}_3(g) \to 3\,\text{O}_2(g)$$
$$\text{Overall: } 2\,\text{O}_3(g) + 2\,\text{NO}(g) \to 2\,\text{NO}_2(g) + 2\,\text{O}_2(g)$$

$$\text{Rxn 2} = \text{Overall} - \text{Rxn 1}$$

Subtract Rxn 1 from both sides of Overall:

$$[2\,\text{O}_3 + 2\,\text{NO} \to 2\,\text{NO}_2 + 2\,\text{O}_2] - [2\,\text{O}_3 \to 3\,\text{O}_2]$$

Cancel $2\,\text{O}_3$; collect $\text{O}_2$: $2\,\text{O}_2 - 3\,\text{O}_2 = -\text{O}_2$ (move to left):

$$\text{Rxn 2: } 2\,\text{NO}(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \to 2\,\text{NO}_2(g)$$

**Answer: C — $2\,\text{NO}(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \to 2\,\text{NO}_2(g)$**

Subtracting Rxn 1 from the overall reaction algebraically cancels the $2\,\text{O}_3$ on both sides and consolidates the $\text{O}_2$ terms, leaving $2\,\text{NO}(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \to 2\,\text{NO}_2(g)$.

> **Trap:** Choosing A [$\text{NO}(g) + \text{O}(g) \to \text{NO}_2(g)$] — correct stoichiometry conceptually but introduces atomic oxygen ($\text{O}(g)$) that does not cancel when added to Rxn 1. Always verify the proposed Rxn 2 actually reproduces the overall equation when combined with Rxn 1.

## Q24. Hess's law: what additional data is needed for $2\,\text{HNO}_3(l) \to \text{N}_2\text{O}_5(g) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$?

Given:
$$\text{H}_2(g) + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\text{O}_2(g) \to \text{H}_2\text{O}(l),\quad \Delta H° = -286\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$$
$$\text{N}_2(g) + 3\,\text{O}_2(g) + \text{H}_2(g) \to 2\,\text{HNO}_3(l),\quad \Delta H° = -348\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$$

Reverse the $\text{HNO}_3$ reaction → get $2\,\text{HNO}_3$ on left. Use the $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ reaction → get $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ on right. What remains after algebraic cancellation is:

$$\text{N}_2(g) + \tfrac{5}{2}\,\text{O}_2(g) \to \text{N}_2\text{O}_5(g)$$

This requires $\Delta H°_f(\text{N}_2\text{O}_5(g))$, which is not in the given table.

**Answer: B — $\Delta H°_f$ for $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5(g)$**

After combining the reversed $\text{HNO}_3$ formation with the $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ formation, the remaining unresolved step is the formation of $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5(g)$ from its elements. This standard enthalpy of formation is the missing piece.

> **Trap:** Choosing A ($\Delta H°_f$ for $\text{N}_2(l)$) or C ($\Delta H°_f$ for $\text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$). $\text{N}_2$ is an element in its standard state so $\Delta H°_f = 0$ and provides no additional information. $\text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$ vs. $(l)$ would shift the answer but the key missing data is the $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$ formation enthalpy, which cannot be derived from any combination of the given two reactions.

## Summary of Key Patterns — Unit 6 MCQ

| Pattern | Rule | Questions |
|---|---|---|
| Exo vs. endothermic from temperature change | Temperature increase → surroundings gained heat → system released heat → **exothermic**; decrease → **endothermic** | Q1, Q2 |
| Direction of "endo/exo" labeling | Classification is from the **reaction's** perspective, not the surroundings; rising $T$ in surroundings = exothermic reaction | Q2, Q3 |
| Thermal energy transfer mechanism | Heat flows via molecular collisions from higher-KE (higher-$T$) particles to lower-KE; amount never determined by mass ratio | Q4, Q5, Q6 |
| $q = mc\Delta T$ calorimetry | Read $\Delta T$ from graph (final plateau minus initial plateau); convert L → g using density; watch units J → kJ | Q7 |
| Energy conservation in calorimeter | $-q_{\text{hot}} = q_{\text{cold}}$; verify numerically; ratio ≠ mass ratio in general | Q8 |
| Significant figures in $\Delta T$ | Subtraction rule: result limited to fewest decimal places; reading thermometer diagrams limits precision | Q9 |
| Vaporization vs. condensation energy | Reverse processes: $|\Delta H_{\text{vap}}| = |\Delta H_{\text{cond}}|$; use ratio if $\Delta H_{\text{vap}}$ values differ | Q10, Q11 |
| Heating curve phase transitions | Plateau width $\propto$ moles; vaporization endothermic (heat absorbed); scale for fraction of mole | Q12 |
| Stoichiometric scaling of $\Delta H$ | Divide given moles by stoichiometric coefficient → mol rxn; multiply by $|\Delta H°|$; check sign separately | Q13, Q14, Q15 |
| Bond enthalpy: $\Delta H = \Sigma(\text{broken}) - \Sigma(\text{formed})$ | Broken bonds cost energy (reactants); formed bonds release energy (products); watch coefficients | Q16, Q17, Q18 |
| Standard enthalpy of formation: $\Delta H°_{\text{rxn}} = \Sigma\Delta H°_f(\text{prod}) - \Sigma\Delta H°_f(\text{react})$ | Elements in standard state have $\Delta H°_f = 0$; apply stoichiometric coefficients; products minus reactants | Q19, Q20, Q21 |
| Hess's law — missing reaction | Algebraically subtract known reactions from the overall to isolate the unknown; identify which element/compound has no formation path yet | Q22, Q23, Q24 |
