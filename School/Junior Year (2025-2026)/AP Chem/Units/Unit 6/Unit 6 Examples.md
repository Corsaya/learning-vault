## Topic 6.1: Endothermic and Exothermic Processes

**Example 1 — Identifying exo/endothermic from temperature change (Krug 6.1):**
When $\text{HCl}(aq)$ is mixed with $\text{NH}_3(aq)$, the temperature of the reaction mixture rises from $24\ °\text{C}$ to $28\ °\text{C}$. Is this reaction exothermic or endothermic?

Step 1: Determine what the temperature change tells us.
The solution (surroundings) gained thermal energy → temperature increased.

Step 2: Apply the system/surroundings relationship.
If surroundings gained energy, the **system** (reaction) released energy.

**Answer: Exothermic.** The reaction released energy to the surroundings. $\Delta H < 0$.

*Key move: A temperature increase in the surroundings always signals an exothermic reaction. The system released heat; the surroundings absorbed it.*

> Trap: Choosing "endothermic because the reaction released energy." The word "released" is correct, but exothermic means the reaction releases energy — not absorbs it. Release = exothermic.

---

**Example 2 — The perspective trap: dissolution of NaOH (AP exam style):**
$\text{NaOH}(s)$ is dissolved in water. The water temperature increases. A student says: "The process is endothermic because the water absorbed heat energy." Evaluate this claim.

Step 1: Identify what absorbed and what released.
The water (surroundings) absorbed heat. The dissolution process (system) released heat.

Step 2: Apply the definition.
Endothermic = system absorbs. Exothermic = system releases.

**Answer: The claim is incorrect.** The process is **exothermic** because the system (dissolution of $\text{NaOH}$) released heat that was absorbed by the water. The fact that water absorbed heat confirms the reaction was exothermic.

*Key move: Always classify endo/exo from the reaction's perspective. "Water absorbed heat" means the reaction released it → exothermic.*

---

**Example 3 — Hand warmer advertisement analysis (Progress Check Q3 type):**
An advertisement for a hand warmer states: "Our product draws out your body's own heat to keep you warm." Is this mechanistically accurate?

Step 1: Interpret the claim.
"Draws out your body's heat" means heat flows **from** the body **into** the hand warmer. This describes an endothermic process in the warmer.

Step 2: Assess the consequence.
If the warmer absorbs heat from your body, your body loses thermal energy → hands feel **colder**.

Step 3: Identify the contradiction.
A hand warmer that cools your hands contradicts its purpose. A functioning hand warmer must be **exothermic** — releasing heat into the hands.

**Answer: The claim is inaccurate.** For the hands to feel warmer, heat must flow from the warmer into the hands. This requires an exothermic reaction in the warmer. An endothermic process would cool the hands, not warm them.

*Key move: For a device to warm something, it must release heat (exothermic). A device that absorbs heat from you makes you colder.*

---

**Example 4 — Identifying endo/exo from a thermochemical equation (AP exam style):**
$$2\ \text{NO}(g) \to \text{N}_2(g) + \text{O}_2(g), \quad \Delta H° = -180\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$$

(a) Is this reaction exothermic or endothermic?
(b) What is $\Delta H°$ for the reverse reaction?

(a) $\Delta H° = -180\ \text{kJ} < 0$ → the reaction releases energy → **exothermic**.

(b) Reversing: $\text{N}_2(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \to 2\ \text{NO}(g)$, $\Delta H° = +180\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$ (flip the sign).

**Answers: (a) Exothermic; (b) $+180\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$**

*Key move: Reversing a reaction multiplies $\Delta H°$ by $-1$. Exothermic forward → endothermic reverse, same magnitude.*

## Topic 6.2: Energy Diagrams

![[enthalpy_diagram_u6.svg|697]]
*Exothermic (left): products lower in energy. Endothermic (right): products higher. $E_a$ measured from reactants to peak.*

**Example 5 — Reading $\Delta H$ and $E_a$ from an energy diagram (Krug 6.2):**
An energy diagram shows reactants at $50\ \text{kJ}$, transition state at $130\ \text{kJ}$, and products at $30\ \text{kJ}$ (all relative to the same zero).

(a) Calculate $\Delta H$.
(b) Calculate $E_a$ for the forward reaction.
(c) Calculate $E_a$ for the reverse reaction.
(d) Is the reaction exothermic or endothermic?

(a) $\Delta H = E(\text{products}) - E(\text{reactants}) = 30 - 50 = -20\ \text{kJ/mol}$

(b) $E_a(\text{forward}) = E(\text{TS}) - E(\text{reactants}) = 130 - 50 = 80\ \text{kJ/mol}$

(c) $E_a(\text{reverse}) = E(\text{TS}) - E(\text{products}) = 130 - 30 = 100\ \text{kJ/mol}$

(d) $\Delta H < 0$ → **exothermic**. Products are lower in energy.

**Note:** $E_a(\text{reverse}) - E_a(\text{forward}) = |\Delta H|$. This is always true.

*Key move: Read each value as a height difference. $\Delta H$ = product level minus reactant level. $E_a$ = peak minus the level it's measured from (reactants for forward, products for reverse).*

> Trap: Reading $E_a$ as the peak height from zero, not from the correct baseline. $E_a$ is always the height of the hill above its starting point.

---

**Example 6 — Catalyst effect on an energy diagram (AP exam style):**
A reaction has $E_a = 80\ \text{kJ/mol}$ and $\Delta H° = -20\ \text{kJ/mol}$. A catalyst is added and lowers $E_a$ to $50\ \text{kJ/mol}$. What is the new $\Delta H°$?

Reasoning: A catalyst provides an alternative, lower-energy pathway. It does **not** change the energy levels of reactants or products. Only the transition state energy changes.

$\Delta H° = E(\text{products}) - E(\text{reactants})$ depends only on the product and reactant levels.

**Answer: $\Delta H° = -20\ \text{kJ/mol}$ — unchanged.** The catalyst lowers $E_a$ but leaves $\Delta H°$ identical.

*Key move: Catalyst lowers $E_a$; $\Delta H°$ is a state function and depends only on reactants and products — a catalyst cannot change it.*

---

**Example 7 — Multistep energy profile: counting TS, intermediates, steps (Krug 6.2):**
An energy diagram shows three humps and two valleys between reactants and products.

(a) How many transition states are present?
(b) How many intermediates are present?
(c) How many elementary steps are there?

(a) One transition state per hump: **3 transition states**.

(b) Intermediates occupy the valleys between humps: **2 intermediates**.

(c) Each hump = one elementary step: **3 steps**.

*Key move: Humps = TS = steps. Valleys between humps = intermediates. There is always one more TS than intermediates. An intermediate is at a local energy minimum; a TS is at a local energy maximum.*

## Topic 6.3: Heat Transfer and Thermal Equilibrium

**Example 8 — Mechanism of thermal energy transfer (Progress Check Q4 type):**
A zinc pellet at $50.0\ °\text{C}$ is placed in water at $27.8\ °\text{C}$. Explain at the molecular level why the water temperature increases.

The zinc atoms at $50.0\ °\text{C}$ have **higher average kinetic energy** than the water molecules at $27.8\ °\text{C}$. At the solid–liquid interface, collisions between the more energetic zinc surface atoms and the less energetic water molecules transfer kinetic energy from the zinc to the water. This increases the average KE of the water molecules, which corresponds to a temperature increase.

**Answer:** Through molecular collisions at the zinc–water interface, kinetic energy transfers from the higher-KE zinc atoms (hotter) to the lower-KE water molecules (cooler). The average KE of the water molecules increases → temperature rises.

> Trap: Saying "metallic bonds in Zn break, releasing energy." Metallic bonds in zinc are not broken by contact with room-temperature water. The energy transfer is purely physical (thermal conduction via collisions), not chemical.

*Key move: Thermal energy transfer at a boundary occurs through collisions. No bonds break. Direction: high KE → low KE (high T → low T).*

---

**Example 9 — Direction of heat flow from mass trap (Progress Check Q6 type):**
Sample 1 is $125.0\ \text{mL}$ of $\text{CH}_3\text{OH}$ at $36.0\ °\text{C}$. Sample 2 is $250.0\ \text{mL}$ at $18.0\ °\text{C}$. When mixed, in which direction does heat flow?

Sample 1 is at **higher temperature** → higher average kinetic energy per molecule.

Heat flows from higher KE (higher $T$) to lower KE (lower $T$): from Sample 1 to Sample 2.

**Answer:** Heat flows from Sample 1 ($36.0\ °\text{C}$) to Sample 2 ($18.0\ °\text{C}$). Sample 2 has more molecules, but direction is determined by temperature, not number of particles.

*Key move: Heat flows from high temperature to low temperature. The size or number of moles of a sample never determines the direction of heat flow.*

> Trap: "Sample 2 transfers heat to Sample 1 because Sample 2 has more particles." More particles ≠ higher energy per particle. Temperature (average KE) determines direction.

**Example 10 — Finding equilibrium temperature (thermal equilibrium calculation):**
$20.0\ \text{g}$ of aluminum at $80.0\ °\text{C}$ ($c_{\text{Al}} = 0.900\ \text{J/g·°C}$) is dropped into $40.0\ \text{g}$ of water at $20.0\ °\text{C}$ ($c_{\text{water}} = 4.18\ \text{J/g·°C}$). Find the final equilibrium temperature.

Step 1: Set up energy conservation.
$$q_{\text{Al}} + q_{\text{water}} = 0$$
$$(20.0)(0.900)(T_f - 80.0) + (40.0)(4.18)(T_f - 20.0) = 0$$

Step 2: Expand.
$$18.0\,T_f - 1440 + 167.2\,T_f - 3344 = 0$$
$$185.2\,T_f = 4784$$

Step 3: Solve.
$$T_f = \frac{4784}{185.2} = 25.8\ °\text{C}$$

**Answer: $T_f = 25.8\ °\text{C}$** — between the two initial temperatures, as expected.

*Key move: Set $q_{\text{hot}} + q_{\text{cool}} = 0$. Let $T_f$ be the unknown; expand and solve algebraically. Check: $T_f$ must lie between $T_i(\text{cool})$ and $T_i(\text{hot})$.*

**Example 11 — "What can/cannot be concluded" from final temperature (AP exam style):**
Two $\text{NaCl}(aq)$ solutions at different temperatures are combined. The colder solution was $12\ °\text{C}$; the warmer was $45\ °\text{C}$. Which of the following is a valid conclusion about the final mixture?

(A) Final temperature is lower than $12\ °\text{C}$ (B) Final temperature equals $45\ °\text{C}$ (C) Final temperature is between $12\ °\text{C}$ and $45\ °\text{C}$ (D) Final temperature depends on which solution has more moles.

**Answer: (C).**
By conservation of energy, the heat lost by the warmer solution equals the heat gained by the cooler. The equilibrium temperature must lie strictly between the two initial values.

What **cannot** be concluded: the exact final temperature (without knowing masses and specific heats). What **can** be concluded: $12\ °\text{C} < T_f < 45\ °\text{C}$.

*Key move: Qualitatively, the final temperature is always intermediate between the two initial values — it cannot exceed the hotter or fall below the cooler.*

## Topic 6.4: Heat Capacity and Calorimetry

![[calorimetry_u6.svg|697]]
*$q_{\text{rxn}} = -q_{\text{cal}}$: opposite signs because energy conserved. Exothermic reaction → solution warms; $q_{\text{rxn}} < 0$, $q_{\text{cal}} > 0$.*

**Example 12 — Basic $q = mc\Delta T$ (Krug 6.4):**
How much heat is needed to raise $150.0\ \text{g}$ of water from $22.0\ °\text{C}$ to $85.0\ °\text{C}$? ($c = 4.18\ \text{J/g·°C}$)

$$q = mc\Delta T = (150.0\ \text{g})(4.18\ \text{J/g·°C})(85.0 - 22.0)\ °\text{C}$$
$$= (150.0)(4.18)(63.0) = 39{,}501\ \text{J} \approx 39.5\ \text{kJ}$$

**Answer: $q = 39.5\ \text{kJ}$**

*Key move: $\Delta T = T_f - T_i$ (final minus initial). Units check: g × J/g·°C × °C = J.*

**Example 13 — Reading $\Delta T$ from a graph with significant figures (Progress Check Q7/Q9 type):**
A graph shows initial water temperature at $22.2\ °\text{C}$ and final temperature at $25.0\ °\text{C}$. The water volume is $2.00\ \text{L}$. Calculate the heat released by the combustion reaction.

Step 1: Find $\Delta T$.
$$\Delta T = 25.0 - 22.2 = 2.8\ °\text{C} \quad (\text{1 decimal place by subtraction rule})$$

Step 2: Convert volume to mass (density of water = $1.00\ \text{g/mL}$).
$$m = 2.00\ \text{L} \times \frac{1000\ \text{mL}}{\text{L}} \times \frac{1.00\ \text{g}}{\text{mL}} = 2000\ \text{g}$$

Step 3: Calculate $q$.
$$q_{\text{cal}} = (2000\ \text{g})(4.2\ \text{J/g·°C})(2.8\ °\text{C}) = 23{,}520\ \text{J} \approx 24\ \text{kJ}$$

Since $q_{\text{rxn}} = -q_{\text{cal}}$: combustion released $\approx 24\ \text{kJ}$.

**Answer: $\approx 25\ \text{kJ}$ released** (matches answer choice B in the progress check due to rounding with $c \approx 4.2$).

> Trap: Using the thermometer peak reading as the final temperature rather than the plateau. Always read the plateau regions (before and after reaction), not a transient spike.

*Key move: L × 1000 mL/L × 1.00 g/mL = g for water. Read $\Delta T$ as (final plateau) − (initial plateau) from graph.*

**Example 14 — Full calorimetry problem: finding $\Delta H$ of neutralization (Krug 6.4):**
$100.0\ \text{mL}$ of $1.00\ \text{M HCl}$ is mixed with $100.0\ \text{mL}$ of $1.00\ \text{M NaOH}$ in a coffee cup calorimeter. Temperature rises from $21.50\ °\text{C}$ to $28.42\ °\text{C}$. Assume density $= 1.00\ \text{g/mL}$ and $c = 4.18\ \text{J/g·°C}$ for the combined solution.

Step 1: Find mass of combined solution.
$$m = 200.0\ \text{mL} \times 1.00\ \text{g/mL} = 200.0\ \text{g}$$

Step 2: Calculate $q_{\text{cal}}$.
$$q_{\text{cal}} = (200.0)(4.18)(28.42 - 21.50) = (200.0)(4.18)(6.92) = 5785\ \text{J} = 5.785\ \text{kJ}$$

Step 3: Find $q_{\text{rxn}}$.
$$q_{\text{rxn}} = -q_{\text{cal}} = -5.785\ \text{kJ}$$

Step 4: Find moles of reaction (limiting: $1.00\ \text{M} \times 0.1000\ \text{L} = 0.100\ \text{mol}$).
$$\Delta H = \frac{q_{\text{rxn}}}{n} = \frac{-5.785\ \text{kJ}}{0.100\ \text{mol}} = -57.9\ \text{kJ/mol}$$

**Answer: $\Delta H_{\text{neutralization}} = -57.9\ \text{kJ/mol}$** (exothermic, consistent with rising temperature).

*Key move: Use mass of the total solution (both solutions combined) in $q = mc\Delta T$. Divide $q_{\text{rxn}}$ by moles of limiting reagent (not combined moles) to get $\Delta H$.*

**Example 15 — Bomb calorimeter with heat capacity $C$ (AP exam style):**
The combustion of $1.00\ \text{g}$ of glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$, $M = 180.2\ \text{g/mol}$) in a bomb calorimeter raises the temperature by $3.11\ °\text{C}$. The heat capacity of the bomb is $C = 14.9\ \text{kJ/°C}$. Calculate $\Delta H_{\text{combustion}}$ per mole.

Step 1: Find $q_{\text{cal}}$.
$$q_{\text{cal}} = C \times \Delta T = (14.9\ \text{kJ/°C})(3.11\ °\text{C}) = 46.3\ \text{kJ}$$

Step 2: Find $q_{\text{rxn}}$.
$$q_{\text{rxn}} = -46.3\ \text{kJ} \quad \text{(negative: exothermic combustion)}$$

Step 3: Find moles of glucose.
$$n = \frac{1.00\ \text{g}}{180.2\ \text{g/mol}} = 5.55 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}$$

Step 4: Calculate $\Delta H$ per mole.
$$\Delta H = \frac{-46.3\ \text{kJ}}{5.55 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}} = -8340\ \text{kJ/mol}$$

**Answer: $\Delta H_{\text{combustion}}(\text{glucose}) \approx -8340\ \text{kJ/mol}$**

*Key move: Bomb calorimeter uses $q = C\Delta T$ (capital $C$, whole-system heat capacity). Result divided by moles of substance burned gives molar enthalpy of combustion.*

> Trap: Using $q = mc\Delta T$ for a bomb calorimeter. The bomb is specified by its total heat capacity $C$ (J/°C or kJ/°C), not specific heat per gram.

**Example 16 — $\Delta T$ significant figures from thermometer readings (Progress Check Q9 type):**
A student dissolves $\text{NH}_4\text{NO}_3$ in water. The initial thermometer reading is $22.0\ °\text{C}$ and the final reading is $16.5\ °\text{C}$ (both read to $0.5\ °\text{C}$ precision). What is $\Delta T$ with correct significant figures?

$$\Delta T = 16.5 - 22.0 = -5.5\ °\text{C}$$

By the subtraction rule: the result has one decimal place (both readings have one decimal place).

**Answer: $\Delta T = -5.5\ °\text{C}$** — two significant figures.

> Trap: Reporting $-5.500\ °\text{C}$ (four sig figs) or $-5.50\ °\text{C}$ (three sig figs). A thermometer read to $0.5\ °\text{C}$ precision cannot support more than one decimal place in the result. Extra digits imply false precision.

*Key move: Subtraction of two measurements: result has same number of decimal places as the less precise measurement. Precision of instrument limits precision of $\Delta T$.*

## Topic 6.5: Energy of Phase Changes

![[heating_curve_u6.svg|697]]
*Slopes → $q = mc\Delta T$; plateaus → $q = n\Delta H$ at constant $T$. Vaporization plateau is much wider because $\Delta H_{\text{vap}} \gg \Delta H_{\text{fus}}$.*

**Example 17 — Identifying plateau vs. slope on heating curve (Krug 6.5):**
On a heating curve, a student sees a segment where heat is being added but the temperature does not change. What is happening, and which equation applies?

This is a **phase transition plateau** — either melting or boiling. Temperature is constant because the energy input is used to overcome intermolecular forces (increasing potential energy), not to increase the kinetic energy of particles.

Equation: $q = n\Delta H_{\text{fus}}$ (at melting) or $q = n\Delta H_{\text{vap}}$ (at boiling). Not $q = mc\Delta T$.

*Key move: Constant-temperature segment on heating curve = phase change = $q = n\Delta H$. Sloped segment = single phase warming = $q = mc\Delta T$.*

---

**Example 18 — Mole scaling for phase transitions (Progress Check Q12 type):**
From a heating curve for $1.00\ \text{mol}$ of $\text{Na}$, the vaporization plateau corresponds to $\approx 97\ \text{kJ}$. How much heat is absorbed when $0.50\ \text{mol}$ of $\text{Na}(l)$ vaporizes?

Step 1: Identify $\Delta H_{\text{vap}}$.
From the 1.00 mol curve: $\Delta H_{\text{vap}} = 97\ \text{kJ/mol}$.

Step 2: Scale to 0.50 mol.
$$q = n \times \Delta H_{\text{vap}} = 0.50\ \text{mol} \times 97\ \text{kJ/mol} = 48.5\ \text{kJ} \approx 49\ \text{kJ}$$

**Answer: $\approx 49\ \text{kJ}$ absorbed** (endothermic — vaporization always absorbs heat).

> Trap: Choosing "heat released" because "vaporization involves atoms spreading out." Vaporization is always endothermic — it requires energy to break the attractive forces between atoms/molecules. Only condensation (reverse) releases heat.

*Key move: For $n$ mol of phase change: $q = n\Delta H_{\text{vap or fus}}$. Moles proportional to heat — halving moles halves the heat.*

---

**Example 19 — Comparing vaporization energies across substances (Progress Check Q11 type):**
$\Delta H_{\text{vap}}(\text{H}_2\text{O}) = 40\ \text{kJ/mol}$ and $\Delta H_{\text{vap}}(\text{H}_2\text{S}) = 20\ \text{kJ/mol}$. How many moles of $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ must condense to release the same heat as $1.00\ \text{mol}$ $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ condensing?

Step 1: Heat released by 1.00 mol $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ condensing.
$|\Delta H_{\text{cond}}(\text{H}_2\text{O})| = 40\ \text{kJ/mol}$; for 1.00 mol: releases $40\ \text{kJ}$.

Step 2: Find moles of $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ to release $40\ \text{kJ}$.
$$n = \frac{40\ \text{kJ}}{20\ \text{kJ/mol}} = 2.00\ \text{mol}$$

**Answer: $2.00\ \text{mol}\ \text{H}_2\text{S}$** must condense.

Why is $\Delta H_{\text{vap}}(\text{H}_2\text{O}) > \Delta H_{\text{vap}}(\text{H}_2\text{S})$? Water has hydrogen bonding ($\text{H–O–H}$), a strong IMF. $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ has only weak London dispersion forces. More energy is needed to vaporize water.

*Key move: Set $n \cdot \Delta H_{\text{cond}}(\text{B}) = 1 \cdot \Delta H_{\text{cond}}(\text{A})$ and solve for $n$. The substance with weaker IMF (lower $\Delta H_{\text{vap}}$) requires more moles to release the same heat.*

**Example 20 — Total heat for a multi-segment process (AP exam style):**
Calculate the total heat required to convert $18.0\ \text{g}$ of ice at $-10.0\ °\text{C}$ to steam at $110.0\ °\text{C}$.
Given: $c_{\text{ice}} = 2.09\ \text{J/g·°C}$; $c_{\text{water}} = 4.18\ \text{J/g·°C}$; $c_{\text{steam}} = 2.01\ \text{J/g·°C}$; $\Delta H_{\text{fus}} = 6.01\ \text{kJ/mol}$; $\Delta H_{\text{vap}} = 40.7\ \text{kJ/mol}$; $M(\text{H}_2\text{O}) = 18.0\ \text{g/mol}$.

$$n = \frac{18.0\ \text{g}}{18.0\ \text{g/mol}} = 1.00\ \text{mol}$$

Segment 1: Ice warming, $-10.0 \to 0\ °\text{C}$
$$q_1 = (18.0)(2.09)(10.0) = 376\ \text{J} = 0.376\ \text{kJ}$$

Segment 2: Melting at $0\ °\text{C}$
$$q_2 = (1.00\ \text{mol})(6.01\ \text{kJ/mol}) = 6.01\ \text{kJ}$$

Segment 3: Liquid warming, $0 \to 100\ °\text{C}$
$$q_3 = (18.0)(4.18)(100.0) = 7524\ \text{J} = 7.52\ \text{kJ}$$

Segment 4: Vaporization at $100\ °\text{C}$
$$q_4 = (1.00\ \text{mol})(40.7\ \text{kJ/mol}) = 40.7\ \text{kJ}$$

Segment 5: Steam warming, $100 \to 110\ °\text{C}$
$$q_5 = (18.0)(2.01)(10.0) = 362\ \text{J} = 0.362\ \text{kJ}$$

$$q_{\text{total}} = 0.376 + 6.01 + 7.52 + 40.7 + 0.362 = 54.97 \approx 55.0\ \text{kJ}$$

**Answer: $q_{\text{total}} \approx 55.0\ \text{kJ}$**

*Key move: Five-step process — alternate between $q = mc\Delta T$ (sloped) and $q = n\Delta H$ (plateau). Vaporization ($40.7\ \text{kJ}$) dominates — always the largest term.*

## Topic 6.6: Introduction to Enthalpy of Reaction

**Example 21 — Stoichiometric scaling from moles of a non-coefficient-1 species (Progress Check Q13 type):**
$$\text{Na}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_3 + 4\ \text{NaOCl} + 2\ \text{NaOH} \to 2\ \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4 + 4\ \text{NaCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O}, \quad \Delta H° = -1236\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$$

How much heat is released when $2.00\ \text{mol NaOCl}$ reacts?

Step 1: Find mol rxn from NaOCl.
$$\text{mol rxn} = \frac{2.00\ \text{mol NaOCl}}{4\ \text{mol NaOCl/mol rxn}} = 0.500\ \text{mol rxn}$$

Step 2: Calculate heat.
$$q = 0.500\ \text{mol rxn} \times 1236\ \text{kJ/mol rxn} = 618\ \text{kJ released}$$

**Answer: $618\ \text{kJ}$ released** ($\Delta H° < 0$, exothermic).

> Trap: Multiplying $2.00 \times 1236 = 2472\ \text{kJ}$ without using the stoichiometric coefficient. The coefficient 4 for NaOCl means each mole of NaOCl represents only $\frac{1}{4}$ mol of the reaction.

*Key move: Always divide by the stoichiometric coefficient for the given species before multiplying by $|\Delta H°|$.*

**Example 22 — Scaling from mass of a reactant (Progress Check Q14 type):**
Thermite reaction: $2\ \text{Al}(s) + \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3(s) \to 2\ \text{Fe}(s) + \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3(s)$, $\Delta H° = -850\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$

How much heat is released when $108\ \text{g}$ of $\text{Al}$ reacts completely?

Step 1: Moles of Al.
$$n_{\text{Al}} = \frac{108\ \text{g}}{27.0\ \text{g/mol}} = 4.00\ \text{mol Al}$$

Step 2: Mol rxn (coefficient of Al = 2).
$$\text{mol rxn} = \frac{4.00}{2} = 2.00\ \text{mol rxn}$$

Step 3: Heat.
$$q = 2.00 \times 850 = 1700\ \text{kJ released}$$

**Answer: $1700\ \text{kJ}$**

*Key move: g → mol (divide by $M$) → mol rxn (divide by stoichiometric coefficient) → heat (multiply by $|\Delta H°|$). Three-step conversion.*

**Example 23 — Endothermic stoichiometry (Progress Check Q15 type):**
$$2\ \text{CO}(g) + \text{C}(g) \to \text{C}_3\text{O}_2(g), \quad \Delta H° = +127.3\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$$

How much heat is absorbed when $1.00\ \text{mol CO}$ reacts?

$$\text{mol rxn} = \frac{1.00\ \text{mol CO}}{2\ \text{mol CO/mol rxn}} = 0.500\ \text{mol rxn}$$

$$q = 0.500 \times 127.3 = 63.7\ \text{kJ absorbed}$$

**Answer: $63.7\ \text{kJ}$ absorbed** ($\Delta H° > 0$, endothermic).

*Key move: $\Delta H°$ applies to the equation as written (2 mol CO). Using 1 mol CO = half the reaction = half the enthalpy change.*

**Example 24 — FRQ-style: complete enthalpy of reaction justification (AP FRQ):**
A student mixes $50.0\ \text{mL}$ of $2.00\ \text{M HCl}$ with $50.0\ \text{mL}$ of $2.00\ \text{M NaOH}$ in a coffee cup calorimeter. The temperature rises from $23.00\ °\text{C}$ to $36.46\ °\text{C}$.

(a) Calculate $\Delta H°_{\text{neutralization}}$ in kJ/mol. Show all work.

$m_{\text{sol}} = 100.0\ \text{mL} \times 1.00\ \text{g/mL} = 100.0\ \text{g}$

$q_{\text{cal}} = (100.0\ \text{g})(4.18\ \text{J/g·°C})(36.46 - 23.00)\ °\text{C} = (100.0)(4.18)(13.46) = 5626\ \text{J} = 5.626\ \text{kJ}$

$q_{\text{rxn}} = -5.626\ \text{kJ}$

$n(\text{HCl}) = 0.0500\ \text{L} \times 2.00\ \text{mol/L} = 0.100\ \text{mol}$ (limiting reagent)

$$\Delta H° = \frac{-5.626\ \text{kJ}}{0.100\ \text{mol}} = -56.3\ \text{kJ/mol}$$

(b) Explain why the actual value (literature: $-57.9\ \text{kJ/mol}$) differs from the experimental value.

Some heat was transferred to the calorimeter walls rather than remaining in the solution. Additionally, the solution's specific heat capacity may differ slightly from $4.18\ \text{J/g·°C}$ (which assumes pure water). Both effects would reduce the measured $q_{\text{cal}}$ and make the calculated $\Delta H°$ less negative than the true value.

**FRQ-quality answer includes:** all given values stated, formula written before numbers substituted, units at every step, sign carried through, moles explicitly calculated, and a mechanistic explanation for error rather than just "human error."

## Topic 6.7: Bond Enthalpies

![[bond_enthalpy_u6.svg|697]]
*Bonds broken (red, energy in) minus bonds formed (green, energy out) = net $\Delta H$. Count bonds by type × balanced-equation coefficient.*

**Example 25 — Basic bond enthalpy calculation (Progress Check Q18 type):**
$$\text{H}_2(g) + \text{Cl}_2(g) \to 2\ \text{HCl}(g)$$
Bond enthalpies: H–H = $436\ \text{kJ/mol}$; Cl–Cl = $243\ \text{kJ/mol}$; H–Cl = $432\ \text{kJ/mol}$.

Step 1: Bonds broken.
$$\Sigma(\text{broken}) = 1(436) + 1(243) = 679\ \text{kJ}$$

Step 2: Bonds formed.
$$\Sigma(\text{formed}) = 2(432) = 864\ \text{kJ}$$

Step 3: $\Delta H_{\text{rxn}}$.
$$\Delta H = 679 - 864 = -185\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$$

**Answer: $\Delta H = -185\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$** (exothermic — more energy released forming bonds than absorbed breaking them).

> Trap: Forming only 1 mol HCl (from 1 H–Cl): $436 + 243 - 432 = 247\ \text{kJ}$. The balanced equation produces **2** mol HCl, so 2 H–Cl bonds form. Always count bonds by multiplying with coefficients.

*Key move: Count each bond type × its coefficient in the balanced equation. Apply $\Delta H = \Sigma(\text{broken}) - \Sigma(\text{formed})$.*

**Example 26 — Solving for an unknown bond enthalpy (Progress Check Q16 type):**
$$2\ \text{O}_3(g) \to 3\ \text{O}_2(g), \quad \Delta H° = -300\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$$
Bond enthalpy of O=O in $\text{O}_2$ = $500\ \text{kJ/mol}$. Each $\text{O}_3$ molecule has 2 O–O bonds. Let $x$ = bond enthalpy of the O–O bond in ozone.

Bonds broken: $2 \times 2 = 4$ O–O bonds in ozone: $4x$
Bonds formed: $3 \times 1 = 3$ O=O bonds in $\text{O}_2$: $3(500) = 1500\ \text{kJ}$

$$-300 = 4x - 1500$$
$$4x = 1200$$
$$x = 300\ \text{kJ/mol}$$

**Answer: O–O bond enthalpy in ozone $= 300\ \text{kJ/mol}$**

Sanity check: The O–O single bond is $\approx 150\ \text{kJ/mol}$ and the O=O double bond is $500\ \text{kJ/mol}$. Ozone has resonance (bond order $\approx 1.5$), so $300\ \text{kJ/mol}$ — midway — makes physical sense.

*Key move: Let the unknown bond enthalpy = $x$. Set up $\Delta H = \Sigma(\text{broken}) - \Sigma(\text{formed})$. Solve for $x$. Verify the answer lies in a physically reasonable range.*

**Example 27 — Setting up the expression to isolate one bond enthalpy (Progress Check Q17 type):**
$$\text{CH}_4(g) + 2\ \text{O}_2(g) \to \text{CO}_2(g) + 2\ \text{H}_2\text{O}(g), \quad \Delta H = -802\ \text{kJ/mol}$$
Bond enthalpies: C–H = $414$; C=O = $799$; O–H = $464$. Let $x$ = O=O bond enthalpy.

Bonds broken: $4(414) + 2x = 1656 + 2x$
Bonds formed: $2(799) + 4(464) = 1598 + 1856 = 3454$

$$-802 = (1656 + 2x) - 3454$$
$$2x = -802 + 3454 - 1656 = 996$$
$$x = 498\ \text{kJ/mol}$$

The expression to give $x$ directly:
$$x = \frac{1}{2}\left[-802 + 2(799) + 4(464) - 4(414)\right] = 498\ \text{kJ/mol}$$

**Answer: O=O bond enthalpy $\approx 498\ \text{kJ/mol}$** (literature value $\approx 498\ \text{kJ/mol}$).

*Key move: Rearrange $\Delta H = \Sigma(\text{broken}) - \Sigma(\text{formed})$ to isolate the unknown. The $\frac{1}{2}$ factor comes from dividing by the stoichiometric coefficient (2 O=O bonds) to get per-mole value.*

**Example 28 — Bond enthalpy limitation: approximation vs. formation enthalpy (AP exam style):**
Using bond enthalpies, $\Delta H_{\text{combustion}}(\text{CH}_4) \approx -802\ \text{kJ/mol}$. The value from standard enthalpies of formation is $-890\ \text{kJ/mol}$. Why do the values differ?

Bond enthalpy calculations use **average values** — the same C–H bond enthalpy is used whether it appears in methane, ethane, or propane, even though the true value varies slightly. The $-890\ \text{kJ/mol}$ is from precise calorimetric measurement of the specific compounds. Bond enthalpies are estimates; formation enthalpies are exact.

Additionally, bond enthalpies assume all species are in the **gas phase**. If products are liquid (e.g., $\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$ instead of $\text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$), condensation energy is not accounted for, increasing the discrepancy.

*Key move: Bond enthalpies are approximate (average, gas-phase only). Standard enthalpies of formation give exact $\Delta H°$ for the actual states of all species.*

## Topic 6.8: Enthalpy of Formation

![[formation_enthalpy_u6.svg|697]]
*Elements in standard state have $\Delta H°_f = 0$. Compounds can form exothermically (below elements line, negative $\Delta H°_f$) or endothermically (above, positive $\Delta H°_f$).*

**Example 29 — Basic $\Delta H°_{\text{rxn}}$ from formation enthalpies (Progress Check Q19 type):**
$$\text{CaCO}_3(s) \to \text{CaO}(s) + \text{CO}_2(g)$$
$\Delta H°_f$: $\text{CaCO}_3(s) = -1207\ \text{kJ/mol}$; $\text{CaO}(s) = -635\ \text{kJ/mol}$; $\text{CO}_2(g) = -394\ \text{kJ/mol}$.

$$\Delta H°_{\text{rxn}} = [\Delta H°_f(\text{CaO}) + \Delta H°_f(\text{CO}_2)] - \Delta H°_f(\text{CaCO}_3)$$
$$= [(-635) + (-394)] - (-1207) = -1029 + 1207 = +178\ \text{kJ/mol}$$

**Answer: $\Delta H°_{\text{rxn}} = +178\ \text{kJ/mol}$** — endothermic (decomposition requires heat; consistent with limestone needing very high temperatures to decompose).

> Trap: Getting $-178\ \text{kJ/mol}$. The most common arithmetic error: correctly computing $-1029$ for products but then adding $-1207$ rather than subtracting it. The reactant value is **subtracted**. Double-check signs.

*Key move: Apply $\Sigma\Delta H°_f(\text{prod}) - \Sigma\Delta H°_f(\text{react})$. Write products bracket minus reactants bracket, then evaluate. A negative reactant $\Delta H°_f$ subtracted becomes positive.*

**Example 30 — Role of elemental oxygen (Progress Check Q20 type):**
$$\text{SO}_2(g) + \tfrac{1}{2}\ \text{O}_2(g) \to \text{SO}_3(g)$$
$\Delta H°_f$: $\text{SO}_2(g) = -296.1\ \text{kJ/mol}$; $\text{SO}_3(g) = -395.2\ \text{kJ/mol}$; $\text{O}_2(g) = 0\ \text{kJ/mol}$ (element in standard state).

$$\Delta H° = \Delta H°_f(\text{SO}_3) - [\Delta H°_f(\text{SO}_2) + \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta H°_f(\text{O}_2)]$$
$$= (-395.2) - [(-296.1) + \tfrac{1}{2}(0)] = -395.2 + 296.1 = -99.1\ \text{kJ/mol}$$

**Answer: $\Delta H° = -99.1\ \text{kJ/mol}_{\text{rxn}}$** (exothermic).

> Trap: Assigning a nonzero $\Delta H°_f$ to $\text{O}_2(g)$. Oxygen gas is an element in its standard state, so $\Delta H°_f(\text{O}_2) = 0$ exactly. It contributes nothing to the sum.

*Key move: Elements in standard state contribute zero. Check each reactant and product: is it an element (standard state)? If yes, its $\Delta H°_f$ is 0.*

**Example 31 — Identifying the correct algebraic expression (Progress Check Q21 type):**
$$\text{CH}_4(g) + 2\ \text{O}_2(g) \to \text{CO}_2(g) + 2\ \text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$$
$\Delta H°_f$: $\text{CH}_4(g) = -75\ \text{kJ/mol}$; $\text{H}_2\text{O}(g) = -240\ \text{kJ/mol}$; $\text{CO}_2(g) = -390\ \text{kJ/mol}$; $\text{O}_2(g) = 0$.

The correct expression is:
$$\Delta H°_{\text{rxn}} = [\Delta H°_f(\text{CO}_2) + 2\,\Delta H°_f(\text{H}_2\text{O})] - [\Delta H°_f(\text{CH}_4) + 2\,\Delta H°_f(\text{O}_2)]$$
$$= [(-390) + 2(-240)] - [(-75) + 0] = [-390 - 480] - [-75] = -870 + 75 = -795\ \text{kJ/mol}$$

**Answer:** The correct expression applies products minus reactants with coefficients as multipliers. $\text{O}_2$ contributes zero.

> Trap: Forgetting the coefficient 2 on $\text{H}_2\text{O}$. $\Delta H°_f$ is per mole of compound; the balanced equation uses 2 mol $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, so the coefficient must appear.

*Key move: Write the brackets first (products bracket, then reactants bracket). Insert coefficients from the balanced equation as multipliers. Then substitute numbers.*

## Topic 6.9: Hess's Law

![[hess_law_u6.svg|697]]
*Cycle: $\Delta H$ is path-independent. Rules: reverse = flip sign; scale = multiply; add = sum and cancel intermediates.*

**Example 32 — Identifying the missing reaction (Progress Check Q22 type):**
Target: $2\ \text{C(graphite)} + 3\ \text{H}_2(g) \to \text{C}_2\text{H}_6(g)$

Given:
- Rxn 1: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_6(g) + \frac{7}{2}\ \text{O}_2(g) \to 2\ \text{CO}_2(g) + 3\ \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$, $\Delta H° = -1560\ \text{kJ}$
- Rxn 2: $\text{C(graphite)} + \text{O}_2(g) \to \text{CO}_2(g)$, $\Delta H° = -394\ \text{kJ}$

Strategy: Reverse Rxn 1 (puts $\text{C}_2\text{H}_6$ as product); use $2 \times$ Rxn 2 (provides 2 C atoms). Remaining gap: the target has $3\ \text{H}_2(g)$ on the left but Rxn 1 reversed has $3\ \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$ on the left. To convert $3\ \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$ to $3\ \text{H}_2(g)$, we need:
$$3\ \text{H}_2(g) + \tfrac{3}{2}\ \text{O}_2(g) \to 3\ \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$
This is $3\times$ the formation of water from elements. The missing data is $\Delta H°$ for $\text{H}_2(g) + \frac{1}{2}\ \text{O}_2(g) \to \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$.

**Answer:** The additional data needed is $\Delta H°$ for $\text{H}_2(g) + \frac{1}{2}\ \text{O}_2(g) \to \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$.

*Key move: Algebraically subtract all given (and adjusted) reactions from the target. The remaining species you cannot cancel identify the missing reaction.*

**Example 33 — Algebraic subtraction to find Rxn 2 (Progress Check Q23 type):**
Rxn 1: $2\ \text{O}_3(g) \to 3\ \text{O}_2(g)$

Overall: $2\ \text{O}_3(g) + 2\ \text{NO}(g) \to 2\ \text{NO}_2(g) + 2\ \text{O}_2(g)$

Find Rxn 2 such that Rxn 1 + Rxn 2 = Overall.

$$\text{Rxn 2} = \text{Overall} - \text{Rxn 1}$$

Subtract Rxn 1 from Overall:

LHS: $[2\ \text{O}_3 + 2\ \text{NO}] - [2\ \text{O}_3] = 2\ \text{NO}$

RHS: $[2\ \text{NO}_2 + 2\ \text{O}_2] - [3\ \text{O}_2] = 2\ \text{NO}_2 - \text{O}_2$

Move $-\text{O}_2$ to LHS as $+\text{O}_2$:

$$\text{Rxn 2}: 2\ \text{NO}(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \to 2\ \text{NO}_2(g)$$

Verify: Rxn 1 + Rxn 2 = $2\ \text{O}_3 + 2\ \text{NO} + \text{O}_2 \to 3\ \text{O}_2 + 2\ \text{NO}_2$. Cancel $\text{O}_2$ from both sides (1 on left, 3 on right → net 2 on right): $2\ \text{O}_3 + 2\ \text{NO} \to 2\ \text{O}_2 + 2\ \text{NO}_2$. ✓ Matches Overall.

**Answer: $2\ \text{NO}(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \to 2\ \text{NO}_2(g)$**

*Key move: Subtract the known reactions from the overall algebraically. Cancel species on both sides. Verify by adding the found Rxn 2 back to Rxn 1 and checking it reproduces the overall.*

**Example 34 — Three-reaction Hess's law combination (AP exam style):**
Find $\Delta H°$ for $\text{C(graphite)} + 2\ \text{H}_2(g) \to \text{CH}_4(g)$ given:

- Rxn A: $\text{CH}_4(g) + 2\ \text{O}_2(g) \to \text{CO}_2(g) + 2\ \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$, $\Delta H° = -890\ \text{kJ}$
- Rxn B: $\text{C(graphite)} + \text{O}_2(g) \to \text{CO}_2(g)$, $\Delta H° = -394\ \text{kJ}$
- Rxn C: $\text{H}_2(g) + \frac{1}{2}\ \text{O}_2(g) \to \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$, $\Delta H° = -286\ \text{kJ}$

Step 1: Target needs $\text{CH}_4$ as product → reverse Rxn A.
$$\text{Rxn A}': \text{CO}_2(g) + 2\ \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \to \text{CH}_4(g) + 2\ \text{O}_2(g), \quad \Delta H = +890\ \text{kJ}$$

Step 2: Keep Rxn B as-is (provides C → CO₂).
$$\text{Rxn B}: \text{C}(s) + \text{O}_2(g) \to \text{CO}_2(g), \quad \Delta H = -394\ \text{kJ}$$

Step 3: Need $2\ \text{H}_2$ → $2\ \text{H}_2\text{O}$: use $2 \times$ Rxn C.
$$2 \times \text{Rxn C}: 2\ \text{H}_2(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \to 2\ \text{H}_2\text{O}(l), \quad \Delta H = 2(-286) = -572\ \text{kJ}$$

Step 4: Add all three. Canceling:
- $\text{CO}_2$: produced in B, consumed in A' → cancels.
- $2\ \text{H}_2\text{O}$: produced in $2\times$C, consumed in A' → cancels.
- $2\ \text{O}_2$: produced in A', 1 consumed in B, 1 consumed in $2\times$C → net 0 → cancels.

Remaining: $\text{C}(s) + 2\ \text{H}_2(g) \to \text{CH}_4(g)$ ✓

$$\Delta H° = +890 + (-394) + (-572) = -76\ \text{kJ/mol}$$

**Answer: $\Delta H°_f(\text{CH}_4) = -76\ \text{kJ/mol}$**

> Note: The literature value is $-74.8\ \text{kJ/mol}$ — this is a formation enthalpy obtained by Hess's law, confirming that $\Delta H°_f$ values can be derived indirectly.

*Key move: For each species in the target reaction, identify which given reaction it appears in. Reverse and scale accordingly. Add $\Delta H$ values with the signs that match the manipulations.*

**Example 35 — Hess's law: what additional data is needed (Progress Check Q24 type):**
Target: $2\ \text{HNO}_3(l) \to \text{N}_2\text{O}_5(g) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$

Given:

This is the formation of $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$. The missing piece is $\Delta H°_f(\text{N}_2\text{O}_5(g))$.
