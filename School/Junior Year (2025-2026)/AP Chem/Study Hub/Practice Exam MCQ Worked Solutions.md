## Q1: Entropy Sign — Phase Change
*Topic: Unit 3 — ΔS from phase transitions (states of matter)*
**Answer: D**

Which process has a negative ΔS°? D: Cl₂(g) → Cl₂(l)

Gas → liquid decreases disorder (fewer microstates, molecules constrained). ΔS° is negative. All other choices either increase disorder or involve no phase change.

> [!check] Key move: gas → liquid → solid each step is negative ΔS. Liquid → gas is always positive ΔS.

> [!warning] Trap: Don't confuse the sign of ΔH (exothermic for condensation) with the sign of ΔS (also negative). They happen to have the same sign here but for independent reasons.

## Q2: Conductivity — Electrolyte Identification
*Topic: Unit 4 — Electrolytes / electrical conductivity in ionic solutions*
**Answer: B**

Which compound, when dissolved, produces the solution with highest electrical conductivity? B: KI

KI is an ionic compound that dissociates completely into K⁺ and I⁻, producing two ions per formula unit. Conductivity depends on concentration of charge carriers. KI fully dissociates → maximum ions → maximum conductivity.

> [!check] Strong electrolytes (fully dissociated ionic compounds) always conduct better than weak electrolytes (partially ionized) or nonelectrolytes.

> [!warning] Trap: Molecular compounds like glucose or ethanol dissolve but don't ionize — zero conductivity.

## Q3: Reaction Rates and Equilibrium — Adding a Reagent
*Topic: Unit 5 — Kinetics (how adding a species immediately affects forward vs. reverse rate)*
**Answer: C**

For a reaction at equilibrium, I₂ is added. What happens to forward and reverse rates immediately after addition?
C: Forward rate immediately increases; reverse rate is initially unchanged.

Adding I₂ increases [I₂]. Forward rate depends on [I₂] (and other reactant concentrations) — it instantly increases. Reverse rate depends on product concentrations, which haven't changed yet — it stays the same initially. Over time, as products accumulate, the reverse rate increases until equal forward and reverse rates are re-established.

> [!check] "Immediately after" = only the rate that depends on the added species changes instantly.

> [!warning] Trap: Both rates do NOT increase simultaneously. Reverse rate lags because products haven't changed yet.

## Q4: Ionization Energy Jumps — Identifying Valence Electrons
*Topic: Unit 1 — Successive ionization energies → identifying group / valence electrons*
**Answer: B**

An element has successive IEs (kJ/mol): 580, 1815, 2740, 11,600, 14,800... Which element? B: Al

The large jump occurs between IE₃ and IE₄ (from ~2740 to ~11,600 kJ/mol). This jump signals removal of the first core electron. The element has 3 valence electrons → Group 13 → Al.

> [!check] The large jump always falls between removing the last valence electron and the first core electron. Count the IEs before the jump to get the group number.

> [!warning] Trap: Don't confuse the jump position with the group number off-by-one. If the jump is between 3rd and 4th IE → 3 valence electrons → Group 13.

## Q7: Manipulating Equilibrium Constants — Reverse and Scale
*Topic: Unit 7 — K manipulation (reverse → 1/K; multiply coefficients → Kⁿ)*
**Answer: A**

A reaction has K = 10¹⁰. The reverse reaction is written, and all coefficients are doubled. What is K for the new reaction?
A: K = (1/10¹⁰)² = 1 × 10⁻²⁰

Step 1 — Reverse: K_new = 1/K = 1/10¹⁰ = 10⁻¹⁰
Step 2 — Double coefficients: K_new = (10⁻¹⁰)² = 10⁻²⁰

> [!check] Rules: Reverse → K becomes 1/K. Multiply coefficients by n → K becomes Kⁿ. These apply sequentially.

> [!warning] Trap: Don't just negate the exponent. Reversing gives reciprocal (flip sign of exponent); doubling gives square (multiply exponent by 2). Order matters.

## Q8: Enzymes and Activation Energy
*Topic: Unit 5 — Catalysis / activation energy (enzymes lower Ea, not ΔG)*
**Answer: B**

An enzyme accelerates a biological reaction. How does it work? B: The enzyme temporarily binds to the reactant, lowering the activation energy.

Enzymes are biological catalysts. They provide an alternative reaction pathway with lower Ea by forming a temporary enzyme-substrate complex. They do not change ΔG°, ΔH°, or the equilibrium constant.

> [!check] Catalyst (enzyme): lowers Ea, speeds rate, no effect on K or ΔG°.

> [!warning] Trap: Enzymes are not "consumed" (they're regenerated). They don't shift equilibrium position — they only reach it faster.

**Answer: D**

## Q11: Standard Cell Potential
*Topic: Unit 9 — Electrochemistry / E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode*
**Answer: C**

E° values: Ag⁺/Ag = +0.80 V; Zn²⁺/Zn = −0.76 V. Zn is the anode. What is E°cell?
C: E°cell = 0.80 − (−0.76) = +1.56 V

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode = (+0.80) − (−0.76) = +1.56 V

Zn has the lower E° → oxidized → anode. Ag⁺ has higher E° → reduced → cathode.

> [!check] Always: E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode. Cathode = higher reduction potential. Never flip the sign of E° when using this formula.

> [!warning] Trap: Do NOT multiply E° by stoichiometric coefficients. E° is an intensive property — it doesn't scale with moles.

## Q14: Ionization Energy — He vs. H
*Topic: Unit 1 — Ionization energy / nuclear charge effect (same shell, higher Z)*
**Answer: B**

Why does He have a greater first ionization energy than H?
B: The nuclear charge of He (Z = 2) is greater than that of H (Z = 1), so the 1s electron in He experiences a stronger attractive force from the nucleus.

Both H and He have electrons in the 1s orbital. He has 2 protons pulling on each electron vs. H's 1 proton. Greater nuclear charge → stronger attraction → harder to remove electron → higher IE.

> [!check] Same shell (1s) means similar shielding, so the higher Z in He directly increases effective nuclear charge and IE.

> [!warning] Trap: He has two electrons, but they shield each other minimally (same shell). The dominant effect is the doubled nuclear charge.

## Q16: Bond Enthalpies — Exothermic vs. Endothermic
*Topic: Unit 6 — Bond enthalpy / energy to break bonds vs. energy released forming bonds*
**Answer: D**

A reaction is exothermic. Which best explains why?
D: The total energy required to break bonds in the reactants is less than the total energy released when bonds form in the products.

ΔH_rxn = energy to break bonds (endothermic, +) − energy released forming bonds (exothermic, −). If energy released forming > energy to break, net ΔH < 0 (exothermic).

> [!check] Breaking bonds always costs energy (+). Forming bonds always releases energy (−). Exothermic = forming bonds releases more than breaking bonds costs.

## Q18: Heat of Combustion — Mass-Based Comparison
*Topic: Unit 6 — Thermochemistry / converting mass → moles → heat released*
**Answer: C**

30 g of ethane (CH₃CH₃, ΔHcomb = −1560 kJ/mol, MM = 30) vs. 30 g of propanol (CH₃CH₂CH₂OH, ΔHcomb = −2020 kJ/mol, MM = 60). Which releases more heat?

> [!check] Always convert mass → moles before multiplying by molar ΔH. Higher molar mass = fewer moles per gram.

## Q19: Kinetic Molecular Theory — Molecular Speed
*Topic: Unit 3 — KMT / same T = same avg KE; lighter molecule → faster speed*
**Answer: B**

At the same temperature, which gas — ethane or propanol vapor — has the greater average molecular speed?
B: Ethane has greater average molecular speed.

At the same temperature, all gases have the same average kinetic energy: KE = ½mv². Since ethane (MM = 30) is lighter than propanol (MM = 60), it must move faster to have the same KE. Lighter molecule → faster speed.

> [!check] Same T = same average KE. Lighter molecule → faster average speed. The relationship: u_rms = √(3RT/M) — speed ∝ 1/√M.

## Q20: Dissolution Thermodynamics — Entropy-Driven
*Topic: Unit 9 — Gibbs free energy / entropy-driven spontaneous dissolution (ΔH > 0, ΔS > 0)*
**Answer: D**

KNO₃ dissolves endothermically (ΔH > 0) yet dissolves spontaneously. Why?
D: The entropy increase from dispersing K⁺ and NO₃⁻ ions throughout the solution makes ΔG° negative despite the positive ΔH.

ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. If ΔH > 0 but ΔS > 0 (dissolution increases disorder), then at room temperature, the −TΔS term can overcome +ΔH, making ΔG < 0 (spontaneous).

> [!check] Ionic dissolution always increases entropy (ions become dispersed). If that increase is large enough, it drives the dissolution even when endothermic.

## Q21: Buffer Response to Added Acid
*Topic: Unit 8 — Buffers / conjugate base neutralizes added strong acid; pH drops slightly*
**Answer: B**

HCl is added to an acetic acid/acetate buffer. What happens?
B: pH decreases slightly because H⁺ reacts with CH₃COO⁻ (acetate).

The added H⁺ is neutralized by the conjugate base: H⁺ + CH₃COO⁻ → CH₃COOH. This converts conjugate base to weak acid — the ratio [A⁻]/[HA] decreases — so pH decreases slightly. It does not drop dramatically because the buffer absorbs the added acid.

> [!check] Buffer + strong acid: the conjugate base (A⁻) neutralizes it. pH decreases slightly (not sharply).

> [!warning] Trap: pH decreases, not increases, when acid is added. If base is added, pH increases slightly.

**Answer: requires diagram**

Reaction: 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)

**Answer: D**

A PE vs. distance graph for two atoms shows a minimum (well) at ~74 pm. At 75 pm, what is the net force?
D: Attractive (net force is attractive)

## Q29: Kw Temperature Dependence — Neutral pH
*Topic: Unit 8 — Kw / neutral pH is not always 7 (neutral means [H⁺] = [OH⁻], not pH = 7)*
**Answer: B**

At 40°C, Kw = 3.0 × 10⁻¹⁴. What is the pH of neutral water at 40°C?
B: pH ≈ 6.8

Neutral = [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = √Kw = √(3.0 × 10⁻¹⁴) = 1.73 × 10⁻⁷ M
pH = −log(1.73 × 10⁻⁷) = 6.76 ≈ **6.8**

The solution is still chemically neutral (equal [H⁺] and [OH⁻]); the neutral pH is just not 7.00 at temperatures other than 25°C.

> [!check] Neutral ≠ pH 7.00. Neutral means [H⁺] = [OH⁻]. At T > 25°C, neutral pH < 7 because Kw increases with temperature.

## Q32: Dilution of Strong Acid — pH Calculation
*Topic: Unit 8 — Strong acid dilution / C₁V₁ = C₂V₂ using [H⁺] from pH*
**Answer: C**

30.0 mL of HNO₃(aq) at pH 4.20 is diluted with distilled water to pH 5.20. How much water was added?
C: 270 mL

HNO₃ is a **strong acid** → [H⁺] = concentration exactly.

[H⁺]₁ = 10⁻⁴·²⁰ = 6.31 × 10⁻⁵ M
[H⁺]₂ = 10⁻⁵·²⁰ = 6.31 × 10⁻⁶ M

Moles of H⁺ are conserved: C₁V₁ = C₂V₂
(6.31 × 10⁻⁵)(30.0) = (6.31 × 10⁻⁶)(V₂)
V₂ = 300.0 mL

Volume added = 300.0 − 30.0 = **270 mL**

> [!check] pH change of 1.00 = factor of 10 in [H⁺] = factor of 10 in volume (at constant moles). 30 × 10 = 300 mL total.

> [!warning] Trap: If this were a weak acid, you could NOT use C₁V₁ = C₂V₂ directly for pH because the equilibrium shifts upon dilution.

## Q33: K vs. Temperature — Inferring ΔH° and ΔS°
*Topic: Unit 9 — Thermodynamics / inferring signs of ΔH° and ΔS° from how K changes with T*
**Answer: D**

K > 1 at temperatures below 500 K; K < 1 at temperatures above 500 K. What are the signs of ΔH° and ΔS°?
D: ΔH° < 0 and ΔS° < 0

K > 1 → ΔG° < 0 → reaction favored. This occurs only at **low T** (below 500 K). From the four-quadrant table: spontaneous only at low T → ΔH < 0 AND ΔS < 0.

When ΔH < 0 and ΔS < 0: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. At low T, ΔH dominates (negative) → ΔG < 0. At high T, the −TΔS term (positive, since ΔS < 0) dominates → ΔG > 0.

The crossover at T = 500 K confirms: 500 K ≈ ΔH°/ΔS°.

> [!check] K > 1 at low T, K < 1 at high T → exothermic AND entropy-decreasing reaction. Favored by enthalpy, disfavored by entropy.

## Q34: Saturated Solution Equilibrium — Constant Concentration During Evaporation
*Topic: Unit 7 — Ksp / dynamic equilibrium in saturated solution (precipitation maintains ion concentration)*
**Answer: D**

Saturated CuSO₄(aq) with solid CuSO₄ present. Water evaporates. [SO₄²⁻] doesn't change. Why?
D: As water evaporates, more CuSO₄(s) precipitates out of solution.

The solution is saturated at constant temperature → Ksp is constant. As water evaporates, the volume decreases, which would increase [SO₄²⁻] above Ksp. The system responds by precipitating more CuSO₄(s) until [SO₄²⁻] returns to its equilibrium value. The dynamic equilibrium (dissolving = precipitating) maintains constant ion concentrations.

> [!check] Saturated solution with solid present = at Ksp equilibrium. Any change that would exceed Ksp causes precipitation to restore it. Temperature (and thus Ksp) is constant here.

## Q37: Back-Calculation from Calibration Graph — HCl Concentration
*Topic: Unit 9 — Quantitative analysis / calibration curve → stoichiometry → molarity*
**Answer: D**

Another eggshell sample completely reacts with 4.0 mL of HCl(aq). The reaction produces 0.095 atm of CO₂. Find [HCl].
D: 1.0 M

From graph: 0.095 atm → ~0.200 g CaCO₃ = 0.200 g ÷ 100.09 g/mol = 0.00200 mol CaCO₃

Reaction: CaCO₃ + 2HCl → Ca²⁺ + CO₂ + H₂O + 2Cl⁻
mol HCl = 2 × 0.00200 = 0.00400 mol

[HCl] = 0.00400 mol / 0.0040 L = **1.0 M**

> [!check] Read from calibration graph → moles of CaCO₃ → moles HCl (×2 from stoichiometry) → [HCl] = mol/volume.

## Q40: Kinetic vs. Thermodynamic Control
*Topic: Unit 5 — Kinetic vs. thermodynamic control / large Ea prevents thermodynamically favorable reaction (diamond → graphite)*
**Answer: C**

C(diamond) → C(graphite), ΔG° = −2.9 kJ/mol. This reaction is not observed at room temperature. Why?
C: The reaction has an extremely large activation energy due to the strong three-dimensional covalent bonding in diamond.

ΔG° < 0 means the reaction is thermodynamically favorable. It doesn't occur because diamond's 3D covalent network requires breaking an enormous number of C–C bonds to reorganize to graphite's layered structure. The activation energy (Ea) is enormous → essentially zero reaction rate at room temperature.

> [!check] Thermodynamic favorability (ΔG° < 0) does NOT guarantee the reaction occurs. Kinetics can block it if Ea is too large. The diamond example is the classic AP case.

> [!warning] Trap: D says "not thermodynamically favorable" — FALSE. ΔG° = −2.9 kJ/mol means it IS favorable. B says entropy decreases in graphite — FALSE; graphite (layered) has more entropy than diamond (rigid 3D).

## Q45: IMF in Solid CCl₄

## Q47: Reaction Quotient — Predicting Shift Direction
*Topic: Unit 7 — Reaction quotient Q / Q < K means forward shift toward products*
**Answer: A**

X₂(g) + Y₂(g) ⇌ 2XY(g), Kc = 3.0. Which initial conditions lead to more product formation?

Calculate Q for each option and compare to K = 3.0. Need Q < K for forward shift.

## Q48: cis vs. trans Isomers — Vapor Pressure
*Topic: Unit 3 — cis/trans isomers / IMF and vapor pressure (cis = polar = stronger IMF = lower VP)*
**Answer: D**

cis-1,2-dichloroethene vs. trans-1,2-dichloroethene. Which has higher vapor pressure at 20°C, and why?
D: The trans-isomer, because it has only London dispersion forces, whereas the cis-isomer also has dipole-dipole interactions.

cis isomer: both Cl's on same side → permanent dipole → has dipole-dipole interactions (+ LDF)
trans isomer: Cl's on opposite sides → dipoles cancel → nonpolar → only LDF

Stronger IMFs (cis) → higher BP → lower vapor pressure.
Weaker IMFs (trans) → lower BP → higher vapor pressure.

## Q49: Ksp with Common Ion Present — Maximum [Ca²⁺]
*Topic: Unit 7 — Ksp / common-ion effect (solve for max [Ca²⁺] given existing [F⁻])*
**Answer: B**

CaF₂(s) ⇌ Ca²⁺(aq) + 2F⁻(aq), Ksp = 4.0 × 10⁻¹¹. Drinking water has [F⁻] = 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ M. Maximum [Ca²⁺] without causing F⁻ to precipitate?
B: 0.025 M

If precipitation does NOT occur, the ion product Q must not exceed Ksp:
[Ca²⁺][F⁻]² ≤ Ksp

[Ca²⁺] ≤ Ksp / [F⁻]² = (4.0 × 10⁻¹¹) / (4.0 × 10⁻⁵)²
= (4.0 × 10⁻¹¹) / (16 × 10⁻¹⁰)
= (4.0 × 10⁻¹¹) / (1.6 × 10⁻⁹)
= **0.025 M**

> [!check] Common-ion problem: use Ksp = [Ca²⁺][F⁻]². Solve for [Ca²⁺] with [F⁻] already set. The condition is Q ≤ Ksp (to avoid precipitation).

> [!warning] Trap: D (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁵) comes from forgetting to square [F⁻]. C comes from a different arithmetic error. Always check: in Ksp for CaF₂, the [F⁻] term is squared.

## Q50: Hess's Law for Equilibrium Constants — Coupled Reactions
*Topic: Unit 7 — Hess's Law for K / multiply K values when adding reactions; K >> 1 → ΔG° < 0*
**Answer: D**

Given at 1000 K:
Rxn 1: 2FeO(s) ⇌ 2Fe(s) + O₂(g), Keq = 1 × 10⁻⁶
Rxn 2: CO₂(g) ⇌ C(s) + O₂(g), Keq = 1 × 10⁻³²

Find Keq and ΔG° for: 2FeO(s) + C(s) ⇌ 2Fe(s) + CO₂(g)

Target = Rxn 1 (forward) + Rxn 2 (reversed):
- Rxn 1 forward: K₁ = 1 × 10⁻⁶
- Rxn 2 reversed (C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g)): K₂_rev = 1/K₂ = 1/(1 × 10⁻³²) = 1 × 10³²

K_target = K₁ × K₂_rev = (1 × 10⁻⁶)(1 × 10³²) = **1 × 10²⁶**

K >> 1 → ΔG° < 0 (negative, spontaneous)

Answer: D (Keq = 1 × 10²⁶, ΔG° = negative)

> [!check] Hess's Law for K: reverse a reaction → K becomes 1/K. Add reactions → multiply K values. K >> 1 → ΔG° < 0. Large K means this coupled reaction is thermodynamically favorable even though neither original reaction alone is.
