## Strong Bases

> [!danger] Strong bases fully dissociate
> **Group 1 hydroxides (all strong):** LiOH, NaOH, KOH, RbOH, CsOH
> **Heavy Group 2 hydroxides (strong):** Ca(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂
> 
> Mg(OH)₂ and Be(OH)₂ are **NOT** strong — they are sparingly soluble.

## Solubility Rules

> [!important] Apply in order — first matching rule wins

| Rule | Soluble? | Exceptions |
|---|---|---|
| Group 1 ions (Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺, Rb⁺, Cs⁺) | **Always soluble** | None |
| NH₄⁺ compounds | **Always soluble** | None |
| NO₃⁻ compounds | **Always soluble** | None |
| ClO₄⁻ (perchlorate) | **Always soluble** | None |
| CH₃COO⁻ (acetate) | **Always soluble** | None |
| Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻ compounds | **Soluble** | Insoluble with Ag⁺, Pb²⁺, Hg₂²⁺ |
| SO₄²⁻ compounds | **Soluble** | Insoluble with Ba²⁺, Pb²⁺, Hg₂²⁺, Ca²⁺ (slightly) |
| OH⁻ compounds | **Insoluble** | Soluble with Group 1, Ca²⁺, Sr²⁺, Ba²⁺ |
| CO₃²⁻, PO₄³⁻, SO₃²⁻ | **Insoluble** | Soluble with Group 1, NH₄⁺ |
| S²⁻ compounds | **Insoluble** | Soluble with Group 1, NH₄⁺, Group 2 |

## Activity Series (Metals)

> [!tip] More active = higher on list = oxidized more easily = displaces metals below it
> **Most active at top → least active at bottom**

| Ca | Reacts with cold water |
| Na | Reacts with cold water |

| Fe | Reacts with steam |
| Ni | |
| Sn | |
| Pb | |
| **H₂** | ← dividing line: metals above displace H₂ from acids |

## Phase Change Names & Energy

> [!abstract] Every phase change name and direction

| Process | Direction | ΔH | ΔS |
|---|---|---|---|
| Melting (fusion) | solid → liquid | + (endothermic) | + |
| Freezing (solidification) | liquid → solid | − (exothermic) | − |
| Vaporization (evaporation/boiling) | liquid → gas | + (endothermic) | + |
| Condensation | gas → liquid | − (exothermic) | − |
| Sublimation | solid → gas | + (endothermic) | + |
| Deposition | gas → solid | − (exothermic) | − |

> [!note] During a phase change, temperature does not change — all energy goes into breaking/forming IMFs, not increasing KE.

## Intermolecular Forces — Hierarchy

> [!tip] Weakest → Strongest
> **LDF < Dipole–Dipole < Hydrogen Bond < Ion–Dipole**

| IMF | Requires | Affects |
|---|---|---|
| London Dispersion Forces (LDF) | All molecules (nonpolar AND polar) | BP, viscosity, surface tension |
| Dipole–Dipole | Polar molecules | BP above LDF prediction |
| Hydrogen Bond | H bonded directly to F, O, or N | Anomalously high BP, water's unique properties |
| Ion–Dipole | Ion + polar molecule | Dissolving ionic compounds in polar solvents |

**LDF strength increases with:** more electrons (higher molar mass), more surface area (linear > branched).

## Electrochemistry — Must Know

> [!danger] These are the hardest to keep straight

| Cell Type | ΔG | E°cell | Reaction | Energy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Galvanic (voltaic) | Negative (−) | Positive (+) | Spontaneous | Produces electrical energy |
| Electrolytic | Positive (+) | Negative (−) | Non-spontaneous | Requires electrical energy input |

**Always:** Anode = oxidation (A = An ox), Cathode = reduction (Red cat)
**Galvanic:** Anode is negative (−), Cathode is positive (+)
**Electrolytic:** Anode is positive (+), Cathode is negative (−)

**Salt bridge:** Allows ion flow to maintain electrical neutrality. Anions flow toward anode; cations flow toward cathode.

## Integrated Rate Laws — Graphical ID

> [!tip] The straight-line graph tells you the order

| Order | Straight-line plot | Slope | Half-life |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zero | [A] vs. time | −k | $t_{1/2} = \frac{[A]_0}{2k}$ |
| First | ln[A] vs. time | −k | $t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{k}$ (constant!) |
| Second | 1/[A] vs. time | +k | $t_{1/2} = \frac{1}{k[A]_0}$ |

> [!note] Only first-order has a constant half-life — this is the most-tested fact about half-life.

## Thermodynamics — Spontaneity Rules

> [!abstract] Four-quadrant table — know without thinking

| ΔH° | ΔS° | ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS° | Spontaneous? |
|---|---|---|---|
| − | + | Always negative | Always (at all T) |
| + | − | Always positive | Never (at any T) |
| − | − | Negative at low T | Low temperature only |
| + | + | Negative at high T | High temperature only |

**Threshold temperature:** When ΔG° = 0 → $T = \Delta H° / \Delta S°$ — this is the temperature at which the reaction switches from favorable to unfavorable (or vice versa).

## Oxidation Number Rules (in order)

1. Pure element = 0
2. Monatomic ion = its charge
3. F is always −1
4. O is usually −2 (except in peroxides: −1; with F: +2)
5. H is +1 with nonmetals; −1 with metals (metal hydrides)
6. Sum of oxidation numbers = charge of species (0 for neutral, charge for ions)

## Acid–Base Quick Reference

**Conjugate pairs differ by exactly one H⁺.**

| Relationship | Formula |
|---|---|
| pH + pOH = 14 (at 25°C) | $[\text{H}^+][\text{OH}^-] = K_w$ |
| Ka × Kb = Kw | For conjugate acid-base pair |
| At half-equivalence point | pH = pKa |
| Henderson-Hasselbalch | $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]}$ |

**Oxyacid strength rule:** More oxygen atoms on central atom → more electronegative environment → weaker O–H bond → stronger acid.
*HClO < HClO₂ < HClO₃ < HClO₄ (strongest)*

**Binary acid strength:** Larger/more polarizable anion → weaker H–X bond → stronger acid. Down a group: HF < HCl < HBr < HI.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

> [!warning] These cost points every year

- **Entropy:** Elements in standard state have S° ≠ 0 (only ΔHf° = 0 for elements)
- **Rate law:** Comes from **experiment**, never from the balanced equation (unless elementary step)
- **Equilibrium:** Solids and pure liquids are **omitted** from K expressions
- **pOH:** Diluting a weak acid → [H₃O⁺] decreases → pH increases → pOH **decreases**
- **Buffers:** Henderson-Hasselbalch numerator is **always** the conjugate base [A⁻], denominator is weak acid [HA]
- **Formal charge:** FC = (valence e⁻) − (nonbonding e⁻) − ½(bonding e⁻)
- **Molar mass vs. atomic mass:** g/mol for calculations; amu is per single atom
- **Strong acid pH:** pH = −log[HA]₀ directly (no ICE needed — fully dissociates)
- **Arrhenius:** Higher Ea → more temperature-sensitive (steeper slope on ln k vs. 1/T graph)
- **Galvanic vs. electrolytic:** If ΔG < 0, it's galvanic (spontaneous). If you're applying voltage, it's electrolytic.
