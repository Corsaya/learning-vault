## Topic 7.1: Introduction to Equilibrium

**Example 1 — Vapor–liquid equilibrium from pressure data (Progress Check Q1 type):**
Acetone is placed in a sealed container with a pressure sensor. The pressure rises for 2 minutes, then holds constant. Liquid acetone is still visible. What does this indicate?

Step 1: Interpret the pressure plateau.
Pressure rose initially because evaporation rate exceeded condensation rate — more vapor was being produced than returning to liquid.

Step 2: Interpret the plateau while liquid remains.
When pressure stops rising, evaporation rate = condensation rate. This is **dynamic equilibrium**. Liquid persists because not all acetone evaporated — the system reached saturation pressure before running out of liquid.

**Answer:** The system is at vapor–liquid equilibrium. Both evaporation and condensation continue at equal rates; the pressure plateau reflects constant vapor concentration, not the cessation of evaporation.

*Key move: A pressure plateau with liquid still present = dynamic equilibrium. Not "evaporation stopped" — both directions continue at equal rates.*

> Trap: Choosing "completely vaporized" because pressure is constant. If all liquid had evaporated, pressure would continue rising as temperature increases. Liquid visible = equilibrium between phases, not completion.

**Example 2 — Distinguishing equilibrium from "stopped" in a colored gas (Progress Check Q2 type):**
$2\ \text{NO}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons \text{N}_2\text{O}_4(g)$

A tube of pure $\text{NO}_2$ (brown) is sealed and placed at $30°\text{C}$. Which observation best supports that equilibrium is established?

(A) Total mass is constant.
(B) The solution becomes completely colorless.
(C) Total pressure decreases then reaches a constant value.
(D) Vaporization stops.

Step 1: Eliminate wrong answers.
(A) Mass is conserved in a sealed container regardless of equilibrium — not a useful signal.
(B) At equilibrium both $\text{NO}_2$ (brown) and $\text{N}_2\text{O}_4$ (colorless) are present; "completely colorless" would mean all $\text{NO}_2$ converted, which a small-ish $K$ won't do.
(D) "Vaporization stops" misidentifies equilibrium as cessation — processes never stop at equilibrium.

Step 2: Evaluate (C).
Forward reaction: $2\ \text{NO}_2 \to \text{N}_2\text{O}_4$ converts 2 mol gas → 1 mol gas → total pressure decreases. When pressure stops changing = concentrations stopped changing = equilibrium.

**Answer: (C)** — Pressure decreases then holds constant; this observable, measurable change confirms equilibrium.

*Key move: Look for a physical observable that changes then stabilizes. Constant mass or "completely one thing" are not equilibrium indicators.*

**Example 3 — Reading equilibrium from a concentration-time graph (Progress Check Q3 type):**
$\text{N}_2\text{O}_4(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\ \text{NO}_2(g)$

A graph shows $[\text{NO}_2]$ rising and $[\text{N}_2\text{O}_4]$ falling. At time $t = D$, both curves become flat and remain flat. At time $t = B$, the curves cross ($[\text{NO}_2] = [\text{N}_2\text{O}_4]$). At which time is equilibrium established?

Step 1: Apply the definition of equilibrium.
Equilibrium requires constant concentrations — flat lines on the graph.

Step 2: Reject the crossing-point argument.
At $t = B$, $[\text{NO}_2] = [\text{N}_2\text{O}_4]$, but concentrations are still changing. Equal concentrations ≠ equilibrium.

**Answer: At time $D$**, where both curves are horizontal and unchanging.

> Trap: Choosing time $B$ because the concentrations are equal there. The equality of concentrations is irrelevant; equal **rates** define equilibrium, and equal rates produce flat concentration lines.

*Key move: Equilibrium = flat graph. Identify the time at which the slopes of both concentration curves become zero and remain zero.*

**Example 4 — Particle diagram: does it represent dynamic equilibrium? (Progress Check Q4 type):**
$\text{AgCl}(s) \rightleftharpoons \text{Ag}^+(aq) + \text{Cl}^-(aq)$

A single particle diagram shows AgCl solid with equal numbers of Ag⁺ and Cl⁻ ions in solution. Does this represent dynamic equilibrium?

Step 1: Apply the requirement for dynamic equilibrium representation.
Dynamic equilibrium requires showing that both dissolution AND precipitation are occurring simultaneously at equal rates.

Step 2: Evaluate the diagram.
A single static snapshot shows composition at one moment — it cannot show whether ions are dissolving or precipitating at this instant.

**Answer:** No — the diagram does not represent dynamic equilibrium because it shows a single static snapshot. A proper representation would need to indicate that both processes (dissolution and precipitation) are occurring simultaneously at equal rates.

> Trap: "Good representation because $[\text{Ag}^+] = [\text{Cl}^-]$." Equal ion concentrations follow from 1:1 stoichiometry regardless of equilibrium status. The missing element is the depiction of both simultaneous processes.

## Topic 7.2 & 7.3: Equilibrium Constants

**Example 5 — Writing the $K_c$ expression (AP exam style):**
Write $K_c$ for each of the following:

(a) $\text{N}_2(g) + 3\ \text{H}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\ \text{NH}_3(g)$

(b) $\text{CaCO}_3(s) \rightleftharpoons \text{CaO}(s) + \text{CO}_2(g)$

(c) $\text{Fe}^{3+}(aq) + \text{SCN}^-(aq) \rightleftharpoons \text{FeSCN}^{2+}(aq)$

(a) All gases: $K_c = \dfrac{[\text{NH}_3]^2}{[\text{N}_2][\text{H}_2]^3}$

(b) Both solid species are omitted: $K_c = [\text{CO}_2]$

(c) All aqueous: $K_c = \dfrac{[\text{FeSCN}^{2+}]}{[\text{Fe}^{3+}][\text{SCN}^-]}$

*Key move: Solids and pure liquids are omitted. Exponents come from stoichiometric coefficients. Products over reactants.*

> Trap: Including $[\text{CaCO}_3]$ or $[\text{CaO}]$ in part (b). Pure solids have fixed "activity" = 1; they are absorbed into the numerical value of $K$.

**Example 6 — $K_c$ calculation from equilibrium concentrations (Progress Check Q7 type):**
$2\ \text{HI}(g) \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2(g) + \text{I}_2(g)$

Initial: $[\text{HI}] = 0.30\ M$, $[\text{H}_2] = [\text{I}_2] = 0$. At equilibrium: $[\text{H}_2] = 0.030\ M$.

Find $K_c$.

Step 1: Use stoichiometry to find $[\text{I}_2]_{\text{eq}}$.
$\text{H}_2$ and $\text{I}_2$ are produced in a 1:1 ratio, so $[\text{I}_2]_{\text{eq}} = [\text{H}_2]_{\text{eq}} = 0.030\ M$.

Step 2: Find $[\text{HI}]_{\text{eq}}$.
$[\text{HI}]_{\text{eq}} = 0.30 - 2(0.030) = 0.30 - 0.060 = 0.24\ M$

Step 3: Calculate $K_c$.
$$K_c = \frac{[\text{H}_2][\text{I}_2]}{[\text{HI}]^2} = \frac{(0.030)(0.030)}{(0.24)^2} = \frac{9.0 \times 10^{-4}}{5.76 \times 10^{-2}} \approx 1.6 \times 10^{-2}$$

**Answer: $K_c \approx 1.6 \times 10^{-2}$**

> Trap: Using $[\text{HI}]_{\text{eq}} = 0.30\ M$ without subtracting the amount consumed. Each mole of $\text{H}_2$ formed requires 2 moles of HI to decompose (coefficient = 2 in the balanced equation).

*Key move: Identify which species are produced in equal molar amounts from their stoichiometric coefficients. Use ICE to track all concentration changes consistently.*

**Example 7 — $K_p$ calculation from partial pressures (Progress Check Q8 type):**
$\text{SO}_2(g) + \text{Cl}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons \text{SO}_2\text{Cl}_2(g)$

Initial: $P_{\text{SO}_2} = P_{\text{Cl}_2} = 1.00\ \text{atm}$. At equilibrium: $P_{\text{SO}_2} = 0.20\ \text{atm}$.

Find $P_{\text{Cl}_2,\text{eq}}$ and $K_p$.

Step 1: Use ICE with pressures.
$\Delta P_{\text{SO}_2} = 0.20 - 1.00 = -0.80\ \text{atm}$. By 1:1:1 stoichiometry:
$\Delta P_{\text{Cl}_2} = -0.80\ \text{atm}$; $\Delta P_{\text{SO}_2\text{Cl}_2} = +0.80\ \text{atm}$.

Step 2: Find equilibrium pressures.
$P_{\text{Cl}_2} = 1.00 - 0.80 = 0.20\ \text{atm}$; $P_{\text{SO}_2\text{Cl}_2} = 0 + 0.80 = 0.80\ \text{atm}$.

Step 3: Calculate $K_p$.
$$K_p = \frac{P_{\text{SO}_2\text{Cl}_2}}{P_{\text{SO}_2} \cdot P_{\text{Cl}_2}} = \frac{0.80}{(0.20)(0.20)} = \frac{0.80}{0.040} = 20.$$

**Answer: $P_{\text{Cl}_2} = 0.20\ \text{atm}$; $K_p = 20$**

> Trap: Using initial pressures in the $K_p$ expression. $K$ always requires equilibrium values — the ICE-derived equilibrium pressures, not the starting values.

## Topic 7.5 & 7.6: Properties and Magnitude of K

**Example 8 — Magnitude of $K$: interpreting $K \ll 1$ (Progress Check Q10 type):**
$\text{HBrO}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_3\text{O}^+(aq) + \text{BrO}^-(aq)$, $K_{eq} = 2.8 \times 10^{-9}$.

Compare $[\text{BrO}^-]_{\text{eq}}$ and $[\text{HBrO}]_{\text{eq}}$.

$K_{eq} = 2.8 \times 10^{-9} \ll 1$ → reactants are overwhelmingly favored at equilibrium.
At equilibrium, $[\text{HBrO}] \gg [\text{BrO}^-]$ and $[\text{H}_3\text{O}^+]$.

Very little HBrO ionizes. Most HBrO remains un-ionized.

**Answer:** $[\text{BrO}^-] \ll [\text{HBrO}]$ — the equilibrium concentration of the product ($\text{BrO}^-$) is much smaller than that of the reactant ($\text{HBrO}$) because $K \ll 1$.

> Trap: Choosing $[\text{H}_3\text{O}^+] \gg [\text{HBrO}]$. This reverses the direction. A small $K$ always means products are scarce, not abundant.

**Example 9 — Large $K$ and stoichiometry: O₂ remaining (Progress Check Q11 type):**
$2\ \text{SO}_2(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\ \text{SO}_3(g)$, $K_p \approx 2 \times 10^5$.
Initial: $P_{\text{SO}_2} = P_{\text{O}_2} = 1\ \text{atm}$ each.

Estimate the equilibrium partial pressure of $\text{O}_2$.

Step 1: Large $K$ → reaction goes nearly to completion. $\text{SO}_2$ is limiting.

Step 2: Check stoichiometry.
The ratio is $2\ \text{SO}_2 : 1\ \text{O}_2$. Each 2 atm of $\text{SO}_2$ consumed requires 1 atm of $\text{O}_2$.
We have 1 atm $\text{SO}_2$ (limiting): this consumes $\frac{1}{2}\ \text{atm}$ of $\text{O}_2$.
$P_{\text{O}_2,\text{eq}} \approx 1.00 - 0.50 = 0.50\ \text{atm}$.

**Answer:** $P_{\text{O}_2} \approx 0.50\ \text{atm}$ — $\text{SO}_2$ runs out first (limiting reactant in 2:1 ratio), leaving excess $\text{O}_2$.

> Trap: Assuming both reactants are fully consumed. The 2:1 stoichiometry means equal initial pressures leaves $\text{SO}_2$ as the limiting reactant; 0.50 atm of $\text{O}_2$ remains unreacted.

**Example 10 — Combining equilibrium constants (Progress Check Q13 type):**

| Rxn | Equation | $K$ |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $\text{HA}(aq) \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+(aq) + \text{A}^-(aq)$ | $K_1 = 0.010$ |
| 2 | $\text{H}_2\text{B}(aq) \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+(aq) + \text{HB}^-(aq)$ | $K_2 = 1.0 \times 10^{-5}$ |

Find $K_3$ for: $\text{HA}(aq) + \text{HB}^-(aq) \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2\text{B}(aq) + \text{A}^-(aq)$

Step 1: Express Rxn 3 in terms of Rxn 1 and 2.
Rxn 3 = Rxn 1 (forward) + Reverse(Rxn 2).

Step 2: Apply rules.
- Rxn 1 (forward): $K_1 = 0.010$
- Reverse Rxn 2: $K = 1/K_2 = 1/(1.0 \times 10^{-5}) = 1.0 \times 10^5$

Step 3: Multiply.
$$K_3 = K_1 \times \frac{1}{K_2} = 0.010 \times 1.0 \times 10^5 = 1.0 \times 10^3$$

**Answer: $K_3 = 1.0 \times 10^3$**

> Trap: Subtracting $K$ values. Equilibrium constants are multiplied when reactions are added — never added or subtracted. Subtracting reactions means dividing $K$ values (multiplying by the reciprocal).

**Example 11 — Finding $K$ for a reversed reaction (Progress Check Q14 type):**
$2\ \text{NO}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons \text{N}_2\text{O}_4(g)$ has $K_1$. Find $K$ for the reverse: $\text{N}_2\text{O}_4(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\ \text{NO}_2(g)$.

Reversing a reaction: $K_{\text{reverse}} = 1/K_1$.

**Answer: $K_{\text{reverse}} = 1/K_1$** — no other information is needed.

*Key move: Reverse reaction → reciprocal K. No need for thermodynamic data or temperature; the relationship is algebraic.*

## Topic 7.7: Calculating Equilibrium Concentrations

**Example 12 — ICE table with a moderate K (Krug 7.7a type):**
$\text{H}_2(g) + \text{I}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\ \text{HI}(g)$, $K_c = 49.0$ at 458°C.

Initial: $[\text{H}_2] = [\text{I}_2] = 0.100\ M$, $[\text{HI}] = 0$.

| | $\text{H}_2$ | $\text{I}_2$ | $2\text{HI}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | 0.100 | 0.100 | 0 |
| C | $-x$ | $-x$ | $+2x$ |
| E | $0.100 - x$ | $0.100 - x$ | $2x$ |

$$K_c = \frac{(2x)^2}{(0.100-x)^2} = 49.0$$

Take the square root of both sides (both sides are perfect squares):
$$\frac{2x}{0.100-x} = 7.0$$

$$2x = 0.700 - 7.0x$$
$$9.0x = 0.700$$
$$x = 0.0778\ M$$

Equilibrium concentrations:
$[\text{H}_2] = [\text{I}_2] = 0.100 - 0.0778 = 0.0222\ M$
$[\text{HI}] = 2(0.0778) = 0.156\ M$

**Answer: $[\text{H}_2] = [\text{I}_2] = 0.0222\ M$; $[\text{HI}] = 0.156\ M$**

*Key move: When both sides of the $K$ expression are perfect squares, take the square root directly — it avoids the quadratic entirely.*

**Example 13 — Small-K approximation (Krug 7.7b type):**
$\text{N}_2(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\ \text{NO}(g)$, $K_c = 1.0 \times 10^{-30}$ at 25°C.

Initial: $[\text{N}_2] = 0.80\ M$, $[\text{O}_2] = 0.20\ M$, $[\text{NO}] = 0$.

| | $\text{N}_2$ | $\text{O}_2$ | $2\text{NO}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | 0.80 | 0.20 | 0 |
| C | $-x$ | $-x$ | $+2x$ |
| E | $0.80 - x$ | $0.20 - x$ | $2x$ |

$$K_c = \frac{(2x)^2}{(0.80 - x)(0.20 - x)} \approx \frac{4x^2}{(0.80)(0.20)} = 1.0 \times 10^{-30}$$

$$4x^2 = 1.0 \times 10^{-30} \times 0.16 = 1.6 \times 10^{-31}$$

$$x^2 = 4.0 \times 10^{-32} \implies x = 2.0 \times 10^{-16}\ M$$

Verify approximation: $x/0.20 = 10^{-15}\ \%$ ← far below 5%. ✓

$[\text{NO}] = 2x = 4.0 \times 10^{-16}\ M$

**Answer: $[\text{NO}] = 4.0 \times 10^{-16}\ M$** — essentially zero, consistent with $K \ll 1$.

> Trap: Not applying the approximation and attempting a full quadratic for $K = 10^{-30}$. At such extreme values, the approximation is valid to many orders of magnitude.

## Topic 7.8: Representations of Equilibrium

**Example 14 — Q from particle diagram and shift direction (Progress Check Q19 type):**
$\text{X}_2(g) + \text{Y}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\ \text{XY}(g)$, $K_c = 25$.

A particle diagram shows: 3 X₂, 5 Y₂, and 2 XY.

Step 1: Write $K_c$ expression.
$$K_c = \frac{[\text{XY}]^2}{[\text{X}_2][\text{Y}_2]}$$

Step 2: Treat particle counts as proportional to concentration.
$$Q_c = \frac{(2)^2}{(3)(5)} = \frac{4}{15} \approx 0.27$$

Step 3: Compare $Q$ to $K$.
$Q = 0.27 < K = 25$ → system must shift **forward** to produce more XY.

**Answer:** $Q < K$; the system is not at equilibrium and will shift forward to produce more $\text{XY}$.

*Key move: Use particle counts as the numerator/denominator quantities in $Q$. Compare to $K$ to determine direction. Never assume equilibrium from a single diagram.*

**Example 15 — Rate-time graph interpretation (Progress Check Q6 type):**
Particle diagrams at $t = 0, 100, 200, 300\ \text{s}$ show increasing amounts of $\text{XY}$ and decreasing $\text{X}$ and $\text{Y}$. Which comparison is supported between 0 and 300 s?

Since XY (product) is still increasing at $t = 300\ \text{s}$: the system has not yet reached equilibrium. Net XY formation means forward rate > reverse rate throughout this interval.

**Answer:** The forward reaction has a faster rate than the reverse reaction between 0 and 300 s — net product formation is occurring.

> Trap: "Rates are equal after 100 s." Equal rates → constant concentrations. But concentrations are still changing at 100 s, so rates are not yet equal.

## Topic 7.9 & 7.10: Le Chatelier's Principle

**Example 16 — Concentration stress: adding $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ (Progress Check Q16 type):**
$\text{Fe}^{3+}(aq) + \text{SCN}^-(aq) \rightleftharpoons \text{FeSCN}^{2+}(aq)$ (light red-brown)

A student adds more $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ to the equilibrium solution. Predict the change in color intensity.

Step 1: Apply Le Chatelier.
Adding $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ (reactant) → $Q$ decreases below $K$ → system shifts **forward** → more $\text{FeSCN}^{2+}$ produced.

Step 2: Connect to observable.
$\text{FeSCN}^{2+}$ is the red-brown species. More of it → **darker color**.

**Answer:** The solution darkens because equilibrium shifts forward, producing more $\text{FeSCN}^{2+}$.

> Trap: "No change, because Fe³⁺ is already present." The presence of a reactant doesn't prevent the system from responding to its addition. Any increase in reactant concentration shifts equilibrium forward.

**Example 17 — Volume change: predicting yield (Progress Check Q17 type):**
$2\ \text{NO}(g) + \text{Cl}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\ \text{NOCl}(g)$

At equilibrium, the volume is halved. Does the yield of $\text{NOCl}$ increase or decrease?

Step 1: Count gas moles.
Reactant side: $2 + 1 = 3\ \text{mol gas}$. Product side: $2\ \text{mol gas}$.

Step 2: Apply the pressure stress.
Halving volume doubles pressure. Le Chatelier: system shifts toward **fewer gas moles** = toward products ($2$ mol).

**Answer:** Yield of $\text{NOCl}$ **increases** — the system shifts toward the product side, which has fewer moles of gas (2 vs. 3), to reduce total pressure.

> Trap: Predicting yield decreases. Answer B of this type correctly identifies that pressure doubled but incorrectly predicts the direction. Always count gas moles on each side and shift toward the side with fewer.

**Example 18 — Temperature change on an endothermic equilibrium (Progress Check Q18 type):**
$\text{Co(H}_2\text{O)}_6^{2+}(\text{pink}) + 4\ \text{Cl}^- \rightleftharpoons \text{CoCl}_4^{2-}(\text{blue}) + 6\ \text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\Delta H > 0$

A blue solution is placed in an ice bath. What change is observed?

Step 1: Identify the endothermic direction.
$\Delta H > 0$ → forward reaction (pink → blue) is endothermic. Heat is a reactant.

Step 2: Apply cooling stress.
Removing heat removes a reactant → equilibrium shifts **left** (reverse) → blue $\text{CoCl}_4^{2-}$ converts back to pink $\text{Co(H}_2\text{O)}_6^{2+}$.

**Answer:** The solution changes from blue to pink — cooling shifts the endothermic equilibrium backward toward the pink species.

> Trap: Attributing the color change to "increased collision frequency." Direction of equilibrium shift is determined thermodynamically, not kinetically. For endothermic reactions, lower temperature always favors reactants.

**Example 19 — Q vs K after adding a product (Progress Check Q21 type):**
$\text{COCl}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons \text{CO}(g) + \text{Cl}_2(g)$

System is at equilibrium. $\text{Cl}_2(g)$ is added. How does the system restore equilibrium?

Step 1: Write correct $Q$ expression.
$$Q = \frac{[\text{CO}][\text{Cl}_2]}{[\text{COCl}_2]}$$

Step 2: Adding $\text{Cl}_2$ increases the numerator → $Q > K$.

Step 3: System shifts reverse (to reduce $[\text{Cl}_2]$ and restore $Q = K$) → more $\text{COCl}_2$ produced.

**Answer:** Adding $\text{Cl}_2$ makes $Q > K$; the system shifts reverse to produce more $\text{COCl}_2$.

> Trap: Inverting the $Q$ expression (putting $[\text{COCl}_2]$ in the numerator). Always write $K = \text{products}/\text{reactants}$ with the correct stoichiometry. An inverted $Q$ gives the wrong shift direction.

## Topic 7.11: Introduction to Solubility Equilibria

**Example 20 — $K_{sp}$ expression for various salts (Progress Check Q22 type):**
For which salt does $K_{sp} = 4s^3$?

Options: (A) $\text{PbCO}_3$; (B) $\text{MnS}$; (C) $\text{Ag}_2\text{SO}_4$; (D) $\text{Mg}_3(\text{PO}_4)_2$

Test each:
- (A) PbCO₃ → Pb²⁺ + CO₃²⁻: $K_{sp} = s^2$ (1:1 salt) ✗
- (B) MnS → Mn²⁺ + S²⁻: $K_{sp} = s^2$ (1:1 salt) ✗
- (C) Ag₂SO₄ → 2Ag⁺ + SO₄²⁻: $K_{sp} = (2s)^2(s) = 4s^3$ ✓
- (D) Mg₃(PO₄)₂ → 3Mg²⁺ + 2PO₄³⁻: $K_{sp} = (3s)^3(2s)^2 = 27s^3 \cdot 4s^2 = 108s^5$ ✗

**Answer: (C) $\text{Ag}_2\text{SO}_4$**

> Trap: Choosing 1:1 salts for $4s^3$. The $4s^3$ form requires a 2:1 or 1:2 salt — one ion appears in double the molar quantity.

**Example 21 — Molar solubility and $K_{sp}$ from $[\text{I}^-]$ (Progress Check Q23 type):**
$\text{Hg}_2\text{I}_2(s) \rightleftharpoons \text{Hg}_2^{2+}(aq) + 2\ \text{I}^-(aq)$; $[\text{I}^-]_{\text{eq}} = 4.6 \times 10^{-10}\ M$.

Find molar solubility $S$ and $K_{sp}$.

Step 1: Relate $[\text{I}^-]$ to $S$.
Each formula unit produces $2\ \text{I}^-$, so $[\text{I}^-] = 2S$.
$$S = \frac{4.6 \times 10^{-10}}{2} = 2.3 \times 10^{-10}\ M$$

Step 2: Find $[\text{Hg}_2^{2+}]$.
$$[\text{Hg}_2^{2+}] = S = 2.3 \times 10^{-10}\ M$$

Step 3: Calculate $K_{sp}$.
$$K_{sp} = [\text{Hg}_2^{2+}][\text{I}^-]^2 = (2.3 \times 10^{-10})(4.6 \times 10^{-10})^2$$

**Answer: $S = 2.3 \times 10^{-10}\ M$; $K_{sp} = (2.3 \times 10^{-10})(4.6 \times 10^{-10})^2$**

> Trap: Setting $S = [\text{I}^-] = 4.6 \times 10^{-10}\ M$. The coefficient of 2 in the dissolution equation means $[\text{I}^-] = 2S$, so $S$ is half of $[\text{I}^-]$.

**Example 22 — $K_{sp}$ to molar solubility (1:1 salt) (Progress Check Q24 type):**
$\text{AgI}(s) \rightleftharpoons \text{Ag}^+(aq) + \text{I}^-(aq)$; $K_{sp} = 8.3 \times 10^{-17}$. Find $S$.

AgI is 1:1: $[\text{Ag}^+] = [\text{I}^-] = S$.
$$K_{sp} = S^2 \implies S = \sqrt{8.3 \times 10^{-17}} = 9.1 \times 10^{-9}\ M$$

**Answer: $S = 9.1 \times 10^{-9}\ M$**

> Trap: Setting $S = K_{sp}$ directly. For a 1:1 salt, $K_{sp} = S^2$, so you must take the square root.

## Topic 7.12: Common-Ion Effect

**Example 23 — Ca(OH)₂ in NaOH solution (Progress Check Q25 type):**
$\text{Ca(OH)}_2(s) \rightleftharpoons \text{Ca}^{2+}(aq) + 2\ \text{OH}^-(aq)$; $K_{sp} = 5.5 \times 10^{-6}$.

Compare dissolving $\text{Ca(OH)}_2$ in distilled water vs. $0.0010\ M$ NaOH.

Step 1: Identify the common ion.
NaOH provides $[\text{OH}^-] = 0.0010\ M$ before any $\text{Ca(OH)}_2$ dissolves.

Step 2: Apply Le Chatelier.
Pre-existing $\text{OH}^-$ is a product ion → equilibrium shifts **left** → less $\text{Ca(OH)}_2$ dissolves.

Step 3: Quantify (optional).
ICE: $K_{sp} = (s)(0.0010 + 2s)^2 \approx s(0.0010)^2$ for small $s$:
$s \approx K_{sp}/(0.0010)^2 = 5.5 \times 10^{-6}/10^{-6} = 5.5\ M$ — wait, this is too large.

Actually for this comparison, the key conceptual answer is: **less solid dissolves** in NaOH than in distilled water.

*Quantitatively: in pure water, $s = (K_{sp}/4)^{1/3}$. With common ion: $s = K_{sp}/([\text{OH}^-])^2$, which is much smaller when $[\text{OH}^-]$ is significant.*

**Answer:** Less $\text{Ca(OH)}_2$ dissolves because the common ion ($\text{OH}^-$) from NaOH shifts equilibrium toward precipitation.

> Trap: Thinking $K_{sp}$ increases when more ions are present. $K_{sp}$ is constant at constant temperature. Only the molar solubility changes due to the common ion.

**Example 24 — AgCN solubility with added NaCN (Progress Check Q26 type):**
$\text{AgCN}(s) \rightleftharpoons \text{Ag}^+(aq) + \text{CN}^-(aq)$; saturated solution $[\text{Ag}^+] = 7.7 \times 10^{-9}\ M$.

Solid NaCN is added. What happens?

Step 1: NaCN dissolves, releasing $\text{CN}^-$ (a common ion — product of AgCN dissolution).

Step 2: Added $\text{CN}^-$ raises $Q$ above $K_{sp}$.
System shifts **left** → more AgCN precipitates.

Step 3: New molar solubility is smaller than $7.7 \times 10^{-9}\ M$.

**Answer:** Molar solubility decreases below $7.7 \times 10^{-9}\ M$; some AgCN precipitates. $K_{sp}$ does not change.

> Trap: Thinking $K_{sp}$ changes when a common ion is added. $K_{sp}$ is a constant at fixed temperature. Adding NaCN only changes the solubility, not $K_{sp}$.

**Example 25 — FRQ-style: Hess's law for $K_{sp}$ from combining reactions (Progress Check Q15 type):**
$\text{PbCl}_2(s) + 2\ \text{Ag}^+(aq) \rightleftharpoons \text{Pb}^{2+}(aq) + 2\ \text{AgCl}(s)$

Given:
- Rxn 1: $\text{PbCl}_2(s) \rightleftharpoons \text{Pb}^{2+}(aq) + 2\ \text{Cl}^-(aq)$; $K_{sp} = K_1$
- Rxn 2: $\text{AgCl}(s) \rightleftharpoons \text{Ag}^+(aq) + \text{Cl}^-(aq)$; $K_{sp} = K_2$

Find $K_{eq}$ for the overall reaction.

Step 1: Express overall as combination of Rxn 1 and Rxn 2.
Overall = Rxn 1 + 2 × Reverse(Rxn 2)

Step 2: Apply rules.
- Rxn 1 (forward): $K_1$
- Reverse Rxn 2, used twice: $(1/K_2)^2 = 1/K_2^2$

Step 3: Multiply.
$$K_{eq} = K_1 \times \frac{1}{K_2^2} = \frac{K_1}{K_2^2}$$

**Answer: $K_{eq} = K_1/(K_2)^2$**

> Trap: Using $K_1 \times (K_2)^2$. Rxn 2 is reversed (gives $1/K_2$) and doubled (squares the $1/K_2$). A reactant coefficient of 2 on $\text{Ag}^+$ means Rxn 2 appears twice in reverse.

*Key move: Write the overall as a combination of given reactions. For each: identify forward vs. reverse and scale factor. Then multiply the adjusted $K$ values.*
