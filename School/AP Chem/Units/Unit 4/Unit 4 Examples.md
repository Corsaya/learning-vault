## Topic 4.1: Balancing Equations

**Example 1 — Balance by inspection:**
$\text{Al}(s) + \text{O}_2(g) \rightarrow \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3(s)$

Al: 1 on left, 2 on right → put 2 in front of Al.
O: 2 on left, 3 on right → use LCM(2,3) = 6. Put 3 in front of O₂ and 2 in front of Al₂O₃.
Recheck Al: need 4 Al on left → coefficient 4.

$$\boxed{4\text{Al}(s) + 3\text{O}_2(g) \rightarrow 2\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3(s)}$$

**Example 2 — Combustion balance (Krug 4.1):**
$\text{C}_3\text{H}_8(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \rightarrow \text{CO}_2(g) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$

C: 3 on left → 3 CO₂. H: 8 on left → 4 H₂O. O: 3(2) + 4(1) = 10 on right → 5 O₂.

$$\boxed{\text{C}_3\text{H}_8(g) + 5\text{O}_2(g) \rightarrow 3\text{CO}_2(g) + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}(g)}$$

**Example 3 — Trap: subscript vs. coefficient (Farabaugh):**
To balance $\text{H}_2(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$:

Wrong: $\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ — this changes the product to hydrogen peroxide.
Correct: $\boxed{2\text{H}_2(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)}$

*Key move: Never change a subscript to balance. Only change coefficients. Changing a subscript changes the identity of the substance.*

## Topic 4.2: Net Ionic Equations

**Example 1 — Precipitation reaction:**
$\ce{AgNO3(aq) + NaCl(aq) -> AgCl(s) + NaNO3(aq)}$

Complete ionic: $\ce{Ag+(aq) + NO3-(aq) + Na+(aq) + Cl-(aq) -> AgCl(s) + Na+(aq) + NO3-(aq)}$

Cancel spectators (Na⁺ and NO₃⁻): $\boxed{\ce{Ag+(aq) + Cl-(aq) -> AgCl(s)}}$

**Example 2 — Strong acid + strong base:**
$\ce{HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) -> NaCl(aq) + H2O(l)}$

NIE: $\boxed{\ce{H+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> H2O(l)}}$

All strong acid + strong base NIEs reduce to this regardless of the spectator ions.

**Example 3 — Weak acid + strong base:**
$\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + NaOH(aq) -> CH3COONa(aq) + H2O(l)}$

CH₃COOH stays molecular (weak acid). NaOH splits. CH₃COONa splits.

NIE: $\boxed{\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + OH-(aq) -> CH3COO-(aq) + H2O(l)}}$

**Example 4 — Predicting products and checking solubility (Progress Check Q4):**
Mix $\text{Pb(NO}_3)_2(aq)$ and $\text{KI}(aq)$.

Swap cations: possible products are PbI₂ and KNO₃.
- KNO₃: K⁺ (Group 1 = always soluble) + NO₃⁻ (always soluble) → stays in solution.
- PbI₂: Pb²⁺ with I⁻ → Pb²⁺ is an exception for halides → **insoluble** → yellow precipitate forms.

NIE: $\boxed{\ce{Pb^2+(aq) + 2I-(aq) -> PbI2(s)}}$

Spectators: K⁺ and NO₃⁻.

*Key move: Always check BOTH possible products against the solubility rules. A reaction only occurs (and an NIE can only be written) if at least one product is insoluble, gaseous, or a weak electrolyte like water.*

**Example 5 — Gas-forming reaction (Farabaugh):**
$\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3(aq) + 2\text{HCl}(aq) \rightarrow$

Products: $\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$ (unstable, decomposes) → $\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2(g)$, and NaCl (soluble).

NIE: $\boxed{\ce{CO3^2-(aq) + 2H+(aq) -> H2O(l) + CO2(g)}}$

Do NOT write $\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$ as a product — it immediately decomposes. Write the gas directly.

## Topic 4.3: Representations of Reactions

**Example 1 — Reading particle counts to write a balanced equation (Krug 4.3/4.4):**
A particulate diagram shows: 4 particles of A (diatomic) and 2 particles of B (single atom) on the left; 2 particles of A₂B on the right.

Count: 4A₂ + 2B → 2A₂B. Reduce by 2: 2A₂ + B → A₂B → but check O count. Actually simplify: the ratio is 4:2:2 = 2:1:1. Balanced equation: $2\text{A}_2 + \text{B} \rightarrow 2\text{A}_2\text{B}$... 

Verify atoms: left has 4 A + 1 B; right shows 2×(2A + 1B) = 4A + 2B. If that doesn't balance, reconsider.

*Key move: Count every atom type on both sides of the diagram. If they aren't equal, the diagram or your reading is wrong. Particle diagrams must satisfy atom conservation exactly.*

**Example 2 — Identify the reaction type from a particulate diagram (Farabaugh):**
A diagram shows:
- Left: separated Na⁺ and CO₃²⁻ ions + separated Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻ ions, all in aqueous phase.
- Right: a cluster of Ca²⁺ and CO₃²⁻ (solid) + Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions still in solution.

Reaction type: **Precipitation.** The Ca²⁺ and CO₃²⁻ ions are removed from solution (disappear) and appear as a solid cluster. The Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are spectators — they appear on both sides unchanged.

NIE from diagram: $\ce{Ca^2+(aq) + CO3^2-(aq) -> CaCO3(s)}$

*Key move: Ions that disappear from the solution phase and reappear as a cluster = precipitate. Ions that remain separate = spectators. Spectators must appear in equal numbers on both sides.*

**Example 3 — Identifying a wrong particulate diagram (Farabaugh Progress Check — MCQ pattern):**
A question shows four diagrams for the reaction $2\text{H}_2(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$.

Diagram A: 4 H₂ molecules + 2 O₂ molecules → 4 H₂O molecules. 
Atom check: left = 8H + 4O; right = 4(2H + 1O) = 8H + 4O. ✓ Ratio check: 4:2:4 = 2:1:2 ✓

Diagram B: 4 H₂ molecules + 1 O₂ molecule → 3 H₂O molecules.
O check: left = 2O; right = 3O. ✗ Atoms not conserved.

Diagram C: 2 H₂ molecules + 2 O₂ molecules → 2 H₂O molecules.
O check: left = 4O; right = 2O. ✗

Diagram D: 2 H₂ + 1 O₂ → 2 H₂O. ✓ (Correct 2:1:2 ratio.)

*Key move: For every candidate diagram, do a full atom count on both sides. Only the one where every element balances AND the stoichiometric ratio matches the balanced equation is correct.*

**Example 4 — Drawing the products side of a diagram (AP FRQ style, Farabaugh):**
Given: a diagram showing 3 formula units of Ag⁺(aq) and NO₃⁻(aq), plus 3 formula units of Na⁺(aq) and Cl⁻(aq). Draw the products.

Step 1 — Write the NIE: $\ce{Ag+(aq) + Cl-(aq) -> AgCl(s)}$
Step 2 — From 3 Ag⁺ and 3 Cl⁻: all form AgCl → draw 3 clustered AgCl solid units.
Step 3 — Spectators: 3 Na⁺ and 3 NO₃⁻ remain in solution — draw them unchanged.

Product diagram: 3 clustered AgCl solid + 3 separate Na⁺ + 3 separate NO₃⁻ in solution.

*Key move: Spectator ions do NOT cluster or change; they appear identically on the products side. Precipitate particles cluster together and are labeled (s) or shown differently from aqueous ions.*

**Example 5 — Completing a product diagram for a gas-forming reaction (AP FRQ style, Farabaugh):**
A particulate diagram shows the reactants side: 2 formula units of Na₂CO₃(aq), represented as 4 Na⁺ and 2 CO₃²⁻ ions in solution, mixed with 4 HCl(aq), represented as 4 H⁺ and 4 Cl⁻ ions in solution. Draw and describe the products side.

Step 1 — Identify the ions present and check for reaction:
- Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are spectator ions (NaCl is soluble; no other insoluble combination forms).
- CO₃²⁻ reacts with H⁺ (acid + carbonate = gas-forming reaction).

Step 2 — Write the NIE:
$$\boxed{\text{CO}_3^{2-}(aq) + 2\text{H}^+(aq) \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \text{CO}_2(g)}$$

Step 3 — Apply stoichiometry to the particles: 2 CO₃²⁻ + 4 H⁺ → 2 H₂O + 2 CO₂.

Step 4 — Draw the products side:
- 2 CO₂ molecules, drawn spread apart and labeled (g) — gas molecules are not close-packed.
- 2 H₂O molecules, drawn as small neutral molecules.
- 4 Na⁺ ions and 4 Cl⁻ ions, drawn separately in solution, unchanged from the reactants side.
- No CO₃²⁻ or H⁺ ions remain — they were fully consumed.

Atom and charge check: C: 2 = 2 ✓; O: 2(3) + 4(1) = 10 on left; 2(2) + 2(1) = 6 on right — wait, count through NIE: CO₃²⁻ contributes 3 O, 2H⁺ contribute 0 O → products 1 H₂O (1 O) + 1 CO₂ (2 O) = 3 O ✓ per pair. H: 2 H⁺ → 2 H in H₂O ✓. Charge: (−2) + 2(+1) = 0 on both sides ✓.

*Key move: Gas-forming reactions consume the carbonate and acid ions entirely and produce neutral gas molecules that are spread apart in the diagram. Spectator ions (Na⁺ and Cl⁻ here) remain in solution, appear in exactly the same count on both sides, and never cluster or disappear. Never show H₂CO₃ as a product — it is unstable and decomposes immediately; write CO₂(g) and H₂O(l) directly.*

## Topic 4.4: Physical and Chemical Changes

**Example 1 — Classify each change (Farabaugh):**

| Change | Physical or Chemical | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| $\text{H}_2\text{O}(s) \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$ | Physical | Only H-bonds broken; same H₂O molecules throughout |
| $2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2(g) + \text{O}_2(g)$ | Chemical | O–H covalent bonds broken; new substances H₂ and O₂ form |
| $\text{AgNO}_3(aq) + \text{NaCl}(aq) \rightarrow \text{AgCl}(s) + \text{NaNO}_3(aq)$ | Chemical | New ionic solid AgCl forms; composition changes |
| Dissolving KNO₃ in water | Physical | K⁺ and NO₃⁻ separate; no new compound; reversible by evaporation |
| $\text{CH}_3\text{OH}(l) \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{OH}(g)$ | Physical | Only IMFs broken; same methanol molecules in gas phase |
| Combustion of ethanol | Chemical | C–H and O–H bonds broken; C=O and O–H bonds in CO₂ and H₂O formed |
| Dissolving $\text{NH}_3$ gas in water | Physical | NH₃ molecules disperse; same molecules present (mostly) |

**Example 2 — The reversibility trap (Farabaugh, Progress Check Q2):**
A student argues that dissolving NaCl is a chemical change because it "can be reversed by evaporation."

This reasoning is wrong on both counts:
1. Reversibility is NOT the criterion for classifying a change. Some chemical reactions are reversible; some physical changes are irreversible.
2. Dissolving NaCl is **physical** because no new compound is formed. The Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions that existed in the crystal still exist in solution — they simply separate and become surrounded by water. No bonds are broken or formed within the formula unit that produce a different substance.

*Key move: Ask "are covalent bonds broken or formed?" If yes → chemical. If only IMFs or lattice forces are disrupted → physical. Reversibility is never the test.*

**Example 3 — Identifying evidence of chemical change (Progress Check Q3):**
A student mixes two colorless solutions and observes a white precipitate forming. The temperature of the solution increases slightly.

Which of the following are evidence of a chemical change?
- White precipitate: ✓ — new substance with different composition (AgCl, BaSO₄, etc.) has formed.
- Temperature increase: ✓ — energy released indicates bonds were formed in products (exothermic reaction).
- Color of the solution changes: N/A here, but if yes, would also be evidence.
- Mixing two liquids: ✗ — mixing alone is not evidence; gases, precipitates, or heat change are required.

*Key move: Precipitate formation and temperature change are two of the most reliable indicators of chemical change on the AP exam.*

**Example 4 — Physical vs. chemical change for dissolution (Progress Check Q4):**
Compare: (a) melting ice, (b) dissolving NaCl in water, (c) dissolving sugar in water, (d) reacting Na metal with water.

- (a) Melting ice: **physical** — H-bonds between H₂O molecules break; same molecules remain.
- (b) Dissolving NaCl: **physical** — Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions that existed in the crystal lattice separate; no new formula unit is created.
- (c) Dissolving sugar (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁): **physical** — sugar molecules disperse; same covalent structure intact.
- (d) Na(s) + H₂O(l) → NaOH(aq) + H₂(g): **chemical** — Na–Na metallic bonds and O–H bonds broken; new O–H bond in NaOH and H–H bond in H₂ formed.

*Key move: Dissolution (regardless of the solute type) is always physical on the AP exam. The reaction of a metal with water, acid, or oxygen is always chemical — new species with different formulas are produced.*

**Example 5 — What can and cannot be concluded from observations (Farabaugh):**
A student stirs iron powder (black solid) into a blue CuSO₄(aq) solution. After several minutes, the solution turns pale blue and a reddish solid appears at the bottom of the beaker.

**Which observations ARE evidence of chemical change?**
- **Reddish solid appears:** A new substance (Cu metal) with a different composition and different physical appearance has formed. This is direct evidence of a chemical change — a new species is present that was not in either starting material.
- **Color change (blue → pale blue):** The decrease in blue color indicates that Cu²⁺ ions (which give CuSO₄(aq) its blue color) are being removed from solution and replaced by Fe²⁺ ions (which are nearly colorless in dilute solution). This color change is not reversible by simple physical means, confirming chemical change.

**Which observations are NOT sufficient evidence alone?**
- **Stirring:** Stirring is a physical action — it mixes the contents but does not itself cause or confirm a chemical change. Many physical mixtures are stirred without reaction.
- **Temperature change (if observed):** A temperature change would support chemical change (exothermic or endothermic reaction), but no temperature change was described in this scenario. If it had been observed, it would count as supporting evidence — but alone it can sometimes accompany physical processes (dissolving).

**NIE for the reaction:**
$$\boxed{\text{Fe}(s) + \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+}(aq) + \text{Cu}(s)}$$

**Oxidation states:**
- Fe: 0 → +2 (oxidized; Fe is the **reducing agent**)
- Cu: +2 → 0 (reduced; Cu²⁺ is the **oxidizing agent**)

**Activity series connection:** Fe is above Cu in the activity series → Fe is a stronger reducing agent → Fe spontaneously displaces Cu²⁺ from solution. The reverse reaction (Cu displacing Fe²⁺) does not occur.

*Key move: For "evidence of chemical change" questions, focus on formation of a new substance (precipitate, gas, new species) and irreversible color changes. Stirring, mixing, and dissolving alone are not evidence. Write the NIE and assign oxidation states to connect the macroscopic observation to the atomic-level change.*

## Topic 4.5: Stoichiometry

**Example 1 — Limiting reactant (Farabaugh H₂ + O₂ → H₂O):**
$\ce{2H2(g) + O2(g) -> 2H2O(l)}$

Experiment: 2.0 mol H₂ and 1.5 mol O₂.

H₂ approach: $2.0\ \text{mol H}_2 \times \frac{1\ \text{mol O}_2}{2\ \text{mol H}_2} = 1.0\ \text{mol O}_2$ needed. Available: 1.5 mol → H₂ needs less O₂ than is present → **H₂ is the limiting reactant.**

Theoretical yield: $2.0\ \text{mol H}_2 \times \frac{2\ \text{mol H}_2\text{O}}{2\ \text{mol H}_2} = \mathbf{2.0\ \text{mol H}_2\text{O}}$

Excess O₂ remaining: $1.5 - 1.0 = 0.5$ mol O₂.

**Example 2 — Percent yield:**
Theoretical yield = $2.0\ \text{mol} \times 18.02\ \text{g/mol} = 36.0\ \text{g}$. Actual = 32.4 g.

$$\% \text{ yield} = \frac{32.4}{36.0} \times 100 = \mathbf{90.0\%}$$

**Example 3 — Solution stoichiometry (Krug 4.5c–d):**
What volume of $0.200\ M\ \text{KMnO}_4$ is needed to react with $25.0\ \text{mL}$ of $0.150\ M\ \text{FeSO}_4$? (Ratio: 1 MnO₄⁻ : 5 Fe²⁺)

$$n_{\text{Fe}^{2+}} = (0.150)(0.0250) = 3.75 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}$$
$$n_{\text{MnO}_4^-} = \frac{3.75 \times 10^{-3}}{5} = 7.50 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{mol}$$
$$V = \frac{n}{M} = \frac{7.50 \times 10^{-4}}{0.200} = \mathbf{3.75 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{L} = 3.75\ \text{mL}}$$

**Example 4 — Doubling one reactant when originally stoichiometric (Progress Check Q12):**
Reaction: $\text{K}_2\text{SO}_3(aq) + 2\text{HNO}_3(aq) \rightarrow 2\text{KNO}_3(aq) + \text{SO}_2(g) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$

Original: 100.0 mL of 0.100 M K₂SO₃ + 100.0 mL of 0.200 M HNO₃.

$$n_{\text{K}_2\text{SO}_3} = 0.0100\ \text{mol}, \quad n_{\text{HNO}_3} = 0.0200\ \text{mol}$$

Ratio 1:2 exactly → both stoichiometric → yield = 0.0100 mol SO₂.

**Scenario: Double only K₂SO₃ (200 mL of 0.100 M):**
New $n_{\text{K}_2\text{SO}_3} = 0.0200$ mol. Needs $0.0200 \times 2 = 0.0400$ mol HNO₃. Only 0.0200 available → HNO₃ is now LR.
Yield from HNO₃: $0.0200 \times \frac{1}{2} = 0.0100$ mol SO₂. **Unchanged.** No gain.

**Scenario: Double both:**
$n = 0.0200$ mol K₂SO₃ and $0.0400$ mol HNO₃ → both doubled, ratio 1:2 preserved → yield = **0.0200 mol SO₂.** Doubled.

*Key move: When the original reaction is stoichiometric, doubling one reactant only creates a new limiting reactant. Yield doubles only when BOTH reactants are doubled in the correct ratio.*

**Example 5 — Gas stoichiometry at constant V and T (Krug 4.5a):**
Methanol decomposes: $\text{CH}_3\text{OH}(g) \rightarrow \text{CO}(g) + 2\text{H}_2(g)$

At 600 K in a rigid vessel, $P(\text{CH}_3\text{OH}) = 1.74\ \text{atm}$ initially. What is the final pressure after complete reaction?

$1\ \text{mol CH}_3\text{OH} \rightarrow 1\ \text{mol CO} + 2\ \text{mol H}_2 = 3\ \text{mol products}$

At constant $V$ and $T$: $P \propto n$. Pressure triples: $P_{\text{final}} = 3 \times 1.74 = \mathbf{5.22\ \text{atm}}$

## Topic 4.6: Titration

**Example 1 — Standard acid–base titration (Farabaugh):**
A 1.25 g sample of KHP ($M = 204.22$ g/mol, monoprotic) titrated with NaOH. At endpoint: 36.00 mL NaOH added.

$$n_{\text{KHP}} = \frac{1.25}{204.22} = 6.12 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}$$
$$n_{\text{NaOH}} = n_{\text{KHP}} = 6.12 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}\ \text{(1:1 ratio)}$$
$$M_{\text{NaOH}} = \frac{6.12 \times 10^{-3}}{0.03600} = \mathbf{0.170\ M}$$

**Example 2 — Buret error: water rinse (Farabaugh Progress Check Q13):**
Student rinses buret with distilled water instead of KMnO₄ before filling. Effect:

- Residual water in buret dilutes the KMnO₄ solution.
- More volume of diluted KMnO₄ is needed to reach the endpoint (pink color persists).
- Student records $n_{\text{KMnO}_4} = M_{\text{listed}} \times V_{\text{used}}$. Since $M_{\text{listed}}$ is higher than the actual diluted concentration, the calculation overestimates moles of KMnO₄.
- Calculated [Fe²⁺] is **too high**.

*Key move: Diluted titrant → more volume needed → moles of titrant overestimated → calculated analyte concentration too high. The buret error always inflates the result.*

**Example 3 — Analyte flask rinsed with water (Farabaugh — the trap):**
Student rinses the Erlenmeyer flask containing the analyte with distilled water before starting.

Effect: **None on the calculated concentration.** Titration measures moles of analyte (fixed by the mass/volume initially added), not concentration of analyte in the flask. Adding water to the analyte flask changes the volume but not the moles of analyte. The equivalence point is reached at the same volume of titrant.

*Key move: Only buret errors (affecting titrant concentration or volume) change the calculated result. Flask rinse with water = no effect.*

**Example 4 — Back titration: CaCO₃ content (Krug 4.6, Farabaugh):**
A 0.500 g mixture containing CaCO₃ (and inert impurities) is reacted with 50.00 mL of 0.200 M HCl (excess). The remaining HCl is back-titrated with 0.100 M NaOH, requiring 10.00 mL to reach the equivalence point. Determine the percent CaCO₃ in the mixture.

Reaction: $\text{CaCO}_3(s) + 2\text{HCl}(aq) \rightarrow \text{CaCl}_2(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \text{CO}_2(g)$ (CaCO₃ : HCl = 1 : 2)

Back-titration: $\text{HCl}(aq) + \text{NaOH}(aq) \rightarrow \text{NaCl}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$ (HCl : NaOH = 1 : 1)

**Step 1 — moles HCl added (excess):**
$$n_{\text{HCl, added}} = (0.200\ \text{M})(0.05000\ \text{L}) = 0.0100\ \text{mol}$$

**Step 2 — moles HCl remaining (from back-titration with NaOH):**
$$n_{\text{NaOH}} = (0.100\ \text{M})(0.01000\ \text{L}) = 0.00100\ \text{mol}$$
Since HCl and NaOH react 1:1:
$$n_{\text{HCl, remaining}} = 0.00100\ \text{mol}$$

**Step 3 — moles HCl that reacted with CaCO₃:**
$$n_{\text{HCl, reacted}} = 0.0100 - 0.00100 = 0.00900\ \text{mol}$$

**Step 4 — moles CaCO₃ (using 2:1 mole ratio, HCl : CaCO₃):**
$$n_{\text{CaCO}_3} = \frac{0.00900\ \text{mol HCl}}{2} = 4.50 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}$$

**Step 5 — mass of CaCO₃:**
$$m_{\text{CaCO}_3} = (4.50 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol})(100.09\ \text{g/mol}) = 0.450\ \text{g}$$

**Step 6 — percent CaCO₃:**
$$\% \text{CaCO}_3 = \frac{0.450\ \text{g}}{0.500\ \text{g}} \times 100 = \mathbf{90.0\%}$$

*Key move: (1) Calculate total moles of excess reagent added. (2) Calculate moles remaining from back-titration. (3) Subtract to find moles that reacted with the analyte. (4) Apply the mole ratio from the balanced equation to find moles of analyte. (5) Convert to mass, then percent. The back-titration step measures what was left over — not what reacted.*

## Topic 4.7: Types of Chemical Reactions

**Example 1 — Identify reaction type and write NIE:**

| Reaction | Type | NIE |
|---|---|---|
| $\text{AgNO}_3(aq) + \text{KBr}(aq) \rightarrow \text{AgBr}(s) + \text{KNO}_3(aq)$ | Precipitation | $\text{Ag}^+ + \text{Br}^- \rightarrow \text{AgBr}(s)$ |
| $\text{HNO}_3(aq) + \text{KOH}(aq) \rightarrow \text{KNO}_3(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$ | Acid–base | $\text{H}^+ + \text{OH}^- \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}$ |
| $\text{Zn}(s) + 2\text{HCl}(aq) \rightarrow \text{ZnCl}_2(aq) + \text{H}_2(g)$ | Redox (single replacement) | $\text{Zn}(s) + 2\text{H}^+(aq) \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+}(aq) + \text{H}_2(g)$ |
| $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3(aq) + 2\text{HCl}(aq) \rightarrow 2\text{NaCl}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \text{CO}_2(g)$ | Gas-forming | $\text{CO}_3^{2-} + 2\text{H}^+ \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2(g)$ |

**Example 2 — Activity series: will the reaction occur? (Krug 4.7a, Farabaugh):**

| Reaction | Prediction | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| $\text{Zn}(s) + \text{CuSO}_4(aq) \rightarrow ?$ | ✓ Reaction | Zn is above Cu on activity series → Zn displaces Cu²⁺ |
| $\text{Cu}(s) + \text{ZnSO}_4(aq) \rightarrow ?$ | ✗ NR | Cu is below Zn → Cu cannot displace Zn²⁺ |
| $\text{Fe}(s) + 2\text{HCl}(aq) \rightarrow ?$ | ✓ Reaction | Fe is above H₂ → reacts with acid, produces H₂ |
| $\text{Cu}(s) + 2\text{HCl}(aq) \rightarrow ?$ | ✗ NR | Cu is below H₂ → does not react with dilute HCl |
| $\text{Ag}(s) + \text{FeSO}_4(aq) \rightarrow ?$ | ✗ NR | Ag is below Fe on the activity series |
| $\text{Mg}(s) + \text{AgNO}_3(aq) \rightarrow ?$ | ✓ Reaction | Mg is far above Ag → vigorous displacement |

For $\text{Mg}(s) + 2\text{AgNO}_3(aq) \rightarrow \text{Mg(NO}_3)_2(aq) + 2\text{Ag}(s)$:
NIE: $\boxed{\text{Mg}(s) + 2\text{Ag}^+(aq) \rightarrow \text{Mg}^{2+}(aq) + 2\text{Ag}(s)}$

*Key move: Look up both metals on the activity series. If the free metal is higher than the ion's metal, reaction occurs. If lower, NR. For metal + acid: if metal is above H₂, H₂ gas is produced.*

**Example 3 — Halogen activity series (Krug 4.9b, Farabaugh):**
$\text{Cl}_2(aq) + 2\text{KBr}(aq) \rightarrow ?$

Cl₂ is above Br₂ on halogen activity series (F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂). Cl₂ displaces Br⁻ from solution.

Molecular: $\text{Cl}_2(aq) + 2\text{KBr}(aq) \rightarrow 2\text{KCl}(aq) + \text{Br}_2(aq)$
NIE: $\boxed{\text{Cl}_2(aq) + 2\text{Br}^-(aq) \rightarrow 2\text{Cl}^-(aq) + \text{Br}_2(aq)}$

Reverse: $\text{Br}_2(aq) + 2\text{KCl}(aq) \rightarrow$ NR — Br₂ cannot displace Cl⁻ (Cl₂ > Br₂).

**Example 4 — Oxidation number assignment (Krug 4.7b):**

| Species | Element | ON | Work |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$ | Cr | +6 | O = −2 × 7 = −14; charge = −2; 2Cr − 14 = −2 → Cr = +6 |
| $\text{MnO}_4^-$ | Mn | +7 | O = −8; charge = −1; Mn − 8 = −1 → Mn = +7 |
| $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ | O | −1 | Peroxide; H = +1 each; 2(+1) + 2O = 0 → O = −1 |
| NaH | H | −1 | Metal hydride; Na = +1; H = −1 |
| $\text{OF}_2$ | O | +2 | F = −1 always; 2(−1) + O = 0 → O = +2 |

## Topic 4.8: Acid–Base Reactions

**Example 1 — Conjugate acid–base pairs from a reaction (Farabaugh Progress Check Q16):**
Reaction: $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COOH}(aq) + \text{NaOH}(aq) \rightarrow \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COONa}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$

Step 1 — Identify the proton transfer: C₆H₅COOH donates H⁺ to OH⁻.
Step 2 — Pair each species with its conjugate:
- Acid: $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COOH}$ → loses H⁺ → Conjugate base: $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COO}^-$ ✓
- Base: $\text{OH}^-$ → gains H⁺ → Conjugate acid: $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ ✓

**NOT a conjugate pair:** $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COOH}$ and $\text{OH}^-$ — these are the reactant acid and base.

*Key move: A conjugate acid-base pair is always one species from the reactant side paired with one species from the product side. Reactant ↔ product, never reactant ↔ reactant.*

**Example 2 — Amphoteric species: HS⁻ (Farabaugh Progress Check Q18):**
$\text{HS}^-$ acts as BOTH:

As an acid: $\text{HS}^-(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq) \rightarrow \text{S}^{2-}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$ (donates H⁺ to OH⁻)
As a base: $\text{HS}^-(aq) + \text{H}^+(aq) \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{S}(aq)$ (accepts H⁺)

Why CH₃COO⁻ is NOT amphoteric: it can only accept H⁺ (acts as a base to form CH₃COOH). It has no ionizable H to donate as an acid.

*Key move: Test for amphoteric: (1) "Can it lose H⁺?" — needs an ionizable H. (2) "Can it gain H⁺?" — needs a lone pair. Both must be yes. CH₃COO⁻ fails step 1.*

**Example 3 — Lewis acid–base identification (Krug 4.8, Farabaugh):**

Reaction: $\text{BF}_3 + \text{NH}_3 \rightarrow \text{BF}_3\text{NH}_3$

Identify Lewis acid and Lewis base.
- BF₃: B has only 6 electrons (electron-deficient). It has an **empty p orbital** → accepts electron pair → **Lewis acid**.
- NH₃: N has a lone pair → donates electron pair to B → **Lewis base**.

This is NOT a Brønsted–Lowry reaction — no H⁺ is transferred. It is purely a Lewis acid-base reaction.

Identify the resulting bond: the N lone pair forms a **coordinate (dative) covalent bond** to B.

*Key move: Lewis acid = empty orbital or electron-deficient center (accepts electrons). Lewis base = lone pair available (donates electrons). No proton transfer is necessary.*

**Example 4 — Lewis acid–base with metal ion (Farabaugh):**
$\text{Al}^{3+}(aq) + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \rightarrow [\text{Al(H}_2\text{O})_6]^{3+}(aq)$

Al³⁺ is a cation with empty orbitals → **Lewis acid** (accepts lone pairs from O).
H₂O has lone pairs on O → **Lewis base** (donates lone pairs to Al³⁺).

This explains why many metal cations form complex ions in solution — they are Lewis acids coordinated by Lewis base ligands (H₂O, NH₃, Cl⁻, CN⁻).

*Key move: All cations in solution are Lewis acids (they accept lone pairs from water). All species with lone pairs that coordinate to metal ions are Lewis bases.*

**Example 5 — HCO₃⁻ amphoteric reactions (Progress Check Q19):**
$\text{HCO}_3^-$ in two different reactions:

As acid (donates H⁺ to OH⁻):
$$\text{HCO}_3^-(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq) \rightarrow \text{CO}_3^{2-}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$

As base (accepts H⁺ from HCl):
$$\text{HCO}_3^-(aq) + \text{H}^+(aq) \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{CO}_3(aq) \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \text{CO}_2(g)$$

The same ion acts oppositely depending on whether a stronger acid or stronger base is present. This is the hallmark of an amphoteric substance.

## Topic 4.9: Oxidation–Reduction Reactions

**Example 1 — Identify oxidized/reduced species:**
$\ce{Zn(s) + CuSO4(aq) -> ZnSO4(aq) + Cu(s)}$

- Zn: $0 \rightarrow +2$ (ON increases → **oxidized** → Zn is the **reducing agent**)
- Cu: $+2 \rightarrow 0$ (ON decreases → **reduced** → Cu²⁺ is the **oxidizing agent**)

**Example 2 — Balance redox in acidic solution (Farabaugh Cr₂O₇²⁻ + H₂O₂):**
$\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}_2(aq) \xrightarrow{\text{H}^+} \text{Cr}^{3+}(aq) + \text{O}_2(g)$

Reduction: $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 14\text{H}^+ + 6e^- \rightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+} + 7\text{H}_2\text{O}$
Oxidation: $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{O}_2 + 2\text{H}^+ + 2e^-$ (× 3 to cancel 6e⁻)

Combined: $\boxed{\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}(aq) + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}_2(aq) + 8\text{H}^+(aq) \rightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+}(aq) + 3\text{O}_2(g) + 7\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)}$

Check: Cr: 2=2 ✓; O: 7+6=13=7+6 ✓; H: 6+8=14=14 ✓; charge: −2+8=+6; +6=2(+3) ✓

**Example 3 — Balance redox in basic solution (Krug 4.9c, Farabaugh):**
$\text{MnO}_4^-(aq) + \text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-}(aq) \rightarrow \text{MnO}_2(s) + \text{CO}_2(g)$ in basic solution.

Step 1 — Split and balance acidically:

Reduction: Mn goes from +7 → +4.
$\text{MnO}_4^- + 3e^- \rightarrow \text{MnO}_2$
Balance O: add 2H₂O to right: $\text{MnO}_4^- + 3e^- \rightarrow \text{MnO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$
Balance H: add 4H⁺ to left: $\text{MnO}_4^- + 4\text{H}^+ + 3e^- \rightarrow \text{MnO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$
Charge check: left = −1+4−3 = 0; right = 0 ✓

Oxidation: C goes from +3 → +4 in CO₂.
$\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-} \rightarrow 2\text{CO}_2 + 2e^-$
Charge check: left = −2; right = 0−2 = −2 ✓

Step 2 — LCM: 3e⁻ and 2e⁻ → LCM = 6. Multiply reduction × 2, oxidation × 3:
$2\text{MnO}_4^- + 8\text{H}^+ + 6e^- \rightarrow 2\text{MnO}_2 + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$
$3\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-} \rightarrow 6\text{CO}_2 + 6e^-$

Step 3 — Combine (acidic):
$2\text{MnO}_4^- + 3\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-} + 8\text{H}^+ \rightarrow 2\text{MnO}_2 + 6\text{CO}_2 + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$

Step 4 — Convert to basic: 8 H⁺ present → add 8 OH⁻ to BOTH sides.
Left: $8\text{H}^+ + 8\text{OH}^- = 8\text{H}_2\text{O}$
Right: $4\text{H}_2\text{O} + 8\text{OH}^-$

After combining left: $8\text{H}_2\text{O}$ on left.
Equation: $2\text{MnO}_4^- + 3\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-} + 8\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 2\text{MnO}_2 + 6\text{CO}_2 + 4\text{H}_2\text{O} + 8\text{OH}^-$

Step 5 — Cancel 4 H₂O from both sides:

$$\boxed{2\text{MnO}_4^-(aq) + 3\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-}(aq) + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \rightarrow 2\text{MnO}_2(s) + 6\text{CO}_2(g) + 8\text{OH}^-(aq)}$$

Check: Mn: 2=2 ✓; C: 6=6 ✓; O: 8+12+4=24=2(2)+6(2)+8 = 4+12+8=24 ✓; H: 8=8 ✓; charge: −2−6+0=−8; right=0+0−8=−8 ✓

*Key move: Always balance for acidic first, then neutralize the H⁺ by adding OH⁻ to both sides. Combine H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O where they meet, then cancel any H₂O on both sides. Never add OH⁻ at the start.*

**Example 4 — Combining given half-reactions with LCM (Farabaugh Progress Check Q20):**
Given:
- Oxidation: $\text{Al}(s) \rightarrow \text{Al}^{3+}(aq) + 3e^-$
- Reduction: $\text{Sn}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^- \rightarrow \text{Sn}(s)$

LCM(3, 2) = 6. Multiply oxidation × 2, reduction × 3:

$2\text{Al}(s) \rightarrow 2\text{Al}^{3+}(aq) + 6e^-$
$3\text{Sn}^{2+}(aq) + 6e^- \rightarrow 3\text{Sn}(s)$

$$\boxed{2\text{Al}(s) + 3\text{Sn}^{2+}(aq) \rightarrow 2\text{Al}^{3+}(aq) + 3\text{Sn}(s)}$$

Check: Al: 2=2 ✓; Sn: 3=3 ✓; charge: +6=+6 ✓; 6e⁻ cancelled ✓

**Example 5 — Disproportionation reaction (Krug 4.9b, Farabaugh):**
$2\text{H}_2\text{O}_2(l) \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \text{O}_2(g)$

Assign ON to O:
- H₂O₂: O = −1 (peroxide).
- H₂O: O = −2. (decreased → O is **reduced**)
- O₂: O = 0. (increased → O is **oxidized**)

Same element (O) in the same reactant species (H₂O₂) is simultaneously oxidized (−1 → 0) and reduced (−1 → −2). This is a **disproportionation reaction.**

H₂O₂ is both the oxidizing agent AND the reducing agent.

*Key move: To identify a disproportionation, find a single reactant species whose element appears in two products with different (one higher, one lower) oxidation numbers than in the reactant. If that element only comes from one reactant, it must be disproportionating.*

**Example 6 — AP 2021 FRQ style: Fe²⁺ titration error analysis (Farabaugh):**
Student titrates Fe²⁺(aq) with 0.0425 M KMnO₄. In Trial 2, extra titrant is added past the endpoint.

Effect: Extra moles of KMnO₄ are recorded. Using the 1:5 stoichiometric ratio (1 MnO₄⁻ : 5 Fe²⁺), the calculated moles of Fe²⁺ = 5 × (moles KMnO₄). If moles KMnO₄ is inflated, calculated [Fe²⁺] is **too high** compared to Trial 1.

This explains why Trial 2 yields a higher concentration value — the error is in overshooting the equivalence point.
