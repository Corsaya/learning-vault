## Topic 3.1: Intermolecular Forces

![[imf_ranking_u3.svg|697]]
*IMF types ranked weakest to strongest — use this to reason through boiling point and solubility comparisons.*

**Example 1 — Farabaugh boiling point comparison (pentane vs. 2,2-dimethylpropane):**
Both are $\text{C}_5\text{H}_{12}$ (same molecular formula, same number of electrons). Pentane BP = 309 K; 2,2-dimethylpropane BP = 283 K.

Why? Pentane is a linear chain → more surface area contact between molecules → stronger LDFs. 2,2-dimethylpropane is spherical/compact → less surface contact area → weaker LDFs → lower BP.

*Key move: When molar mass and electron count are equal, surface area determines LDF strength. Linear > branched.*

---

**Example 2 — Farabaugh CCl₄ vs. HCl (real gas data overrides prediction):**
$\text{CCl}_4$: nonpolar (large electron cloud, M = 154 g/mol), LDF only. $\text{HCl}$: polar (smaller, M = 36.5 g/mol), LDF + dipole–dipole.

At 340 K: $\text{CCl}_4$ condenses (liquid), $\text{HCl}$ remains gas. Therefore $\text{CCl}_4$ has higher BP and stronger IMFs — the LDF from the much larger electron cloud dominates over $\text{HCl}$'s added dipole–dipole.

*Key move: When a nonpolar molecule is much larger than a polar one, its LDFs can be stronger. Never assume "polar = stronger IMF" without considering electron count and molar mass.*

---

**Example 3 — H-bond donor/acceptor classification (Farabaugh):**

| Substance | H-bond donor? | H-bond acceptor? | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\text{NH}_3$ | Yes | Yes | H bonded to N (donor); N has lone pair (acceptor) |
| $\text{NH}_4^+$ | Yes | **No** | H bonded to N (donor); N has NO lone pair (4 bonds used) |
| $\text{NCl}_3$ | No | Yes | H NOT bonded to N; N has lone pair |
| $\text{NH}_2\text{Cl}$ | Yes | Yes | H bonded to N (donor); N has lone pair (acceptor) |
| $\text{CH}_3\text{F}$ | No | No | H bonded to C, not F; cannot donate. F lone pairs present but no donor |
| $\text{HF}$ | Yes | Yes | H bonded to F (donor); F has lone pairs (acceptor) |

*Key move: Check two things: (1) is H covalently bonded to N/O/F? → donor. (2) Does N/O/F have a lone pair available? → acceptor. Both must be true for H-bonding.*

---

**Example 4 — Anomalous boiling points, Group 15–17 series (Farabaugh):**

| Set | Trend (expected) | Exception at top of group |
|---|---|---|
| $\text{NH}_3, \text{PH}_3, \text{AsH}_3, \text{SbH}_3$ | BP increases down group (larger electron cloud) | $\text{NH}_3$: anomalously HIGH BP — H-bonding |
| $\text{H}_2\text{O}, \text{H}_2\text{S}, \text{H}_2\text{Se}, \text{H}_2\text{Te}$ | Same pattern | $\text{H}_2\text{O}$: anomalously high — H-bonding |
| $\text{HF}, \text{HCl}, \text{HBr}, \text{HI}$ | Same pattern | HF: anomalously high — H-bonding |

The three anomalous substances: **$\text{NH}_3$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, HF** — all have H bonded to N, O, or F.

*Key move: If asked "which substance breaks the expected trend in its group?" — identify the one at the top of the group (smallest, lightest). If it has H on N/O/F, its H-bonding makes it higher than expected.*

---

**Example 5 — Ion–dipole comparison using Coulomb's law (Farabaugh and Progress Check Q2):**
Four cations: $\text{Li}^+$ (charge +1, smallest group 1), $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ (charge +2, Period 3), $\text{Na}^+$ (charge +1), $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ (charge +2, Period 4). Which has the strongest ion–dipole attraction to water?

Step 1 — Compare charges: $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ and $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ both have charge $+2$; $\text{Li}^+$ and $\text{Na}^+$ have charge $+1$. The $2+$ ions immediately beat the $1+$ ions by Coulomb's law ($F \propto q$).

Step 2 — Among the $2+$ ions: $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ is in Period 3 (smaller), $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ is in Period 4 (larger). Smaller radius → stronger force.

**Answer:** $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ has the highest charge-to-size ratio → strongest ion–dipole force with water.

*Key move: Always rank charge first. A $2+$ ion beats a $1+$ ion nearly always. Use radius as tiebreaker only within the same charge group.*

---

**Example 6 — Methanol in water — what is the major attractive force? (Progress Check Q22):**
$\text{CH}_3\text{OH}$ dissolved in water; the solution does not conduct electricity.

Both methanol and water have O–H groups → H-bond donors. Both have lone pairs on O → H-bond acceptors. The major attractive force between methanol molecules and water molecules is **hydrogen bonding** (H bonded to O of one molecule attracted to the lone pair on O of the other molecule).

The diagram showing the O lone pair of methanol accepting the H from water's O–H group represents this correctly.

*Key move: Methanol + water = both polar, both capable of H-bonding. Solution non-conductivity confirms no ions; the force is H-bonding, not ion–dipole.*

---

**Example 7 — Reading an ion hydration shell diagram (Progress Check Q3):**
A group 1 metal + group 17 halide dissolves in water. A particle diagram shows a large central particle surrounded by water molecules. The small H atoms of each water molecule point AWAY from the central particle; the O atoms point INWARD.

Step 1 — Identify which end of water faces the ion: O end ($\delta^-$) points inward → by Coulomb's law, opposite charges attract → the ion must be **positive** (cation, e.g., $\text{Na}^+$ or $\text{K}^+$).

Step 2 — Name the force: ion + polar molecule = **ion–dipole**. Not hydrogen bonding (the ion has no H directly bonded to N/O/F). Not dipole–dipole (the ion has a full charge, not a partial charge).

**Answer:** Positive ion, ion–dipole interaction.

**Diagram-reading rule (always apply):**

| Water orientation | Ion charge | Force |
|---|---|---|
| O end ($\delta^-$) points toward ion | Positive ($\text{M}^+$, $\text{M}^{2+}$) | Ion–dipole |
| H end ($\delta^+$) points toward ion | Negative ($\text{X}^-$) | Ion–dipole |

*Key move: Read the diagram first — which atom of water faces the central particle? O inward = positive ion. H inward = negative ion. The force is always ion–dipole, never H-bonding.*

---

**Example 8 — H-bonding is the strongest IMF when O–H or N–H is present (Progress Check Q1):**
Two ethanol ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$) molecules interact. Which IMF is strongest?

Step 1 — Check for H-bonding: ethanol has an O–H group. H is directly bonded to O → can donate an H-bond. The O on another ethanol has lone pairs → can accept. H-bonding is possible.

Step 2 — Compare available IMFs: LDF (all molecules), dipole–dipole (ethanol is polar), H-bonding (O–H present). H-bonding > dipole–dipole > LDF.

Step 3 — Select the correct diagram: the diagram showing the O–H of one molecule pointing at the O lone pair of the second molecule (O–H···O geometry) represents H-bonding. This is the strongest force.

**Common trap:** choosing "dipole–dipole" because both molecules are polar. Dipole–dipole exists, but H-bonding is STRONGER and is the correct answer when O–H or N–H is present. The question asks for the strongest IMF — always go as high as possible on the IMF hierarchy.

*Key move: If H is bonded to N, O, or F → H-bonding is the strongest IMF between those molecules, not dipole–dipole. Don't stop halfway up the IMF hierarchy.*

## Topic 3.2: Properties of Solids

![[solids_types_u3.svg|697]]
*Rapid identification table for all four solid types. Memorize conductivity patterns — they are always tested.*

**Example 1 — Farabaugh solid classification from experimental data:**

| Solid | MP | Conductivity | Solubility | Identity | Type |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 80°C | Nonelectrolyte | Slightly soluble | Naphthalene ($\text{C}_{10}\text{H}_8$) | Molecular |
| B | 1414°C | Nonelectrolyte | Insoluble | Silicon (Si) | Covalent network |
| C | 770°C | Conducts when dissolved | Very soluble | KCl | Ionic |
| D | 1455°C | Conducts as solid | Insoluble | Nickel (Ni) | Metallic |

*Key move: The four-step identification: (1) Very high MP + never conducts → network. (2) High MP + conducts when dissolved → ionic. (3) Low MP + never conducts → molecular. (4) Conducts as solid → metallic.*

---

**Example 2 — SiH₄ vs. SiO₂ (Krug 3.2):**
$\text{SiH}_4$: molecular solid, IMFs only (LDF) → BP = 161 K, MP = 88 K. Very low.
$\text{SiO}_2$: covalent network solid, covalent Si–O bonds throughout 3D lattice → MP = 1986 K, BP = 3220 K.

Both contain Si, but the bonding is completely different. The Si–O covalent bonds in $\text{SiO}_2$ extend throughout the entire lattice; melting requires breaking ALL of them, requiring extreme energy. $\text{SiH}_4$ only requires overcoming weak Si–H LDFs between molecules.

*Key move: Never use IMF reasoning for a covalent network solid. The particle interactions are covalent bonds, not IMFs.*

---

**Example 3 — Two allotropes of phosphorus (Progress Check Q4):**
Allotrope I: discrete $\text{P}_4$ tetrahedral molecules → molecular solid held by LDF between $\text{P}_4$ units.
Allotrope II: extended 3D covalent network with P–P bonds throughout the lattice.

Which has higher MP? Allotrope II. Melting requires breaking covalent P–P bonds throughout the lattice. Allotrope I only requires overcoming LDFs between $\text{P}_4$ molecules.

The trap: $\text{P}_4$ molecules contain covalent P–P bonds, but those are *intramolecular* — they are NOT broken during melting. Only the intermolecular LDFs between $\text{P}_4$ units are broken.

*Key move: Melting always breaks inter-particle forces only. In a molecular solid, that means IMFs. In a network solid, that means covalent bonds. Never confuse the two.*

---

**Example 4 — Conductivity vs. mass graph for ionic solid (Progress Check Q23):**
A student dissolves increasing masses of an unknown brittle solid in water. Conductivity increases linearly with mass added.

What CAN be concluded:
- The solid is ionic (releases mobile ions when dissolved → conducts).
- Ion concentration is proportional to mass dissolved in the tested range.
- The solid dissociates into ions in water (strong electrolyte behavior).

What CANNOT be concluded:
- The solid has infinite solubility — the graph only shows the tested range. Saturation may occur beyond the data.
- The solid is a covalent network compound — those don't ionize in water.
- The solid is metallic — metals don't dissolve to give ion-conducting solutions like this.

*Key move: Linear conductivity vs. mass → ionic. Does NOT imply infinite solubility. The graph ends before saturation is tested.*

---

**Example 5 — Diamond vs. graphite hardness (Progress Check Q6):**
Diamond: each C forms 4 covalent bonds in a 3D tetrahedral network → rigid, extremely hard.
Graphite: C forms 3 covalent bonds within planar hexagonal layers; layers held to each other by weak LDFs.

Why diamond is harder: deforming diamond's structure requires breaking strong C–C covalent bonds in all directions. Graphite's layers slide easily over each other because the inter-layer forces are only LDFs.

Trap: Diamond does NOT have delocalized electrons. It is a non-conductor. Graphite DOES have delocalized electrons within its layers (which is why it conducts along the layers).

*Key move: Hardness in network solids = how strong and extensive the covalent bonding network is. Conductivity in graphite comes from delocalized π electrons within layers — not from breaking the layers apart.*

## Topic 3.3: Solids, Liquids, and Gases

**Example 1 — Phase comparison MCQ (Krug 3.3):**
Which is true about the particles in liquid water compared to ice?
- In ice: $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecules locked in hexagonal lattice structure by H-bonds; vibrational motion only.
- In liquid water: $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecules still close together and still H-bonding, but now free to move past each other; translational and rotational motion.
- In both phases, the intramolecular O–H covalent bonds remain intact. Only the H-bonds between molecules are broken/reformed.
- Liquid water has slightly higher density than ice (unusual — the hexagonal lattice in ice has open pockets; liquid water fills those).

*Key move: Phase changes involve breaking/forming IMFs only, not intramolecular bonds. The same covalent molecule exists in all phases.*

## Topic 3.4: Ideal Gas Law

![[gaslaws_separation_u3.svg|697]]
*Gas law relationships (left) and separation method decision tree (right).*

**Example 1 — Farabaugh molar mass from gas density:**
A pure gas has density $d = 1.31\ \text{g/L}$ at 1.00 atm, 25.0°C (298 K).

$$M = \frac{dRT}{P} = \frac{(1.31\ \text{g/L})(0.08206\ \text{L·atm/mol·K})(298\ \text{K})}{1.00\ \text{atm}} = \frac{32.0\ \text{g·atm/mol}}{1.00\ \text{atm}} = 32.0\ \text{g/mol}$$

Identity: $\text{O}_2$ (32.0 g/mol) ✓

*Key move: $M = dRT/P$ — density, temperature, and pressure all given → molar mass directly. Always convert T to Kelvin.*

---

**Example 2 — Farabaugh water displacement (Dalton's Law):**
Butane gas collected over water. $T = 22°\text{C}$, $P_{\text{atm}} = 755.0\ \text{torr}$. $P_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}$ at 22°C = 19.83 torr. Volume collected = 100.0 mL. Mass of lighter decreased by 0.22 g.

$$P_{\text{butane}} = 755.0 - 19.83 = 735.2\ \text{torr} = \frac{735.2}{760} = 0.9674\ \text{atm}$$

$$n = \frac{PV}{RT} = \frac{(0.9674\ \text{atm})(0.1000\ \text{L})}{(0.08206\ \text{L·atm/mol·K})(295\ \text{K})} = 3.99 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}$$

$$M = \frac{0.22\ \text{g}}{3.99 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}} = 55.1\ \text{g/mol} \quad (\text{actual } \text{C}_4\text{H}_{10} = 58.1\ \text{g/mol};\ \%\text{error} \approx 5\%)$$

*Key move: Always subtract water vapor pressure first. Then use $n = PV/RT$ with the corrected gas pressure and T in Kelvin.*

---

**Example 3 — Gay-Lussac's Law with Kelvin conversion (Progress Check Q8):**
$\text{O}_2$ at 10°C, 2.1 atm in rigid container → heated to 40°C. Find new pressure.

Convert: $T_1 = 283\ \text{K}$, $T_2 = 313\ \text{K}$ (adding 273, not 270 — use precise values).

$$P_2 = P_1 \times \frac{T_2}{T_1} = 2.1\ \text{atm} \times \frac{313}{283} = 2.32\ \text{atm} \approx 2.3\ \text{atm}$$

This matches answer B. The pressure increased because temperature increased (Gay-Lussac: $P \propto T$ at constant V, n).

Trap: using Celsius: $2.1 \times (40/10) = 8.4\ \text{atm}$ — wildly wrong. Always Kelvin.

*Key move: For any $P$–$T$ or $V$–$T$ calculation, convert both temperatures to Kelvin before setting up the ratio.*

---

**Example 4 — Boyle's Law engine cylinder (Progress Check Q7):**
Air-fuel mixture at 600 mL, 1.0 atm compressed to 50 mL at constant T.

$$P_2 = P_1 \times \frac{V_1}{V_2} = 1.0\ \text{atm} \times \frac{600\ \text{mL}}{50\ \text{mL}} = 1.0 \times 12 = 12\ \text{atm}$$

Volume ratio: $600/50 = 12$ (not 120, not 550). Pressure increases by factor of 12.

*Key move: In Boyle's Law, identify the ratio $V_1/V_2$. Pressure and volume are inversely proportional — if volume decreases by factor $k$, pressure increases by factor $k$.*

---

**Example 5 — Dalton's Law: two containers combined (Progress Check Q9):**
Two rigid 5.0 L containers: $\text{O}_2$ at 0.8 atm, $\text{N}_2$ at 1.0 atm. Transfer all $\text{O}_2$ into the $\text{N}_2$ container (same 5.0 L volume).

By Dalton's Law: $P_{\text{total}} = P_{\text{O}_2} + P_{\text{N}_2}$. Since volume and temperature unchanged for each gas:
$$P_{\text{total}} = 0.8\ \text{atm} + 1.0\ \text{atm} = 1.8\ \text{atm}$$

Partial pressures add independently; each gas behaves as if it were alone in the container.

Trap: Thinking IMFs between different gas molecules would change the total pressure. Under ideal conditions, there are no IMFs — each gas contributes its partial pressure independently.

*Key move: Dalton's Law requires the same volume and temperature. If volume changes, use $P_i = n_i RT/V_{\text{new}}$ for each gas before summing.*

---

**Example 6 — Dalton's Law with mole fractions (Krug 3.4c):**
$10.0\ \text{L}$ of $\text{H}_2$ at 3.0 atm + 4.0 L of $\text{CH}_4$ at 2.0 atm, mixed into 14.0 L.

Each gas's partial pressure in the final mixture:
$$P_{\text{H}_2} = \frac{(3.0\ \text{atm})(10.0\ \text{L})}{14.0\ \text{L}} = 2.14\ \text{atm}$$

$$P_{\text{CH}_4} = \frac{(2.0\ \text{atm})(4.0\ \text{L})}{14.0\ \text{L}} = 0.571\ \text{atm}$$

$$P_{\text{total}} = 2.14 + 0.57 = 2.71\ \text{atm}$$

*Key move: When volumes change on mixing, use $P_i V_i = P_i' V_{\text{final}}$ to find new partial pressures (constant T, n for each gas). Then sum for total.*

## Topic 3.5: Kinetic Molecular Theory

![[maxwell_boltzmann_u3.svg|697]]
*Maxwell-Boltzmann curves — same T different mass (left) and same gas different T (right).*

**Example 1 — Labeling Maxwell-Boltzmann curves: two gases, same T (Farabaugh):**
Two curves at the same temperature for $\text{H}_2(g)$ and $\text{O}_2(g)$:
- Same temperature → same average KE.
- $\text{H}_2$ is lighter (M = 2) → higher average speed → **broader curve shifted right = $\text{H}_2$**.
- $\text{O}_2$ (M = 32) → slower → **narrower curve shifted left = $\text{O}_2$**.
- Area under each curve is equal (same number of molecules, same sample).

*Key move: At same T, lighter molecule → peak shifts right, lower, broader. Heavier molecule → peak shifts left, taller, narrower. Area always the same.*

---

**Example 2 — Same gas, two temperatures (Farabaugh):**
$\text{O}_2$ at 300 K vs. 600 K:
- Higher T → broader, flatter curve, peak shifted right = **600 K**.
- Lower T → narrower, taller curve, peak shifted left = **300 K**.
- Area under each curve equal (same number of molecules).
- The fraction of molecules with speed greater than some threshold $v$ is larger at 600 K.

*Key move: Higher T always gives the dashed line that is broader, lower peak, shifted right. If the dashed curve is taller, that is the LOWER temperature.*

---

**Example 3 — Same T, same gas, different diagram correctness (Progress Check Q10):**
Two diagrams: Diagram 1 has particles with arrows of varying lengths AND directions. Diagram 2 has particles with shorter, more uniform arrows.

At a fixed temperature, KMT says particles have a DISTRIBUTION of speeds (Maxwell-Boltzmann). Diagram 2 correctly shows varying arrow lengths = variety of speeds at fixed T.

Trap: Choosing the diagram where all arrows are the same length (one speed). Temperature determines average KE, not a single uniform speed. Only at T = 0 K would all particles be stationary.

*Key move: Fixed T = distribution of speeds, not a single speed. The diagram showing varied arrow lengths is always correct for KMT at any finite temperature.*

---

**Example 4 — Two gases same T: Gas Z vs. Gas X molar mass (Progress Check Q11):**
Gas X has a narrower, taller Maxwell-Boltzmann peak (peak shifted left). Gas Z has a broader, lower peak shifted right. Both at the same temperature.

Same T → same average KE. Gas Z peak is at higher speeds → Gas Z molecules are moving faster → Gas Z has lower molar mass.

$$KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{3}{2}RT \Rightarrow v \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}$$

Faster average speed at same KE → smaller $M$. Gas Z: smaller molar mass. Gas X: larger molar mass.

*Key move: Right-shifted peak = faster average speed = lower molar mass. Left-shifted peak = slower = heavier. Never confuse peak position with kinetic energy — those are independent.*

---

**Example 5 — FRQ-style KMT justification:**
A 1.0 mol sample of $\text{N}_2$ and a 1.0 mol sample of $\text{O}_2$ are placed in separate containers at 400 K. Compare their (a) average kinetic energies, (b) average speeds.

(a) Average KE: Same. $KE_{\text{avg}} = \frac{3}{2}RT$ depends only on temperature. Both gases are at 400 K → same average KE.

(b) Average speeds: $\text{N}_2$ faster. $\text{N}_2$ (M = 28 g/mol) is lighter than $\text{O}_2$ (M = 32 g/mol). Since $KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$ is equal, lighter $\text{N}_2$ must have higher average speed to maintain the same KE.

*Key move: Always state "same temperature → same average KE" first. Then use $v \propto 1/\sqrt{M}$ to compare speeds.*

---

**Example 6 — Which graph shows the same gas at higher T? Peak-direction trap (Progress Check Q12):**
A solid curve shows the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution for $\text{O}_2(g)$ at some temperature. Four dashed-line options are given for the distribution at a higher temperature.

What must be true at higher T:
1. Peak shifts **RIGHT** — higher average speed (more KE)
2. Peak height **decreases** — distribution spreads out; fewer molecules at the most probable speed
3. Curve is **broader** — wider range of speeds
4. Area under curve is **identical** — same number of $\text{O}_2$ molecules

Eliminate options immediately:
- Dashed curve taller than original → WRONG (taller = lower T, not higher)
- Dashed curve shifted LEFT → WRONG (left = slower = lower T)
- Dashed curve narrower → WRONG (narrower = lower T)

Correct option: dashed curve is right-shifted, broader, and lower-peaked than the solid curve.

**Trap:** Choosing the curve that shifts left and gets taller. This reverses the direction. Students sometimes think "higher T → peak goes up" because higher feels like more. But the area is conserved — a broader distribution MUST have a lower peak. And faster molecules = peak moves to the RIGHT side of the speed axis.

**Memory anchor:** "Hotter = faster = right. And if it spreads right, the peak drops."

*Key move: For any higher-T Maxwell-Boltzmann question: right-shifted + lower peak + broader + same area = correct. Reject any curve that is taller or left-shifted — those are lower-temperature curves.*

## Topic 3.6: Deviations from Ideal Gas Law

**Example 1 — Which gas deviates more at low T? (Krug 3.6, Farabaugh):**
$\text{CH}_4$ vs. $\text{NH}_3$: $\text{NH}_3$ has permanent dipole + H-bonding (H on N) → much stronger IMF. At low T, IMF attractions between $\text{NH}_3$ molecules become significant, reducing pressure below ideal. $\text{NH}_3$ deviates more at low T.

*Key move: Low T deviation → stronger IMF → more deviation. Identify the substance with the strongest intermolecular forces.*

---

**Example 2 — Which gas deviates more at high P?**
He vs. Xe: Xe is a much larger atom → larger physical volume. At high pressure, Xe's actual molecular volume is a significant fraction of the container volume → deviates more from ideal.

Among $\text{H}_2$, $\text{N}_2$, Ne, $\text{SO}_2$ (Progress Check Q15): $\text{SO}_2$ (M = 64 g/mol) is the largest → greatest molecular volume → most deviation at high P. The real gas graph showing pressure HIGHER than ideal indicates particle volume domination.

*Key move: High P deviation → larger molecular volume → more deviation. Find the heaviest, largest molecule.*

---

**Example 3 — Why C₂H₆ deviates more than CH₄ (Progress Check Q13):**
Both are nonpolar hydrocarbons with only LDFs. Same T, identical containers.

$\text{C}_2\text{H}_6$ (M = 30 g/mol, 18 electrons) has a larger, more polarizable electron cloud than $\text{CH}_4$ (M = 16 g/mol, 10 electrons). Stronger LDFs in $\text{C}_2\text{H}_6$ → greater deviation from ideal pressure.

Neither has H-bonding (H is bonded to C in both, not N/O/F).

*Key move: For nonpolar molecules, the only IMF is LDF. More electrons = stronger LDF = more deviation. Never claim H-bonding for C-H bonds.*

## Topic 3.7 & 3.8: Solutions, Molarity, and Representations

**Example 1 — Farabaugh preparation of Na₂SO₄(aq):**
Prepare 250.0 mL of $0.0500\ M\ \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4$.

$$n = MV = (0.0500\ \text{mol/L})(0.2500\ \text{L}) = 0.01250\ \text{mol}$$

$$m = nM = (0.01250\ \text{mol})(142.04\ \text{g/mol}) = 1.78\ \text{g}\ \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4$$

Student claims $[\text{Na}^+] = 0.0500\ M$ — **WRONG.** $\text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow 2\text{Na}^+ + \text{SO}_4^{2-}$, so $[\text{Na}^+] = 2 \times 0.0500 = 0.100\ M$.

*Key move: After finding molarity of the compound, multiply by the stoichiometric ratio for each ion. $[\text{ion}] = n \times [compound]$ where $n$ = ions per formula unit.*

---

**Example 2 — Molarity calculation (Progress Check Q17):**
Prepare $\text{Na}_3\text{PO}_4$ solution: 82 g in 500. mL, molar mass = 164 g/mol.

$$n = \frac{82\ \text{g}}{164\ \text{g/mol}} = 0.50\ \text{mol}$$

$$M = \frac{n}{V} = \frac{0.50\ \text{mol}}{0.500\ \text{L}} = 1.0\ M$$

*Key move: Two-step: $n = m/M$, then $M = n/V$. The most common error is using 500 as liters instead of converting to 0.500 L.*

---

**Example 3 — Mass from molarity (Progress Check Q18):**
Prepare 100. mL of $0.25\ M$ NaCl (molar mass = 58 g/mol).

$$n = MV = (0.25\ \text{mol/L})(0.100\ \text{L}) = 0.025\ \text{mol}$$

$$m = nM = (0.025\ \text{mol})(58\ \text{g/mol}) = 1.45\ \text{g} \approx 1.5\ \text{g}$$

*Key move: Convert mL → L first (100 mL = 0.100 L). Then $n = MV$, then $m = nM$.*

---

**Example 4 — Dilution calculation (Progress Check Q16):**
Prepare 250.00 mL of $0.0250\ M$ HCl from $0.100\ M$ HCl stock.

$$M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$$

$$V_1 = \frac{M_2 V_2}{M_1} = \frac{(0.0250\ \text{M})(250.00\ \text{mL})}{0.100\ \text{M}} = 62.5\ \text{mL}$$

Measure 62.5 mL of stock, transfer to 250 mL volumetric flask, dilute to the mark.

*Key move: In $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$, moles of solute are conserved. Place stock on left ($M_1, V_1$) and target on right ($M_2, V_2$). Solve for the unknown.*

---

**Example 5 — Farabaugh student errors in KNO₃ preparation:**
Student was told to prepare 100.0 mL of $3.00\ M\ \text{KNO}_3(aq)$ but:

Error 1: Added solid directly to distilled water already at the 100 mL mark → solid dissolved but total volume exceeds 100 mL → concentration too low.

Error 2: Added water to the calibration mark before the solid fully dissolved → volume measured before dissolution complete → if solid hadn't fully dissolved, some solute missed.

Correct procedure: dissolve in a small amount of water first → verify complete dissolution → then dilute to the calibration mark.

*Key move: Volumetric glassware calibration assumes final volume = labeled amount. Add water to mark AFTER solute is completely dissolved.*

## Topic 3.9 & 3.10: Separation Methods and Solubility

**Example 1 — $R_f$ calculation and polarity (Krug 3.9/3.10):**
In paper chromatography (polar stationary phase, nonpolar mobile phase):
- Component Y traveled 4.2 cm; solvent front traveled 7.0 cm. $R_f(Y) = 4.2/7.0 = 0.60$.
- Component X traveled 2.1 cm. $R_f(X) = 2.1/7.0 = 0.30$.

Y has higher $R_f$ → Y traveled farther → Y has greater affinity for the nonpolar solvent → **Y is less polar** than X. X stuck closer to the polar paper → X is more polar.

*Key move: Higher $R_f$ = greater affinity for mobile phase. With polar stationary phase, higher $R_f$ = less polar compound.*

---

**Example 2 — Farabaugh ethyl acetate vs. butyl acetate distillation:**
Both are esters (LDF + dipole–dipole). Butyl acetate is larger (more electrons, M = 116 g/mol) → stronger LDFs → higher BP (126°C). Ethyl acetate M = 88 g/mol, BP = 77°C. In distillation, **ethyl acetate** (lower BP) appears first in the distillate.

*Key move: In distillation, always identify which compound has the lower BP (weaker IMF) — it distills first.*

---

**Example 3 — TLC solvent selection for column chromatography (Progress Check Q21):**
TLC run with four solvents on same two-component mixture:
- Solvent A: one spot near origin → not separated, sticking to stationary phase
- Solvent B: two spots at intermediate $R_f$, well separated (e.g., $R_f = 0.3$ and $0.7$)
- Solvent C: two spots, less spread ($R_f = 0.4$ and $0.6$)
- Solvent D: both spots near solvent front ($R_f \approx 0.9$ each) → not separated

Best for column chromatography: **Solvent B** — largest $R_f$ difference at intermediate positions.

Trap: Solvent D — when both $R_f$ values are near 1.0, both compounds elute quickly and together from a column; no separation achieved.

*Key move: Optimal TLC solvent for column chromatography = largest spread between spot positions at intermediate $R_f$ values (0.3–0.7 range). Spots too high or too low = poor column separation.*

---

**Example 4 — "Like dissolves like" with specific IMF reasoning (Krug 3.10):**
- $\text{CCl}_4$ miscible with pentane ($\text{C}_5\text{H}_{12}$): both nonpolar → only LDFs between molecules → dissolve freely.
- $\text{CH}_3\text{OH}$ immiscible with pentane: methanol is polar (H-bonding with O–H); pentane is nonpolar → very different IMF types → do not mix.
- NaCl in $\text{H}_2\text{O}$: ionic compound in polar solvent → strong ion–dipole forces overcome lattice energy → dissolves. NaCl in hexane: nonpolar solvent → no ion–dipole → does not dissolve.

*Key move: On the AP exam, always name the specific IMF involved — "ion–dipole" for ionic solutes in water, "H-bonding" for polar solute in water, "LDF" for nonpolar-nonpolar. Never just say "like dissolves like."*

---

**Example 5 — KCl solubility in water: which diagram? (Progress Check Q24):**
$\text{KCl}$ is ionic. Dissolving requires water molecules to exert ion–dipole forces on $\text{K}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ to pull them from the lattice. The diagram showing separated, individually hydrated ions (water molecules around each ion) with appropriate orientation represents this process.

Correct force: **ion–dipole** (ion interacting with water's permanent dipole). NOT dipole–dipole (that's between two polar molecules). NOT H-bonding (that's H bonded to N/O/F).

*Key move: When an ionic compound dissolves in water, the force is ALWAYS ion–dipole — not H-bonding, not dipole–dipole.*

## Topic 3.11: Spectroscopy and the EM Spectrum

![[em_spectrum_u3.svg|697]]
*EM spectrum with energy ranges, molecular events, and the visible energy bracket for table-based MCQs.*

**Example 1 — Farabaugh ozone photodecomposition:**
$\text{O}_3(g) \rightarrow \text{O}_2(g) + \text{O}(g)$. Photon frequency $= 1.00 \times 10^{15}\ \text{s}^{-1}$.

$$E_{\text{photon}} = h\nu = (6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J·s})(1.00 \times 10^{15}\ \text{s}^{-1}) = 6.63 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J/photon}$$

$$\text{Per mole: } 6.63 \times 10^{-19} \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 3.99 \times 10^5\ \text{J/mol} = 399\ \text{kJ/mol}$$

Minimum bond energy needed = 387 kJ/mol. Since 399 > 387, **yes**, this photon has sufficient energy to break the O–O bond.

*Key move: Calculate $E_{\text{photon}} = h\nu$, then convert to per-mole by multiplying by Avogadro's number. Compare to the bond energy threshold.*

---

**Example 2 — What happens when visible light is absorbed by a dye? (Progress Check Q25):**
Visible light causes electronic transitions — electrons in the dye molecule are promoted from a lower electronic energy level to a higher one. The photon energy exactly equals the energy gap: $E_{\text{photon}} = \Delta E_{\text{electronic}}$.

This is NOT vibrational (that requires IR) and NOT rotational (that requires microwave).

*Key move: Match the EM region to the molecular event. Visible/UV = electronic; IR = vibrational; microwave = rotational. The energy of the photon equals the energy gap of the transition.*

---

**Example 3 — IR spectrum of CO₂ (Progress Check Q26):**
The IR spectrum of $\text{CO}_2$ shows absorption dips at specific wavelengths. This represents the absorption of IR photons causing increased bond vibration (stretching and bending of the C=O bonds within the molecule). The bonds absorb at their characteristic vibrational frequencies.

This is NOT electronic transitions (which require UV/Vis) and NOT rotational transitions (which require microwave).

*Key move: IR spectra always represent bond vibrational transitions. Any absorption dip in the IR spectrum = a bond vibrational mode is being excited.*

---

**Example 4 — Why IR confirms carbonyl product (Progress Check Q27):**
An alcohol is oxidized to a carbonyl compound. IR spectroscopy confirms the conversion because:
- IR radiation causes transitions in **molecular vibrational energy levels**.
- The C=O bond (carbonyl) has a characteristic strong absorption at approximately $1700\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ due to its stretching vibration.
- The O–H stretch of the starting alcohol ($3200–3600\ \text{cm}^{-1}$) disappears; the C=O peak appears.

Trap: IR does NOT cause electronic transitions (that's UV/Vis) and does NOT involve electrons in the pi bond changing electronic levels.

*Key move: IR identifies functional groups through characteristic bond stretching frequencies. Always state "bond vibrational energy level" not "electronic energy level" for IR.*

---

**Example 5 — Frequency and energy calculations (Progress Check Q28–Q29):**
Q28: Wavelength = 540 nm = $5.4 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}$. Find frequency.

$$\nu = \frac{c}{\lambda} = \frac{3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}}{5.4 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}} = \frac{3.00}{5.4} \times 10^{8-(-7)} = 0.556 \times 10^{15} = 5.6 \times 10^{14}\ \text{s}^{-1}$$

Q29: Frequency $= 7 \times 10^{14}\ \text{s}^{-1}$. Find energy.

$$E = h\nu = (6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J·s})(7 \times 10^{14}\ \text{s}^{-1}) = 46.4 \times 10^{-20}\ \text{J} \approx 5 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$$

Exponent arithmetic: $10^{-34} \times 10^{14} = 10^{-34+14} = 10^{-20}$. Then $6.626 \times 7 \approx 46 \to 4.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J} \approx 5 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$.

*Key move: $\nu = c/\lambda$ — divide c by λ (never multiply). Exponent rule: $10^8 \div 10^{-7} = 10^{8-(-7)} = 10^{15}$. For E = hν, add exponents: $-34 + 14 = -20$.*

---

**Example 6 — Identifying visible energy from a table (Progress Check Q30):**
AP table gives: UV range $5 \times 10^{-19}$ to $2 \times 10^{-17}\ \text{J}$; IR range $2 \times 10^{-22}$ to $3 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$.

Visible range = between top of IR and bottom of UV:
- Top of IR: $3 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$
- Bottom of UV: $5 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$
- Visible window: $3 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$ to $5 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$

Check answers: $4 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$ falls in this window ✓. $9 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$ exceeds $5 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$ → UV range ✗.

*Key move: Visible = between IR top and UV bottom. On the AP exam, these boundaries are given in a table. Never memorize exact numbers — bracket between what's provided.*

## Topic 3.12: Photoelectric Effect

**Example 1 — Intensity vs. frequency distinction (Krug 3.12):**
A student doubles the intensity of UV light hitting a metal surface. A second student doubles the frequency (while keeping intensity constant).

Student 1 (double intensity): more photons per second → more electrons ejected per second, but each electron has the same kinetic energy as before. Number of electrons increases; KE per electron unchanged.

Student 2 (double frequency): higher-energy photons → each electron ejected has more kinetic energy. The number of electrons ejected may change slightly (only matters if near threshold), but the key change is KE per electron increases.

*Key move: Intensity → number of photons → number of ejected electrons. Frequency → energy per photon → KE of each ejected electron. These are independent.*

---

**Example 2 — Threshold frequency concept:**
A metal has binding energy of $5 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$. A photon has frequency $6 \times 10^{14}\ \text{s}^{-1}$.

$$E_{\text{photon}} = h\nu = (6.626 \times 10^{-34})(6 \times 10^{14}) = 3.98 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$$

Since $3.98 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J} < 5 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$ (binding energy), no electron is ejected — even if intensity is very high.

*Key move: If $E_{\text{photon}} <$ binding energy → NO ejection, regardless of intensity. Ejection requires $E_{\text{photon}} \geq E_{\text{binding}}$.*

## Topic 3.13: Beer–Lambert Law

![[beers_law_errors_u3.svg|697]]
*Complete error analysis table for Beer's Law cuvette experiments.*

**Example 1 — Farabaugh Beer's Law cobalt ore analysis:**
A 0.630 g ore sample is dissolved and diluted to 50.00 mL. Absorbance = 0.74. From calibration curve, $[\text{Co}^{2+}] = 0.040\ M$.

$$n_{\text{Co}} = (0.040\ \text{M})(0.05000\ \text{L}) = 0.0020\ \text{mol}$$

$$m_{\text{Co}} = (0.0020\ \text{mol})(58.93\ \text{g/mol}) = 0.118\ \text{g}$$

$$\% \text{Co} = \frac{0.118\ \text{g}}{0.630\ \text{g}} \times 100 = 18.7\%$$

*Key move: Read concentration from the calibration curve graph at the measured absorbance. Then use $n = cV$ and $m = nM$ to find mass, then mass percent.*

---

**Example 2 — Fingerprints on cuvette (Progress Check Q31):**
Student neglected to wipe fingerprints off the transparent sides before measuring absorbance of a red dye.

Effect: The absorption peak would appear higher (larger A value). Fingerprints scatter and absorb additional light — the detector receives less transmitted intensity $I_t$ than it should. Since $A = -\log(I_t/I_0)$, lower $I_t$ gives higher A.

The peak *position* (wavelength, ~520 nm for the red dye) does not change. Only the peak *height* (absorbance value) increases.

Trap: Choosing a wavelength shift. Fingerprints do not change which wavelength the dye absorbs — they uniformly increase scattering loss at all wavelengths.

*Key move: Anything that reduces transmitted light → measured A is too high. Peak position (λ) is unchanged; only the absorbance value (y-axis) increases.*

---

**Example 3 — Frosted cuvette wall error (Progress Check Q32):**
A student calibrated the spectrophotometer with light through the transparent sides of the cuvette. When measuring the sample, the cuvette was incorrectly oriented with light through the frosted sides.

Effect: Measured A will be **higher** than actual. The frosted glass scatters more light than the transparent sides used for calibration. The detector sees reduced $I_t$ compared to calibration → instrument interprets this as higher absorbance.

*Key move: Same mechanism as fingerprints — extra scattering → less $I_t$ → A too high. The error is in the path of light through the sample, not in the sample concentration.*

---

**Example 4 — Water droplets remaining (Progress Check Q33):**
A student rinsed the cuvette with distilled water but did not rinse with the blue dye solution first. Water droplets diluted the dye when the solution was added.

Effect: Measured A will be **too low**. The dye concentration is less than intended because water diluted it. Since $A = \varepsilon bc$ and $c$ decreased, $A$ decreases.

Trap: Choosing "too high because water absorbs light." Distilled water does not significantly absorb visible light — it is transparent in the visible range. The error is purely a concentration effect.

*Key move: Dilution of sample → lower c → lower A. This is the most straightforward Beer's Law error. Water itself does not contribute meaningfully to absorbance in the visible range.*

---

**Example 5 — Beer's Law MCQ error analysis summary (AP style):**

| Error | Effect | Trap to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Fingerprint on transparent side | A too high | "Shifts peak wavelength" — no, peak position unchanged |
| Frosted wall in light path | A too high | "A too low because less light enters" — no, less $I_t$ → higher A |
| Path length longer than standard | A too high | "No effect" — b appears in $A = \varepsilon bc$ |
| Water droplets / not rinsed with sample | A too low | "Water absorbs visible light" — no, water is transparent |
| Wavelength not at $\lambda_{\text{max}}$ | A too low | "Shifts peak position on absorption spectrum" — no, only changes measured A value |

*Key move: Always ask: does this reduce transmitted light ($I_t$)? → A too high. Does this dilute the sample ($c$)? → A too low. Wavelength error only affects the measured A, never the position of the absorption peak on the spectrum.*
