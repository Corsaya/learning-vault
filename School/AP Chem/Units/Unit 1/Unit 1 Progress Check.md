## Q1. Which numerical expression gives the number of moles in 5.0 g of CaO?

$n = \frac{m}{M} = \frac{5.0\ \text{g}}{56\ \text{g/mol}}$

**Answer: B — 5.0 g / (56 g/mol)**

To find moles from mass, divide mass by molar mass: $n = m/M$. CaO has M = 40.08 + 16.00 ≈ 56 g/mol. Dividing grams by g/mol cancels units correctly to give mol.

> **Trap:** (A) multiplies instead of divides, giving units of g²/mol. Always check units — grams ÷ (g/mol) = mol. (C) and (D) invert the fraction or double-invert.

## Q2. A 21 g sample of pure Cu metal — most useful info for determining number of Cu atoms?

Chain: mass (g) → moles → atoms. Step 1 needs molar mass; step 2 uses Avogadro's number (given on reference sheet).

$n = \frac{21\ \text{g}}{63.55\ \text{g/mol}} = 0.330\ \text{mol}$

$N = 0.330 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = \mathbf{1.99 \times 10^{23}\ \text{atoms}}$

**Answer: A — The molar mass of Cu**

Molar mass is the essential bridge between macroscopic mass and amount in moles; Avogadro's number (already on the reference sheet) then converts moles to atoms. Density gives volume, not particle count; isotope ratios are irrelevant once molar mass is known; temperature and pressure only matter for gases.

> **Trap:** Don't choose (D) isotope ratio — the molar mass on the periodic table already accounts for natural isotopic abundances. You do not need to recalculate it.

## Q3. Which 1.0 mol sample has the greatest mass?

1 mol of any substance has mass equal to its molar mass in grams.

- $\text{NO}$: M = 14.01 + 16.00 = **30.01 g**
- $\text{NO}_2$: M = 14.01 + 2(16.00) = **46.01 g**
- $\text{N}_2\text{O}$: M = 2(14.01) + 16.00 = **44.02 g**
- $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$: M = 2(14.01) + 5(16.00) = **108.02 g**

**Answer: D — $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$**

For equal mole amounts, the sample with the greatest molar mass has the greatest mass. N₂O₅ has the highest molar mass (108 g/mol) of the four nitrogen oxides listed.

> **Trap:** Don't confuse number of atoms/elements with mass. NO has only 2 atoms but each 1.0 mol still weighs its molar mass in grams.

## Q4. Mass spectrum with peaks at m/z ≈ 90 (tallest ~55%), 91, 92, 94, 96 — atomic mass closest to?

The dominant isotope is at m/z = 90 (~55% abundance), pulling the average close to but above 90. Estimating with only the two largest peaks (90 at ~55% and 92 at ~12%):

$\bar{m} \approx (90)(0.55) + (92)(0.12) + \text{minor contributions} \approx 91.2\ \text{amu}$

This is zirconium (Zr, actual = 91.22 amu).

**Answer: B — 91 amu**

The weighted average is pulled toward the most abundant isotope (m/z = 90), but the several heavier isotopes shift it upward to ~91. The average cannot equal the most abundant peak exactly when other isotopes are present.

> **Trap:** Never just report the tallest peak mass as the average — the average is always a weighted mean. Here 90 is the most abundant peak but the answer is 91, not 90.

## Q5. Mass spectrum with peaks at m/z 154–162 — most consistent with which element?

The spectrum shows ~7–8 peaks spread over m/z 154–162, indicating many naturally occurring isotopes with broadly distributed abundances and an average near ~157–158. Gadolinium (Gd, Z=64) has 7 stable isotopes ranging from 152–160 amu with average 157.25 amu — this is the only element with this many isotopes in this mass range. Eu has 2 isotopes (~151, 153); Tb has 1 isotope (159); Dy has 7 isotopes but centered higher (~162.5).

**Answer: B — Gd**

The number of peaks equals the number of naturally occurring isotopes. A spectrum showing ~7 peaks spread across m/z 154–160 with the average near 157 is uniquely consistent with gadolinium's known isotopic pattern.

> **Trap:** Count the number of peaks first — it equals the number of stable isotopes. Then match the mass range and approximate average to narrow down the element.

## Q6. Mass spectrum with two peaks: m/z = 185 (~35%) and m/z = 187 (~65%)

$\bar{m} = (185)(0.35) + (187)(0.65) = 64.75 + 121.55 = \mathbf{186.3\ \text{amu}}$

The average is closer to 187 because that isotope is more abundant (~65%).

**Answer: C — 186.3 amu**

The weighted average atomic mass is calculated by multiplying each isotope's mass by its fractional abundance and summing. Since the m/z = 187 peak is taller (more abundant), the average falls closer to 187 than to 185. This is rhenium (Re, actual = 186.21 amu).

> **Trap:** Converting % to decimal is mandatory — using 35 and 65 instead of 0.35 and 0.65 gives a nonsensical result. Also, 186.0 (answer B) would be the average only if both isotopes were exactly 50/50.

## Q7. BaCl₂ dissolved, Na₂SO₄ added, BaSO₄ precipitate filtered and dried. Which question can be answered?

The experiment measures: initial mass of BaCl₂ powder, mass of BaSO₄ precipitate formed. The 1:1 ratio of Ba²⁺ to BaSO₄ allows back-calculation of moles of Ba²⁺ → moles of BaCl₂ → mass of BaCl₂ in the sample. If the powder is impure, the recovered BaCl₂ mass < original sample mass → % purity can be determined.

**Answer: B — Is the BaCl₂(s) used in the experiment pure?**

By measuring the mass of BaSO₄ precipitate and using stoichiometry (1 mol BaSO₄ per 1 mol BaCl₂), the student can determine how much pure BaCl₂ was present. Comparing this to the original 10.0 g sample reveals whether the powder is 100% pure BaCl₂ or contains impurities.

> **Trap:** (A) asks about purity of Na₂SO₄, but excess Na₂SO₄ is added so its purity doesn't limit the result. (D) is not answerable — BaSO₄ is essentially insoluble, so solubility cannot be determined from this setup.

## Q8. Student measures mass of M, heats in air → MO. Which question can be answered?

Known from experiment: initial mass of M, final mass of MO. From these two masses, we can calculate: mass of O incorporated = mass(MO) − mass(M), moles of each element (if molar mass is known), and ultimately the empirical formula of MO — which is already given. However, the mass ratio M:O is directly determinable, and with molar masses, the percent composition is answerable. The most directly answerable question is about molar mass — if we know the formula is MO and the mass ratio, we can compute M's molar mass.

**Answer: B — What is the molar mass of M?**

From the masses before and after, the mass of oxygen gained = mass(MO) − mass(M). Since the formula is MO (1:1 ratio), moles O = moles M. Thus: moles M = mass(O)/16.00, and molar mass of M = mass(M)/moles(M). Density, melting points, and solubility are physical properties not determinable from mass measurements alone.

> **Trap:** (C) and (D) ask about melting points — a physical property requiring direct temperature measurement, not inferable from mass data.

## Q9. 42.0 g sample with 36.0 g C and 6.0 g H — which question can be answered?

Available data: masses of C and H → moles of each → simplest whole-number ratio.

$$n_C = \frac{36.0}{12.01} = 3.00\ \text{mol} \qquad n_H = \frac{6.0}{1.008} = 5.95 \approx 6.00\ \text{mol}$$

Ratio C:H = 3:6 = 1:2 → Empirical formula = $\text{CH}_2$

Cannot determine: volume (no density), molar mass (need molecular formula data), chemical stability (requires different experiment).

**Answer: D — What is the empirical formula of the compound?**

The mass data allows conversion to moles and determination of the simplest whole-number molar ratio of atoms, which is the empirical formula (CH₂). Volume requires density; molar mass requires additional data (e.g., boiling point elevation or mass spectrum); chemical stability is not determinable from composition alone.

> **Trap:** (B) molar mass is a common wrong choice — the empirical formula only gives the *simplest ratio*, not the actual molecular formula or molar mass without additional information.

## Q10. Jar of powder labeled NaCl; table shows mass 27.3 g, mass % Na = 35.2%, density = 2.05 g/cm³, color = white. Most helpful for determining purity?

Pure NaCl has a fixed mass % Na = 22.99/58.44 × 100 = **39.3% Na**. The measured value is 35.2%, which differs from the theoretical 39.3% — indicating the sample is NOT pure NaCl.

Mass alone doesn't tell you composition. Density of pure NaCl is 2.16 g/cm³ — close to 2.05 but not definitive without knowing what the impurity is. Color (white) is consistent with NaCl but many white powders exist.

**Answer: B — Mass percent of Na**

A pure substance has a fixed elemental composition. The mass percent of Na in pure NaCl is theoretically 39.3%; any deviation indicates the presence of impurities. This is the most direct and quantitative test of purity available in the table.

> **Trap:** Mass tells you how much sample you have, not whether it's pure. Density is a physical property that could confirm purity but is less precise here. Color is a qualitative property — insufficient on its own.

## Q11. Mixture of XCl and ZCl (mass % of X and Z are known). Additional info needed to find mole percent?

We have: mass % of X and mass % of Z in the mixture. To get mole percent of XCl and ZCl, we need to convert mass → moles. The conversion requires molar mass of XCl and ZCl, which in turn requires molar masses of X and Z (since Cl's molar mass is known = 35.45 g/mol).

$n_{XCl} = \frac{m_{XCl}}{M_X + 35.45}$; mole % XCl = $\frac{n_{XCl}}{n_{XCl} + n_{ZCl}} \times 100$

**Answer: B — The molar masses of X and Z**

With the mass percent of X and Z known and the molar mass of Cl fixed at 35.45 g/mol, knowing the molar masses of X and Z allows calculation of the molar masses of XCl and ZCl. These molar masses then convert each mass fraction into moles, enabling calculation of mole percent.

> **Trap:** (A) Number of isotopes of Cl is irrelevant — Cl's molar mass (35.45) is on the periodic table. (D) mass % of Cl is already derivable since mass % X + mass % Z + mass % Cl = 100%.

## Q12. Mass of each gas is known — what additional info is needed to find mole fraction?

Mole fraction = $\chi_A = \frac{n_A}{n_{total}}$ where $n = m/M$.

We have masses. We need molar masses to convert mass → moles. Then:

$\chi_A = \frac{m_A/M_A}{\sum(m_i/M_i)}$

**Answer: A — The molar mass of each gas**

Mole fraction is defined in terms of moles, not mass. Given only the masses, molar masses are the one additional piece of information required to convert each mass to moles and compute the mole fraction. Density, pressure, and atom count per molecule do not enable this conversion.

> **Trap:** (D) number of atoms per molecule does not give molar mass directly without knowing which elements are present. Don't confuse molecular formula (# atoms) with molar mass (g/mol).

## Q13. Electron configuration $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6$ — how many unpaired electrons?

This is the complete ground-state configuration. Total electrons = 2+2+6+2+6 = 18, Argon (Z=18).

- $1s^2$: both paired
- $2s^2$: both paired
- $2p^6$: all three orbitals full (each with 2 electrons) — all paired
- $3s^2$: both paired
- $3p^6$: all three 3p orbitals full — all paired

**Answer: A — 0**

Argon has a completely filled noble gas configuration; every orbital in every subshell is fully occupied with paired electrons. There are zero unpaired electrons, consistent with Ar's extreme chemical inertness.

> **Trap:** Don't apply Hund's rule to $3p^6$ — Hund's rule applies when filling an *incomplete* subshell. A full $p^6$ subshell has 3 orbitals × 2 electrons each = all paired.

## Q14. Orbital diagram for oxygen (Z=8) ground state?

O: $1s^2 2s^2 2p^4$. Need to fill 2p with 4 electrons across 3 orbitals.

By Hund's rule: fill each 2p orbital singly first, then pair. 2p (3 orbitals): one paired, two singly occupied. This gives 2 unpaired electrons.

- (A): 2p has 3 electrons (one paired, one up, one empty) — wrong count (that is N, Z=7)
- (B): 2p has 5 electrons — wrong count for O
- **(C): $1s^2 2s^2$, 2p with 4 electrons: one paired, two singly occupied — Hund's rule obeyed ✓**
- (D): 2p electrons paired in two orbitals before filling the third — violates Hund's rule

**Answer: C**

Oxygen's 2p subshell contains 4 electrons distributed across 3 orbitals. By Hund's rule, electrons maximize unpaired occupancy before pairing: one orbital gets 2 electrons (paired) and the other two each get 1 (unpaired). This is the lowest energy arrangement.

> **Trap:** (D) pairs electrons in the first two 2p orbitals before filling the third — a Hund's rule violation. The ground state always maximizes unpaired electrons within a subshell.

## Q15. Ground-state electron configuration of Mg (Z=12)?

Filling order: $1s \rightarrow 2s \rightarrow 2p \rightarrow 3s$

$1s^2(2) + 2s^2(4) + 2p^6(10) + 3s^2(12) = 12$ electrons total ✓

**Answer: C — $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2$**

Magnesium has 12 electrons. Following the Aufbau principle, they fill in order: $1s^2$ (2e), $2s^2$ (4e), $2p^6$ (10e), $3s^2$ (12e). The valence electrons are the 2 electrons in the 3s subshell, consistent with Mg being in Group 2.

> **Trap:** (B) includes $3p^6$ which would give too many electrons (18 = Ar). (D) includes 3d which doesn't fill until after 4s. Always count total electrons to verify.

## Q16. PES spectrum with two peaks: X at ~5 MJ/mol (tall) and Y at ~0.5 MJ/mol (small) — which is 1s?

Higher binding energy = harder to remove = closer to nucleus = inner shell electrons. The x-axis goes from high binding energy on the left to low on the right. Peak X is at higher binding energy (~5 MJ/mol) and is the taller peak. Peak Y is at lower binding energy (~0.5 MJ/mol).

The 1s electrons are the innermost — they experience the full nuclear charge with minimal shielding → highest binding energy → leftmost peak.

**Answer: B — Peak X, because 1s electrons have the strongest attraction to the nucleus**

The 1s subshell is closest to the nucleus and experiences the greatest effective nuclear charge with no inner-shell shielding. This corresponds to the highest binding energy on the PES spectrum, which is Peak X (leftmost, highest on the x-axis).

> **Trap:** (A) has the right peak (X) but wrong reason — 1s electrons are the *hardest* to remove, not easiest. The reason must cite nuclear attraction/binding energy, not ease of removal.

## Q17. Boron PES spectrum (Z=5, 1s²2s²2p¹) — three peaks. Which answer is consistent with the electron shell model?

Boron: 1s²(peak at ~19 MJ/mol, height 2), 2s²(peak at ~1.4 MJ/mol, height 2), 2p¹(peak at ~0.8 MJ/mol, height 1).

The spectrum shows: innermost peak (1s, 2e), middle peak (2s, 2e), outermost peak (2p, 1e). The 2p peak has height 1 = single electron in the 2p subshell.

**Answer: B — The spectrum shows a single electron in the 2p subshell**

Boron's electron configuration is 1s²2s²2p¹. The PES peak for 2p has a relative height of 1, directly showing that there is only one electron in the 2p subshell. This is the key feature distinguishing B from C (which would show 2p height of 2) and directly demonstrates the shell model's subshell filling.

> **Trap:** (C) says equal electrons in 1st and 2nd shells — 1st shell has 2 (1s²), 2nd shell has 3 (2s²2p¹). They are NOT equal. (D) says three electrons with the same binding energy in the 2nd shell — wrong, 2s and 2p have different binding energies and appear as separate peaks.

## Q18. Carbon PES spectrum (Z=6, $1s^2 2s^2 2p^2$) — three peaks. Which statement is consistent?

Carbon configuration: $1s^2$(2e), $2s^2$(2e), $2p^2$(2e). Each subshell holds exactly 2 electrons. The PES spectrum shows three peaks of equal relative height. The inner shell (n=1) has 2 electrons; the outer shell (n=2) has 4 electrons ($2s^2 + 2p^2$), so outer > inner — ruling out (D).

**Answer: B — The spectrum shows equal numbers of electrons in the three occupied electron subshells**

Carbon's three occupied subshells are 1s², 2s², and 2p², each containing exactly 2 electrons. The PES spectrum reflects this as three peaks of equal relative height, consistent with 2 electrons per subshell across all three energy levels.

> **Trap:** (A) says 4 electrons in the inner shell — wrong, only 1s is the inner shell with 2 electrons. (D) reverses inner vs. outer electron count. (C) claims all valence electrons have the same binding energy — but 2s and 2p appear as separate peaks at different binding energies.

## Q19. Atomic radii of N group: N(65), P(100), As(115), Sb(145), Bi(160) pm — why does radius increase N→Bi?

**Answered B (wrong) — Correct: C**

**What was answered — B:** "The number of electrons in the outermost shell of the atom increases."

**Why B is wrong (AP explanation):** All Group 15 elements have five valence electrons — the number of electrons in the outermost shell does NOT change going down the group. This answer confuses the shell number with the electron count. The AP explanation states: "All of the atoms in the nitrogen group have five valence electrons; thus this cannot help explain the trend of increasing atomic radius."

**Correct answer: C — The attractive force between the valence electrons and the nuclei of the atoms decreases.**

Going down Group 15, each successive element adds a new principal energy level, placing valence electrons farther from the nucleus while adding more inner-shell electrons that shield the valence electrons from the nucleus. By Coulomb's law ($F \propto q_1q_2/r^2$), the combination of greater distance and increased shielding reduces the net attractive force between the valence electrons and the nucleus. Weaker attraction allows the electron cloud to expand, producing larger atomic radii: $\text{N}(65) < \text{P}(100) < \text{As}(115) < \text{Sb}(145) < \text{Bi}(160)\ \text{pm}$.

> **Mistake pattern:** Conflating principal energy level (shell number, $n$) with valence electron count. Group number fixes the valence electron count across all periods; what changes going down a group is $n$ (which shell the valence electrons occupy), not how many there are. When explaining a group trend, cite shielding and nuclear attraction, not electron count.

## Q20. Why is atomic radius of K greater than Br?

**Answered B (wrong) — Correct: D**

**What was answered — B:** "The valence electrons in K are in a higher principal energy level than those of Br."

**Why B is wrong (AP explanation):** Both K ($[\text{Ar}]\ 4s^1$) and Br ($[\text{Ar}]\ 3d^{10}\ 4s^2\ 4p^5$) have their valence electrons in the fourth principal energy level ($n = 4$). The AP explanation states: "The valence electrons in both K and Br are in the fourth principal energy level." Principal quantum number is the same for both — B is factually incorrect.

**Correct answer: D — The effective nuclear charge experienced by valence electrons is smaller for K than for Br.**

K has $Z = 19$ with 18 core electrons shielding → $Z_{\text{eff}} \approx 1$ for the single $4s^1$ electron. Br has $Z = 35$ with 28 core electrons shielding → $Z_{\text{eff}} \approx 7$ for its valence electrons. Although both elements' valence electrons occupy $n = 4$ orbitals, Br's much higher effective nuclear charge pulls its electrons significantly closer to the nucleus, giving Br a smaller radius than K. The AP explanation states: "The atomic radius of K is greater than that of Br because the effective nuclear charge of K is smaller than that of Br. The attraction between its valence electron and the nucleus is less than the attraction between the valence electrons in Br and the nucleus."

> **Mistake pattern:** Defaulting to principal energy level ($n$) as the explanation when comparing elements in the same period. Within a period, $n$ is the same for all valence electrons; the variable that drives the radius trend is $Z_{\text{eff}}$. Reserve the "higher $n$ = larger radius" argument for group trends (going down), not period trends (going across).

## Q21. Why is IE₁ of K less than that of Ca?

K (Z=19): [Ar] 4s¹ — Z_eff for 4s¹ ≈ 1 (18 core electrons shield almost completely)
Ca (Z=20): [Ar] 4s² — Z_eff for 4s² slightly higher ≈ 2

Moving left to right in Period 4, nuclear charge increases while shielding stays ~constant → Z_eff increases → valence electrons held more tightly → higher IE. K has lower Z_eff than Ca → lower IE.

**Answer: C — The valence electron of K experiences a lower effective nuclear charge than the valence electrons of Ca**

Potassium (Z=19) has one fewer proton than calcium (Z=20) while both have 18 core electrons providing similar shielding. The lower nuclear charge in K results in a lower effective nuclear charge on the 4s valence electron(s), making the electron easier to remove. This directly explains K's lower first ionization energy compared to Ca.

> **Trap:** (A) K has LOWER electronegativity than Ca (electronegativity increases left to right), so this is backward and also irrelevant — electronegativity doesn't directly explain IE. (B) K has a LARGER atomic radius than Ca (wrong direction), and even if true, larger radius alone doesn't explain IE without citing Z_eff.

## Q22. Why does Rb more easily lose an electron than Li?

Both are Group 1 alkali metals with 1 valence electron in an s subshell. Going down Group 1: Li (n=2, 2s¹) → Rb (n=5, 5s¹). The valence electron in Rb is in the 5th principal energy level — much farther from the nucleus than Li's 2s electron.

By Coulomb's law: greater distance → weaker attractive force → lower IE → easier to lose the electron.

**Answer: D — In the Rb atom the valence electron is farther from its nucleus than the valence electron of Li is from its nucleus**

Rubidium's valence electron occupies the 5s orbital (n=5), while lithium's valence electron is in the 2s orbital (n=2). The greater distance between Rb's nucleus and its valence electron weakens the electrostatic attraction (Coulomb's law: force ∝ 1/r²), making the electron much easier to remove. This is the defining trend in ionization energy down a group.

> **Trap:** (C) says Rb has more protons and neutrons — TRUE, but this would suggest stronger pull (harder to remove), which is the opposite conclusion. The distance effect (Coulomb's law denominator) dominates over the nuclear charge for group trends.

## Q23. Rb reacts with O in 2:1 ratio forming Rb₂O. Which element reacts with O in a 2:1 ratio?

Rb₂O formula: Rb is +1, O is −2, so 2(+1) + (−2) = 0 ✓. Any element that forms a +1 cation will react with O²⁻ in a 2:1 (metal:O) ratio. Rb is in Group 1 → +1 ion. Elements in the same group have the same number of valence electrons → same ionic charge → same stoichiometric ratio.

- Cs: Group 1, same group as Rb → +1 → Cs₂O → **2:1 ratio ✓**
- S: Group 16 → −2 (nonmetal, won't form cation with O)
- Sr: Group 2 → +2 → SrO → 1:1 ratio
- Br: Group 17 → −1 or variable, not +1

**Answer: B — Cs, because it is in the same group as Rb**

Cesium is in Group 1, the same group as rubidium. All Group 1 elements form +1 cations in ionic compounds. Since oxygen forms a −2 anion, the charge-balance formula requires two +1 cations per one −2 anion, giving a 2:1 mole ratio (metal:oxygen) and the general formula M₂O.

> **Trap:** (C) Sr is in the SAME PERIOD as Rb (Period 5), not the same group. Same period ≠ same valence electrons ≠ same ionic charge. Same GROUP is what matters for predicting ionic charge.

## Q24. What charge does Al typically have in ionic compounds, and why?

Al: Z=13, configuration = [Ne] 3s²3p¹ → 3 valence electrons (Group 13).

Ionic compounds form when metals lose electrons to achieve a noble gas configuration. Al loses all 3 valence electrons → [Ne] configuration → Al³⁺.

**Answer: C — +3, because it has three valence electrons**

Aluminum is in Group 13 with the electron configuration [Ne]3s²3p¹, giving it three valence electrons. In ionic compound formation, aluminum loses all three valence electrons to achieve the stable [Ne] noble gas configuration, resulting in a +3 charge. The charge on a main-group metal ion equals the number of valence electrons lost, which equals the group number for Groups 1–3.

> **Trap:** (A) describes an element with 1 unpaired electron — this is true for Al's 3p¹ orbital, but "unpaired" doesn't determine ionic charge; total valence electrons do. (B) the 2s subshell belongs to the core [Ne], not the valence shell — Al's valence electrons are 3s²3p¹, not 2s².
