# AP Chem — Unit 8 Progress Check: MCQ Answers

---

*Questions 1–3 refer to the following scenario: A student prepares three buffers using $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$ and $\text{NaHCO}_3$ at different ratios. $K_b(\text{CO}_3^{2-}) = 2.1 \times 10^{-4}$.*

---

## Q1. $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$ / $\text{NaHCO}_3$ buffer — which equation shows correct response when a strong base is added?

The buffer pair here is $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$ (conjugate base) and $\text{HCO}_3^-$ (weak acid). When strong base ($\text{OH}^-$) is added, it is neutralized by the weak acid component: $\text{HCO}_3^-(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq) \rightarrow \text{CO}_3^{2-}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$.

**Answer: B — $\text{HCO}_3^-(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq) \rightleftharpoons \text{CO}_3^{2-}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$**

Buffers resist pH change by neutralizing added acids or bases. The weak acid ($\text{HCO}_3^-$) donates a proton to the incoming $\text{OH}^-$, converting it to $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$ (the conjugate base) and water. This reaction is the one that actually consumes the base and prevents large pH swings.

> **Trap:** Choosing D — the reverse hydrolysis reaction $\text{CO}_3^{2-} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{HCO}_3^- + \text{OH}^-$. That equation actually produces $\text{OH}^-$; it does not consume it. Only the weak acid component ($\text{HCO}_3^-$) can neutralize added $\text{OH}^-$.

## Q2. Expression for the pH of Buffer 1 — which correctly applies Henderson-Hasselbalch in $\text{pOH}$ form?

Since $K_b$ is given for $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$ (a base), the appropriate form uses pOH: $\text{pOH} = \text{p}K_b + \log\frac{[\text{BH}^+]}{[\text{B}]} = \text{p}K_b + \log\frac{[\text{HCO}_3^-]}{[\text{CO}_3^{2-}]}$, then $\text{pH} = 14 - \text{pOH}$.

**Answer: C — $\text{pH} = [14.00 + \log(2.1 \times 10^{-4})] + \log\!\left(\frac{0.100}{0.150}\right) = 10.15$**

Convert $K_b$ to $K_a$: $\text{p}K_a = 14.00 + \log K_b = 14.00 + \log(2.1 \times 10^{-4}) = 10.32$. Then apply Henderson-Hasselbalch with $[\text{A}^-]/[\text{HA}] = [\text{CO}_3^{2-}]/[\text{HCO}_3^-] = 0.100/0.150$: $\text{pH} = 10.32 + \log(0.667) = 10.32 - 0.18 = 10.15$.

> **Trap:** Choosing D — that expression uses $\log(0.150/0.100)$, which inverts the ratio (puts $[\text{HCO}_3^-]$ in the numerator). In H-H, the numerator is always the conjugate base ($\text{CO}_3^{2-}$), not the weak acid ($\text{HCO}_3^-$). Inverting the ratio gives 10.50 instead of the correct 10.15.

## Q3. Buffers 2 and 3 have the same pH despite different concentrations — why?

Henderson-Hasselbalch: $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]}$. pH depends only on the **ratio** of conjugate base to weak acid, not on their absolute concentrations. If the molar ratio $[\text{CO}_3^{2-}]/[\text{HCO}_3^-]$ is identical in Buffers 2 and 3, their pH is identical.

**Answer: A — The ratio of $[\text{CO}_3^{2-}]$ to $[\text{HCO}_3^-]$ is the same in Buffers 2 and 3.**

Since pH is determined by $\text{p}K_a + \log(\text{ratio})$, two buffers with the same ratio but different total concentrations have identical pH values. Doubling or halving all concentrations equally leaves the ratio unchanged and therefore leaves pH unchanged.

> **Trap:** Thinking equal concentrations (not ratios) are required for equal pH. This mistake ignores the logarithmic nature of pH. Buffer capacity (ability to resist pH change) depends on total concentration, but pH depends only on the ratio.

## Q4. Pure water at 25°C, $K_w = 1 \times 10^{-14}$ — what is the pOH?

In pure water, $[\text{H}^+] = [\text{OH}^-]$. $K_w = [\text{H}^+][\text{OH}^-] = (x)(x) = 10^{-14}$, so $x = 10^{-7}\ \text{M}$. $\text{pOH} = -\log(10^{-7}) = 7.00$.

**Answer: D — $\text{pOH} = 7.00$**

Pure water at 25°C is neutral: $[\text{H}^+] = [\text{OH}^-] = 1 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{M}$. $\text{pH} = \text{pOH} = 7.00$. This is the definition of neutral pH at 25°C.

> **Trap:** Confusing pH and pOH. Both equal 7.00 in pure water at 25°C. The question asks for pOH specifically — the numerical value is the same, but students should confirm which they're asked for.

## Q5. At 55°C, $K_w = 7 \times 10^{-14}$ — what is the pH of pure water?

$[\text{H}^+] = [\text{OH}^-] = \sqrt{K_w} = \sqrt{7 \times 10^{-14}}$. $\text{pH} = -\log\sqrt{7 \times 10^{-14}} = -\frac{1}{2}\log(7 \times 10^{-14})$.

**Answer: C — $\text{pH} = -\log(\sqrt{7 \times 10^{-14}}) \approx 6.58$**

$\sqrt{7 \times 10^{-14}} \approx 2.65 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{M}$, so $\text{pH} \approx -\log(2.65 \times 10^{-7}) \approx 6.58$. Water is still neutral at 55°C (pH = pOH), but the neutral point has shifted to pH 6.58 because $K_w$ increased with temperature.

> **Trap:** Calling 55°C water "acidic" because pH < 7. At 55°C, the neutral pH is 6.58, not 7.00. A solution is acidic only if $[\text{H}^+] > [\text{OH}^-]$, i.e., pH < pOH. Pure water at any temperature has pH = pOH, so it is always neutral.

## Q6. Water autoionization ($\Delta H = +56\ \text{kJ/mol}$) — at higher temperature, $K_w$ is larger and pH decreases. Is the solution acidic?

Autoionization is endothermic. Higher temperature increases $K_w$ (Le Chatelier: adding heat to an endothermic process drives it forward). More $\text{H}^+$ and $\text{OH}^-$ are produced equally, so the solution remains neutral (pH = pOH), but both $[\text{H}^+]$ and $[\text{OH}^-]$ are larger — pH is numerically lower than 7.

**Answer: D — The solution is still neutral because $[\text{H}^+] = [\text{OH}^-]$, but pH decreases as temperature increases.**

Neutral means $[\text{H}^+] = [\text{OH}^-]$, regardless of temperature. Since autoionization produces equal amounts of $\text{H}^+$ and $\text{OH}^-$, pure water is always neutral. The pH scale shifts with temperature, so "neutral pH" at higher temperature is below 7.

> **Trap:** Concluding the solution is acidic because pH < 7. Acidity requires $[\text{H}^+] > [\text{OH}^-]$. Autoionization produces them in a 1:1 ratio — so pure water remains neutral at all temperatures, even though the numerical pH changes.

## Q7. $0.10\ \text{M}\ \text{HBr}$ — what is the pH?

HBr is a strong acid — it dissociates completely: $[\text{H}^+] = 0.10\ \text{M} = 1.0 \times 10^{-1}\ \text{M}$.
$\text{pH} = -\log(1.0 \times 10^{-1}) = 1.00$.

**Answer: B — $\text{pH} = 1.00$**

Strong acids (HCl, HBr, HI, $\text{HNO}_3$, $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$, $\text{HClO}_4$) dissociate 100%. $[\text{H}^+]$ equals the initial acid concentration.

> **Trap:** Using a $K_a$ expression for a strong acid. Strong acids have no meaningful $K_a$ equilibrium to solve — they are fully dissociated. Skip the ICE table; set $[\text{H}^+] =$ concentration directly.

---

## Q8. $1 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{M}\ \text{HClO}_4$ — what is the pH?

$\text{HClO}_4$ is a strong acid: $[\text{H}^+] = 1 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{M}$. $\text{pH} = -\log(1 \times 10^{-5}) = 5.00$.

**Answer: B — $\text{pH} \approx 5.0$**

$\text{HClO}_4$ (perchloric acid) is one of the seven strong acids. Complete dissociation gives $[\text{H}^+]$ equal to the stated molarity. $-\log(10^{-5}) = 5$.

> **Trap:** Worrying that the pH approaches neutral (pH = 5 is close to 7 for a strong acid). At very low concentrations of strong acid, the autoionization of water contributes $10^{-7}\ \text{M}\ \text{H}^+$, but this is negligible compared to $10^{-5}\ \text{M}$ from the acid. Only if $[\text{HClO}_4] < 10^{-6}\ \text{M}$ would water's contribution matter.

## Q9. $5 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{M}\ \text{Sr(OH)}_2$ — what is the pH?

$\text{Sr(OH)}_2$ is a strong base that dissociates completely, releasing 2 $\text{OH}^-$ per formula unit: $[\text{OH}^-] = 2 \times (5 \times 10^{-4}) = 1 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M}$. $\text{pOH} = -\log(10^{-3}) = 3.00$. $\text{pH} = 14 - 3 = 11.00$.

**Answer: D — $\text{pH} \approx 11.0$**

Diprotic strong bases like $\text{Sr(OH)}_2$, $\text{Ba(OH)}_2$, and $\text{Ca(OH)}_2$ release 2 $\text{OH}^-$ per formula unit. Multiply the molar concentration by 2 to get $[\text{OH}^-]$, then find pOH, then pH.

> **Trap:** Using $[\text{OH}^-] = 5 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{M}$ (ignoring the factor of 2). The "2" in $\text{Sr(OH)}_2$ means 2 moles of $\text{OH}^-$ are released per mole of salt. Forgetting this gives a pH of about 10.3 instead of 11.0.

## Q10. $\text{HCOOH}$ (formic acid) at concentrations 0.150 M, 0.125 M, 0.100 M, 0.075 M — which statement about 0.125 M sample is accurate?

Comparing 0.125 M to 0.100 M (a more dilute solution): 0.125 M is more concentrated, so it has **lower** % ionization, **higher** $[\text{H}_3\text{O}^+]$, and therefore higher pOH (higher $[\text{H}_3\text{O}^+]$ → lower pH → higher pOH since pH + pOH = 14).

**Answer: C — It has a lower percent ionization, a higher $[\text{H}_3\text{O}^+]_{\text{eq}}$, and a higher pOH than a 0.100 M HCOOH solution does.**

At 0.125 M relative to 0.100 M: more concentrated → fraction ionized decreases, absolute $[\text{H}_3\text{O}^+]$ increases, pH decreases, pOH increases. All three claims in C are correct.

> **Trap:** Choosing B (comparing to 0.150 M). While it is true that 0.125 M has higher % ionization and lower $[\text{H}_3\text{O}^+]$ than 0.150 M, B also claims **higher pOH** — which is wrong. Lower $[\text{H}_3\text{O}^+]$ → higher pH → **lower** pOH (pH + pOH = 14). The pOH direction in B is backwards.

## Q11. $0.100\ \text{M}\ \text{HClO}$ has $\text{pH} = 4.2$ — predict pH of $0.200\ \text{M}\ \text{HClO}$

Doubling the concentration of a weak acid increases $[\text{H}^+]$ (more acid to ionize), so pH decreases (becomes more acidic). The pH will be lower than 4.2, but not by a full log unit (not pH 3.2) because HClO is a weak acid with equilibrium.

**Answer: C — The pH is lower than 4.2 (more acidic) at 0.200 M.**

More weak acid → more ionization → more $[\text{H}^+]$ → lower pH. For a weak acid, doubling concentration roughly halves the ionization fraction but doubles the total acid, so $[\text{H}^+]$ increases by a factor of $\sqrt{2}$, decreasing pH by about 0.15 units.

> **Trap:** Predicting pH decreases by a full unit (to 3.2). Full-unit decrease applies only to strong acids. For weak acids, the relationship is $\text{pH} = \frac{1}{2}(\text{p}K_a - \log C)$, so doubling $C$ decreases pH by only ~0.15 units.

## Q12. $0.200\ \text{M}\ \text{NH}_3$ has $\text{pH} = 11.28$ — predict pH of $0.100\ \text{M}\ \text{NH}_3$

Halving the concentration of a weak base reduces $[\text{OH}^-]$, increasing pOH and decreasing pH. The solution is still basic but less basic than at 0.200 M.

**Answer: B — The pH is lower than 11.28 at 0.100 M.**

Diluting a weak base reduces the absolute amount of $\text{OH}^-$ produced (even though % ionization increases). Lower $[\text{OH}^-]$ → higher pOH → lower pH.

> **Trap:** Predicting pH increases (less basic = more acidic = higher pH?). Remember: lower pH always means more acidic / less basic. Halving $\text{NH}_3$ concentration makes the solution less basic → pH drops from 11.28 toward 7. Correct direction: pH decreases.

## Q13. Mix $10.0\ \text{mL}$ of $1.0\ \text{M}\ \text{HCl}$ with $11.0\ \text{mL}$ of $1.0\ \text{M}\ \text{NaOH}$ — what is the pH?

Moles $\text{HCl} = 0.0100\ \text{L} \times 1.0\ \text{M} = 0.0100\ \text{mol}$.
Moles $\text{NaOH} = 0.0110\ \text{L} \times 1.0\ \text{M} = 0.0110\ \text{mol}$.
Excess $\text{OH}^- = 0.0110 - 0.0100 = 0.0010\ \text{mol}$.
Total volume $= 21.0\ \text{mL} = 0.0210\ \text{L}$.
$[\text{OH}^-] = 0.0010/0.0210 = 4.76 \times 10^{-2}\ \text{M}$.
$\text{pOH} = -\log(4.76 \times 10^{-2}) \approx 1.32$. $\text{pH} \approx 12.68$.

**Answer: C — $\text{pH} = 14.00 + \log\!\left(\frac{0.0010}{0.0210}\right) = 12.68$**

After the stoichiometric neutralization of all HCl, $0.0010\ \text{mol}$ of $\text{OH}^-$ remains in $21.0\ \text{mL}$ total volume. $[\text{OH}^-] = 0.0010/0.0210 = 0.0476\ \text{M}$; $\text{pH} = 14.00 + \log(0.0476) = 12.68$.

> **Trap:** Choosing D — that expression divides by $0.0110\ \text{L}$ (only the NaOH volume), ignoring that both solutions were combined. Always use the total final volume ($10.0 + 11.0 = 21.0\ \text{mL}$) when calculating concentrations after mixing.

## Q14. $500.\ \text{mL}$ of $0.150\ \text{M}\ \text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$ ($\text{p}K_a = 4.76$) + $0.0200\ \text{mol}\ \text{NaOH}$ — find pH

Moles $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} = 0.500\ \text{L} \times 0.150\ \text{M} = 0.0750\ \text{mol}$.
NaOH converts acid to conjugate base: moles $\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^- = 0.0200\ \text{mol}$; moles $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$ remaining $= 0.0750 - 0.0200 = 0.0550\ \text{mol}$.
Henderson-Hasselbalch: $\text{pH} = 4.76 + \log(0.0200/0.0550) = 4.76 + \log(0.364) = 4.76 - 0.439 \approx 4.32$.

**Answer: C — $\text{pH} \approx 4.32$**

Adding NaOH partially neutralizes the weak acid, creating a buffer. Use the mole ratio (not concentration ratio — volume cancels) in Henderson-Hasselbalch. The ratio of moles can be used directly since both species are in the same volume.

> **Trap:** Using initial concentrations (before adding NaOH) instead of post-reaction moles. You must first complete the stoichiometry of the neutralization reaction, then apply Henderson-Hasselbalch with the resulting moles.

## Q15. $\text{NH}_3/\text{HCl}$ buffer: $\text{p}K_a(\text{NH}_4^+) = 9.25$ — find pH using specific moles given

Apply Henderson-Hasselbalch with $K_a$ of the conjugate acid $\text{NH}_4^+$: $\text{pH} = 9.25 + \log\frac{[\text{NH}_3]}{[\text{NH}_4^+]}$.

**Answer: B — pH calculated using H-H with the given moles of $\text{NH}_3$ and $\text{NH}_4^+$.**

The $\text{NH}_3/\text{NH}_4^+$ buffer is best treated using $K_a(\text{NH}_4^+)$: $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\text{base}]}{[\text{acid}]} = 9.25 + \log\frac{n(\text{NH}_3)}{n(\text{NH}_4^+)}$.

> **Trap:** Using $K_b(\text{NH}_3)$ and solving for pOH, then forgetting to convert to pH. Both approaches work, but the $K_a$ approach for the conjugate acid ($\text{NH}_4^+$) is direct and less error-prone.

## Q16. $60.0\ \text{mL}$ of NaOH of unknown concentration is titrated with $0.10\ \text{M}\ \text{HCl}$; equivalence point at ~30 mL — find $[\text{NaOH}]$

At equivalence: moles $\text{HCl}$ = moles $\text{NaOH}$.
$n(\text{HCl}) = 0.030\ \text{L} \times 0.10\ \text{M} = 0.0030\ \text{mol}$.
$[\text{NaOH}] = 0.0030\ \text{mol} / 0.060\ \text{L} = 0.050\ \text{M}$.

**Answer: B — $[\text{NaOH}] = 0.050\ \text{M}$**

At the equivalence point, moles of acid = moles of base. Divide the moles of titrant (HCl) by the volume of analyte (NaOH) to find the unknown concentration.

> **Trap:** Dividing by the volume of HCl (titrant) instead of the volume of NaOH (analyte). $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$ gives the correct relationship — solve for the unknown $M$ using its corresponding $V$.

## Q17. $0.10\ \text{M}\ \text{HNO}_2$ titrated with $0.10\ \text{M}\ \text{KOH}$: $6.0\ \text{mL}$ KOH added to $10.0\ \text{mL}\ \text{HNO}_2$ — which species dominates?

Moles $\text{HNO}_2 = 0.0100\ \text{L} \times 0.10\ \text{M} = 0.0010\ \text{mol}$.
Moles KOH added $= 0.0060\ \text{L} \times 0.10\ \text{M} = 0.00060\ \text{mol}$.
After neutralization: $\text{HNO}_2$ remaining $= 0.0010 - 0.00060 = 0.00040\ \text{mol}$; $\text{NO}_2^-$ formed $= 0.00060\ \text{mol}$.
The half-equivalence point is at $5.0\ \text{mL}$ KOH. At $6.0\ \text{mL}$ we are past it: $n(\text{NO}_2^-) = 0.00060 > n(\text{HNO}_2) = 0.00040$, so $\text{NO}_2^-$ is the dominant species.

**Answer: C — $\text{NO}_2^-(aq)$**

At $6.0\ \text{mL}$ of $0.10\ \text{M}$ KOH added to $10.0\ \text{mL}$ of $0.10\ \text{M}\ \text{HNO}_2$, the system is past the half-equivalence point ($5.0\ \text{mL}$). More $\text{HNO}_2$ has been converted to $\text{NO}_2^-$ than remains as $\text{HNO}_2$, so $\text{NO}_2^-$ is in the greatest concentration.

> **Trap:** Choosing B ($\text{HNO}_2$). At exactly $5.0\ \text{mL}$ KOH (the half-equivalence point), $[\text{HNO}_2] = [\text{NO}_2^-]$. At $6.0\ \text{mL}$ — past the half-equivalence — the conjugate base $\text{NO}_2^-$ exceeds the remaining weak acid.

## Q18. Titration curve for KHP (weak acid salt) — reading $\text{p}K_a$ from the half-equivalence point

At the half-equivalence point on a weak acid titration curve, $[\text{HA}] = [\text{A}^-]$. By Henderson-Hasselbalch: $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log(1) = \text{p}K_a$. The pH at the half-equivalence volume directly equals $\text{p}K_a$.

**Answer: B — $\text{p}K_a \approx 5.4$** (reading the pH at the half-equivalence volume from the given curve).

Half-equivalence volume = equivalence volume / 2. The pH at that volume on the titration curve equals $\text{p}K_a$. This is the single most testable concept in titration curves.

> **Trap:** Reading the pH at the equivalence point instead of the half-equivalence point. At equivalence for a weak acid/strong base titration, pH > 7 (conjugate base hydrolyzes). The $\text{p}K_a$ is read at the halfway point, not the endpoint.

## Q19. Two brominated acetic acids compared — more Br substituents = stronger acid. Why?

More electronegative Br atoms pull electron density away from the O–H bond via induction. This weakens the O–H bond, making proton release easier. More importantly, more Br atoms stabilize the conjugate base anion more effectively through inductive withdrawal, increasing $K_a$.

**Answer: D — More Br atoms more effectively stabilize the conjugate base anion through inductive electron withdrawal, increasing acid strength.**

Acid strength correlates with conjugate base stability. Electron-withdrawing groups (like Br) stabilize the negative charge on the conjugate base by dispersing it through induction. More Br atoms → greater stabilization → weaker O–H bond → stronger acid → lower $\text{p}K_a$.

> **Trap:** Choosing resonance as the explanation. Induction (through-bond electron withdrawal), not resonance, is the mechanism here. Resonance would require delocalization within the same functional group; Br on the alpha carbon acts inductively.

## Q20. $\text{H}_2\text{SeO}_4$ vs. $\text{H}_2\text{TeO}_4$ — which is stronger and why?

Going down Group 16: Se and Te are both less electronegative than O. Te is larger and less electronegative than Se. Less electronegativity of the central atom → less electron withdrawal from the O–H bond → less stable conjugate base → weaker acid.

**Answer: D — $\text{H}_2\text{TeO}_4$ is the weaker acid because Te is less electronegative than Se, providing less inductive stabilization of the conjugate base.**

For oxyacids of the same structure, acid strength increases with the electronegativity of the central atom. Se (more electronegative than Te) withdraws more electron density, weakening the O–H bond more and stabilizing the anion better → $\text{H}_2\text{SeO}_4$ is stronger.

> **Trap:** Thinking the larger central atom always gives a stronger acid. For binary acids (H–X), larger atom = stronger acid (weaker H–X bond). For oxyacids, electronegativity of the central atom governs strength, not size directly.

## Q21. $\text{NH}_3$ vs. $\text{NF}_3$ as bases — which is weaker and why?

Both have N as the central atom with a lone pair. In $\text{NF}_3$, three highly electronegative F atoms pull electron density away from N, reducing the availability of N's lone pair for proton acceptance. In $\text{NH}_3$, H is less electronegative and does not reduce N's electron density. $\text{NF}_3$ is therefore a far weaker base.

**Answer: C — $\text{NF}_3$ is the weaker base because the electronegative F atoms withdraw electron density from N, making the lone pair less available for bonding with $\text{H}^+$.**

Base strength depends on lone pair availability. Electron-withdrawing substituents (like F) reduce electron density on N, weakening its basic character. Electron-donating substituents (like alkyl groups) enhance N's basicity.

> **Trap:** Confusing with the steric argument. While F atoms are large, the primary reason is electronic (inductive withdrawal), not steric hindrance. The AP exam expects an electronegativity/electron density explanation.

## Q22. $\text{HClO}/\text{NaClO}$ buffer with $\text{pH} = 8.40$ and $\text{p}K_a(\text{HClO}) = 7.40$ — compare $[\text{ClO}^-]$ and $[\text{HClO}]$

$\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\text{ClO}^-]}{[\text{HClO}]}$. $8.40 = 7.40 + \log\frac{[\text{ClO}^-]}{[\text{HClO}]}$. $\log\frac{[\text{ClO}^-]}{[\text{HClO}]} = 1.00$. $\frac{[\text{ClO}^-]}{[\text{HClO}]} = 10$. So $[\text{ClO}^-] = 10 \times [\text{HClO}]$.

**Answer: B — $[\text{ClO}^-] > [\text{HClO}]$; specifically 10 times greater.**

When $\text{pH} > \text{p}K_a$: the conjugate base is the dominant species. When $\text{pH} < \text{p}K_a$: the weak acid dominates. When $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a$: equal concentrations. Here pH is 1 unit above $\text{p}K_a$, so the ratio is 10:1 in favor of $\text{ClO}^-$.

> **Trap:** Reversing the comparison. Remember: pH above $\text{p}K_a$ → more conjugate base. A quick way to remember: higher pH = more basic = more of the base form ($\text{A}^-$). Lower pH = more acidic = more of the acid form (HA).

## Q23. $\text{HCNO}$ with $\text{p}K_a = 3.5$ — at which pH does $[\text{CNO}^-] > [\text{HCNO}]$?

$[\text{CNO}^-] > [\text{HCNO}]$ when the log term is positive in H-H, i.e., when $\text{pH} > \text{p}K_a = 3.5$. Any pH above 3.5 gives $[\text{CNO}^-] > [\text{HCNO}]$.

**Answer: D — pH = 4.0** (the only answer choice above $\text{p}K_a = 3.5$).

The conjugate base dominates when pH > $\text{p}K_a$; the weak acid dominates when pH < $\text{p}K_a$. Since $\text{p}K_a = 3.5$, pH = 4.0 > 3.5 means $[\text{CNO}^-] > [\text{HCNO}]$.

> **Trap:** Choosing pH = 3.5 (equal concentrations). At pH = $\text{p}K_a$, the acid and conjugate base are present in exactly equal concentrations. Only pH values above 3.5 tip the balance toward the conjugate base.

## Q24. $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{COOH}$ (propanoic acid, $\text{p}K_a = 4.25$): measured pH = 3.55 — what does this indicate about concentration?

$\text{pH} = 3.55 < \text{p}K_a = 4.25$. When pH is below $\text{p}K_a$, the weak acid ($[\text{HA}]$) dominates over the conjugate base ($[\text{A}^-]$). This means the sample is predominantly in the acid form.

**Answer: B — $[\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{COOH}] > [\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{COO}^-]$ (weak acid dominates conjugate base).**

pH < $\text{p}K_a$ always means the acid form is in excess. At the measured pH of 3.55, the ratio $[\text{A}^-]/[\text{HA}] = 10^{(3.55-4.25)} = 10^{-0.70} \approx 0.20$, confirming that $[\text{HA}]$ is about 5 times $[\text{A}^-]$.

> **Trap:** Confusing the direction of the inequality. Below $\text{p}K_a$ = acid dominates; above $\text{p}K_a$ = conjugate base dominates. This is one of the most frequently tested H-H interpretation skills.

## Q25. Propanoic acid buffer — small amount of NaOH is added — what happens to pH?

Adding a small amount of strong base to a buffer: the base reacts with the weak acid component ($\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{COOH} + \text{OH}^- \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{COO}^- + \text{H}_2\text{O}$), slightly increasing $[\text{A}^-]$ and decreasing $[\text{HA}]$. The ratio $[\text{A}^-]/[\text{HA}]$ increases slightly, so pH increases slightly.

**Answer: D — The pH increases slightly.**

Buffers resist large pH changes by consuming added acid or base. A small addition of strong base is partially neutralized, converting some weak acid to conjugate base. The resulting pH increase is small — this is the definition of buffer action.

> **Trap:** Choosing "pH stays exactly the same." Buffers minimize pH change but do not eliminate it. The pH does change slightly — just much less than it would in an unbuffered solution.

## Q26. $\text{HCOOH}/\text{HCOOK}$ buffer — small amount of HCl is added — which species neutralizes it?

Adding $\text{HCl}$ (strong acid, source of $\text{H}^+$): the conjugate base ($\text{HCOO}^-$) accepts the proton: $\text{HCOO}^-(aq) + \text{H}^+(aq) \rightarrow \text{HCOOH}(aq)$.

**Answer: A — $\text{HCOO}^-$ accepts the added proton, converting to $\text{HCOOH}$.**

Buffers neutralize added acid using the conjugate base component. $\text{HCOO}^-$ (formate) is the conjugate base of formic acid, so it accepts $\text{H}^+$ from HCl, generating more $\text{HCOOH}$ and preventing a sharp pH drop.

> **Trap:** Choosing HCOOH as the species that reacts. HCOOH is the weak acid — it reacts with added base, not added acid. The conjugate base reacts with added acid; the weak acid reacts with added base.

## Q27. $1.0\ \text{L}$ of $0.30\ \text{M}\ \text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$ ($\text{p}K_a = 4.76$) + $0.10\ \text{mol}\ \text{NaOH}$ — find pH

Moles $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} = 0.30\ \text{mol}$. NaOH converts $0.10\ \text{mol}$ to conjugate base.
After reaction: $[\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}] = 0.20\ \text{mol}$; $[\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-] = 0.10\ \text{mol}$.
$\text{pH} = 4.76 + \log(0.10/0.20) = 4.76 + \log(0.5) = 4.76 - 0.301 \approx 4.46$.

**Answer: B — $\text{pH} = 4.76 + \log\dfrac{0.10}{0.20} = 4.46$**

Perform the stoichiometry first: $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$ remaining $= 0.30 - 0.10 = 0.20\ \text{mol}$; $\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-$ formed $= 0.10\ \text{mol}$. Then Henderson-Hasselbalch with the ratio $[\text{A}^-]/[\text{HA}] = 0.10/0.20$: $\text{pH} = 4.76 + \log(0.5) = 4.76 - 0.30 = 4.46$.

> **Trap:** Choosing C — that expression uses $\log(0.20/0.10)$, inverting the H-H ratio (putting $[\text{HA}]$ in the numerator instead of $[\text{A}^-]$). Inverted ratio gives 5.06 instead of 4.46. In H-H, the numerator is always the conjugate base (the species formed by the NaOH reaction).

## Q28. Student preparing $\text{NH}_3/\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$ buffer — accidentally adds less $\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$ than intended — what changes?

Less $\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$ means lower $[\text{NH}_4^+]$ than intended. By H-H: $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\text{NH}_3]}{[\text{NH}_4^+]}$. With less $[\text{NH}_4^+]$ in the denominator, the ratio increases → pH is higher than intended. The buffer also has greater capacity to absorb added acid (more base $\text{NH}_3$ relative to conjugate acid).

**Answer: D — The solution has a greater buffer capacity for the addition of acid than for base, because $[\text{NH}_3] > [\text{NH}_4^+]$.**

Less $\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$ added means $[\text{NH}_4^+]$ is lower than intended while $[\text{NH}_3]$ stays at $1.0\ \text{M}$, so $[\text{NH}_3] > [\text{NH}_4^+]$. More $\text{NH}_3$ available → greater capacity to neutralize added acid. Less $\text{NH}_4^+$ → less capacity to neutralize added base.

> **Trap:** Choosing B — B states greater capacity for base, which is backwards. It is the conjugate acid ($\text{NH}_4^+$) that neutralizes added base; the base ($\text{NH}_3$) neutralizes added acid. With less $\text{NH}_4^+$, base-neutralizing capacity decreases, not increases.

## Q29. $\text{HC}_3\text{H}_5\text{O}_2$ buffer + $10\times$ more concentrated $\text{NaOH}$ than intended — what happens?

Adding 10× more concentrated NaOH likely far exceeds the buffer capacity — it may completely neutralize all the weak acid (and then some). If all weak acid is consumed, the buffer is destroyed. pH rises dramatically (excess strong base dominates).

**Answer: C — The pH is much higher than intended; the acid component is likely completely neutralized, destroying the buffer.**

Buffer capacity is finite. Overwhelming the buffer with excess strong base consumes all the weak acid. Once the buffer is destroyed, any additional NaOH is free to raise the pH sharply, far beyond the intended buffer range.

> **Trap:** Thinking the buffer still "resists" a 10× excess. Buffers only work up to their capacity. Once either the acid or base component is exhausted, the system behaves like an unbuffered solution.

## Q30. HNO₂ buffer pH measurements: probe not rinsed between samples; Sample 3 is acidic (low pH), Sample 4 immediately follows — what systematic error occurs?

If the pH probe is not rinsed after measuring Sample 3 (which has low pH due to high $[\text{H}^+]$), residual acid from Sample 3 is transferred to Sample 4's measurement environment. This artificially lowers the measured pH of Sample 4 relative to its true value.

**Answer: B — Residual acid from the low-pH sample (Sample 3) is transferred to Sample 4, causing Sample 4's measured pH to be lower than its true pH.**

Cross-contamination from an acidic sample lowers the apparent pH of the next sample. This is a systematic error: if the pattern is acidic sample → unrinsed probe → next sample, the next measurement is always biased low.

> **Trap:** Thinking the error only applies to the probe reading and not to the actual solution. The probe itself doesn't change the solution — but trace amounts of acid solution carried on the probe tip do affect the apparent measurement and introduce cross-sample contamination.

## Q31. Saturated $\text{Mg(OH)}_2$ solution — which substance added would make it cloudier (more precipitate)?

Cloudier = more precipitate of $\text{Mg(OH)}_2$. For the equilibrium $\text{Mg(OH)}_2(s) \rightleftharpoons \text{Mg}^{2+}(aq) + 2\text{OH}^-(aq)$:
- Adding $\text{OH}^-$ (from a base like KOH) → common-ion effect → shifts left → more $\text{Mg(OH)}_2$ precipitates → cloudier.
- Adding an acid removes $\text{OH}^-$, shifts right → more dissolves → less cloudy.

**Answer: D — Adding KOH (or another source of $\text{OH}^-$) increases $[\text{OH}^-]$, shifting the equilibrium left and causing more $\text{Mg(OH)}_2$ to precipitate.**

The common-ion effect with $\text{OH}^-$: excess hydroxide suppresses dissolution, precipitating more solid. This makes the solution cloudier, not clearer.

> **Trap:** Choosing acetic acid, thinking more reaction = cloudier. Adding acid ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$) removes $\text{OH}^-$ by neutralization, shifting equilibrium right → more $\text{Mg(OH)}_2$ dissolves → less cloudy (clearer). Adding base makes it cloudier.

## Q32. $\text{CaF}_2$ solubility — how does decreasing pH (adding acid) affect solubility?

$\text{CaF}_2(s) \rightleftharpoons \text{Ca}^{2+}(aq) + 2\text{F}^-(aq)$. In acidic solution: $\text{H}^+ + \text{F}^- \rightarrow \text{HF}(aq)$. This removes $\text{F}^-$ from solution, shifting the dissolution equilibrium right → more $\text{CaF}_2$ dissolves. Lower pH → higher solubility.

**Answer: C — $\text{CaF}_2$ is more soluble at lower pH because $\text{H}^+$ reacts with $\text{F}^-$ to form $\text{HF}$, removing the product and driving further dissolution.**

This is the "acid effect on solubility": salts of weak acids dissolve more readily in acidic solution. $\text{F}^-$ is the conjugate base of the weak acid HF, so it is protonated in acid. Removing $\text{F}^-$ from equilibrium pulls more $\text{CaF}_2$ into solution.

> **Trap:** Choosing that solubility is unaffected by pH. Only salts of strong acids (e.g., $\text{AgCl}$, $\text{BaSO}_4$) are pH-independent because $\text{Cl}^-$ and $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ are conjugate bases of strong acids and do not react with $\text{H}^+$. Salts of weak acids are always pH-sensitive.

## Q33. $\text{Cu(OH)}_2$ absorbance at different pH values — lower pH → higher absorbance. Why?

$\text{Cu(OH)}_2(s) \rightleftharpoons \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + 2\text{OH}^-(aq)$. At lower pH (higher $[\text{H}^+]$): $\text{H}^+$ reacts with $\text{OH}^-$, removing it from equilibrium and driving dissolution forward → higher $[\text{Cu}^{2+}]$ → higher absorbance (since $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ is the colored species).

**Answer: A — Lower pH leads to greater dissolution of $\text{Cu(OH)}_2$, increasing $[\text{Cu}^{2+}]$ and thus absorbance.**

$\text{Cu(OH)}_2$ is a salt of the weak base $\text{OH}^-$; adding acid removes $\text{OH}^-$, increasing solubility. Higher $[\text{Cu}^{2+}]$ in solution = higher absorbance (Beer's Law: $A = \varepsilon lc$, higher $c$ → higher $A$).

> **Trap:** Thinking absorbance should be higher at higher pH (thinking "more basic = more Cu(OH)₂ = more colored"). At higher pH, $\text{Cu(OH)}_2$ is less soluble (common-ion effect from $\text{OH}^-$). Less $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ in solution = lower absorbance.
