## Topic 8.1: Introduction to Acids and Bases

**Example 1 — Conjugate acid-base pair identification (Krug 8.1a type):**
For each reaction, identify the acid, base, conjugate acid, and conjugate base:

(a) $\text{HF}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \rightleftharpoons \text{F}^-(aq) + \text{H}_3\text{O}^+(aq)$

(b) $\text{NH}_3(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \rightleftharpoons \text{NH}_4^+(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq)$

(a): HF donates $\text{H}^+$ → acid. $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ accepts $\text{H}^+$ → base. $\text{F}^-$ is the conjugate base of HF. $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$ is the conjugate acid of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$.

(b): $\text{NH}_3$ accepts $\text{H}^+$ → base. $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ donates $\text{H}^+$ → acid. $\text{NH}_4^+$ is the conjugate acid of $\text{NH}_3$. $\text{OH}^-$ is the conjugate base of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$.

*Key move: Conjugate pairs differ by exactly one $\text{H}^+$. The acid and its conjugate base are one pair; the base and its conjugate acid are the second pair.*

---

**Example 2 — $K_w$ and neutral pH at elevated temperature (Progress Check Q5/Q6 type):**
At 55°C, $K_w = 7.0 \times 10^{-14}$. (a) Find the pH of pure water at 55°C. (b) Is the water acidic, basic, or neutral?

(a) In pure water, $[\text{H}^+] = [\text{OH}^-] = \sqrt{K_w}$:
$$[\text{H}^+] = \sqrt{7.0 \times 10^{-14}} = 2.65 \times 10^{-7}\ M$$
$$\text{pH} = -\log(2.65 \times 10^{-7}) = 6.58$$

(b) $[\text{H}^+] = [\text{OH}^-]$, so the water is **neutral** — not acidic. The numerical pH is below 7, but neutrality is defined by equal concentrations, not by pH = 7.

**Answers: (a) pH ≈ 6.58; (b) Neutral**

> Trap: Calling 55°C water "acidic" because pH < 7. The scale shifts with temperature. At 55°C the neutral pH is 6.58. "Acidic" means $[\text{H}^+] > [\text{OH}^-]$, which is not true in pure water at any temperature.

*Key move: Neutral = $[\text{H}^+] = [\text{OH}^-]$, not pH = 7. The value of neutral pH depends on $K_w$, which depends on temperature.*

---

**Example 3 — Endothermic autoionization and Le Chatelier (Progress Check Q6 type):**
The autoionization of water has $\Delta H = +56\ \text{kJ/mol}$. Explain why $K_w$ increases with temperature and whether the solution remains neutral.

Higher temperature adds heat to an **endothermic** process. Le Chatelier: adding heat to a heat-requiring (endothermic) reaction shifts equilibrium forward → more $\text{H}^+$ and $\text{OH}^-$ produced → $K_w$ increases.

The water remains **neutral** because both $[\text{H}^+]$ and $[\text{OH}^-]$ increase equally (the stoichiometry is 1:1). Neutrality requires $[\text{H}^+] = [\text{OH}^-]$, which always holds for pure water.

*Key move: Use Le Chatelier to explain the temperature dependence of $K_w$. The solution is still neutral — temperature changes the scale, not the neutrality.*

## Topic 8.2: pH and pOH of Strong Acids and Bases

**Example 4 — pH of a strong acid (Progress Check Q7/Q8 type):**
Find the pH of (a) $0.10\ M\ \text{HBr}$ and (b) $1.0 \times 10^{-5}\ M\ \text{HClO}_4$.

(a) HBr is strong: $[\text{H}^+] = 0.10\ M$.
$$\text{pH} = -\log(0.10) = 1.00$$

(b) $\text{HClO}_4$ is strong: $[\text{H}^+] = 1.0 \times 10^{-5}\ M$.
$$\text{pH} = -\log(1.0 \times 10^{-5}) = 5.00$$

**Answers: (a) pH = 1.00; (b) pH = 5.00**

> Trap: Using a $K_a$ expression for HBr or $\text{HClO}_4$. These are strong acids with complete dissociation. There is no equilibrium to solve — set $[\text{H}^+]$ equal to the stated concentration and take the negative log.

---

**Example 5 — pH of a diprotic strong base (Progress Check Q9 type):**
Find the pH of $5.0 \times 10^{-4}\ M\ \text{Sr(OH)}_2$.

$\text{Sr(OH)}_2$ dissociates completely, releasing 2 $\text{OH}^-$ per formula unit:
$$[\text{OH}^-] = 2 \times (5.0 \times 10^{-4}) = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\ M$$
$$\text{pOH} = -\log(1.0 \times 10^{-3}) = 3.00$$
$$\text{pH} = 14.00 - 3.00 = 11.00$$

**Answer: pH = 11.00**

> Trap: Using $[\text{OH}^-] = 5.0 \times 10^{-4}\ M$ (forgetting the factor of 2). Each mole of $\text{Sr(OH)}_2$ yields 2 mol $\text{OH}^-$. The pH would then be 14 − (−log 5×10⁻⁴) = 14 − 3.30 = 10.70 — a significant error.

**Example 6 — Mixing strong acid and strong base (Progress Check Q13 type):**
$10.0\ \text{mL}$ of $1.0\ M\ \text{HCl}$ is mixed with $11.0\ \text{mL}$ of $1.0\ M\ \text{NaOH}$. Find the pH.

Step 1: Calculate moles.
$n(\text{HCl}) = 0.0100\ \text{L} \times 1.0\ \text{M} = 0.0100\ \text{mol}$
$n(\text{NaOH}) = 0.0110\ \text{L} \times 1.0\ \text{M} = 0.0110\ \text{mol}$

Step 2: Find excess.
Excess $\text{OH}^- = 0.0110 - 0.0100 = 0.0010\ \text{mol}$

Step 3: Total volume.
$V_{\text{total}} = 10.0 + 11.0 = 21.0\ \text{mL} = 0.0210\ \text{L}$

Step 4: Calculate $[\text{OH}^-]$.
$$[\text{OH}^-] = \frac{0.0010\ \text{mol}}{0.0210\ \text{L}} = 4.76 \times 10^{-2}\ M$$

Step 5: Find pH.
$$\text{pOH} = -\log(4.76 \times 10^{-2}) = 1.32$$
$$\text{pH} = 14.00 - 1.32 = 12.68$$

**Answer: pH = 12.68**

> Trap: Dividing by only the NaOH volume ($0.0110\ \text{L}$) instead of the total volume. After mixing, both solutions occupy the full combined volume. Always use $V_{\text{total}} = V_1 + V_2$.

*Key move: Three steps — (1) calculate moles, (2) find excess reactant, (3) divide by total volume. Never divide by only one original volume.*

## Topic 8.3: Weak Acid and Base Equilibria

**Example 7 — Weak acid ICE table: finding pH (Krug 8.3a type):**
Find the pH of $0.100\ M\ \text{HF}$, $K_a = 6.8 \times 10^{-4}$.

| | $\text{HF}$ | $\text{H}^+$ | $\text{F}^-$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | 0.100 | 0 | 0 |
| C | $-x$ | $+x$ | $+x$ |
| E | $0.100 - x$ | $x$ | $x$ |

$$K_a = \frac{x^2}{0.100 - x} = 6.8 \times 10^{-4}$$

Approximation: $x \ll 0.100$:
$$x^2 \approx (6.8 \times 10^{-4})(0.100) = 6.8 \times 10^{-5}$$
$$x = \sqrt{6.8 \times 10^{-5}} = 8.25 \times 10^{-3}\ M$$

Verify: $8.25 \times 10^{-3}/0.100 = 8.25\%$ > 5% — approximation fails! Solve quadratic:
$$x^2 + 6.8 \times 10^{-4}x - 6.8 \times 10^{-5} = 0$$
$$x = \frac{-6.8 \times 10^{-4} + \sqrt{(6.8 \times 10^{-4})^2 + 4(6.8 \times 10^{-5})}}{2}$$
$$x \approx 7.93 \times 10^{-3}\ M$$
$$\text{pH} = -\log(7.93 \times 10^{-3}) = 2.10$$

**Answer: pH ≈ 2.10**

*Key move: Always verify the 5% approximation. If it fails, solve the quadratic. Take only the positive root that gives a physically meaningful (positive) equilibrium concentration.*

**Example 8 — Dilution effect on weak acid (Progress Check Q10/Q11 type):**
$0.150\ M\ \text{HCOOH}$ has a measured pH. Predict: (a) does $[\text{H}^+]$ increase or decrease at $0.100\ M$? (b) Does % ionization increase or decrease?

(a) At lower concentration, there are fewer moles of acid total. Even though a larger fraction ionizes, the absolute $[\text{H}^+]$ is smaller → **pH increases** (less acidic).

(b) Diluting a weak acid shifts equilibrium right (Le Chatelier: fewer ions, equilibrium moves toward ionization). A larger fraction ionizes → **% ionization increases**.

**Answers: (a) $[\text{H}^+]$ decreases (pH increases); (b) % ionization increases**

> Trap: Assuming higher % ionization means higher $[\text{H}^+]$. These trend in opposite directions on dilution. More of a smaller amount can still be less than less of a larger amount.

---

**Example 9 — Comparing weak acid concentrations from pH data (Progress Check Q11 type):**
$0.100\ M\ \text{HClO}$ has $\text{pH} = 4.2$. Predict the pH of $0.200\ M\ \text{HClO}$.

For a weak acid, $[\text{H}^+] = \sqrt{K_a C}$. Doubling $C$:
$$[\text{H}^+]_{\text{new}} = \sqrt{K_a \times 2C} = \sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{K_a C} = \sqrt{2} \times [\text{H}^+]_{\text{old}}$$

$$\text{pH}_{\text{new}} = \text{pH}_{\text{old}} - \log\sqrt{2} = 4.2 - 0.15 = 4.05$$

**Answer: pH ≈ 4.05** (slightly lower than 4.2; less acidic change than a strong acid).

> Trap: Predicting pH drops to 3.2 (a full unit). A full unit drop per 10× concentration applies to strong acids. Weak acids decrease by $\frac{1}{2}\log 10 = 0.5$ units per 10× concentration, so 2× concentration drops pH by $\frac{1}{2}\log 2 \approx 0.15$ units.

## Topic 8.4: Acid-Base Reactions and Buffers

**Example 10 — Buffer neutralization direction (Progress Check Q1/Q26 type):**
A buffer is made from $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$ and $\text{NaHCO}_3$ ($K_b(\text{CO}_3^{2-}) = 2.1 \times 10^{-4}$).

(a) Which component neutralizes added strong base? Write the equation.
(b) Which component neutralizes added strong acid? Write the equation.

(a) Strong base ($\text{OH}^-$) is neutralized by the **weak acid component** ($\text{HCO}_3^-$):
$$\text{HCO}_3^-(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq) \to \text{CO}_3^{2-}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$

(b) Strong acid ($\text{H}^+$) is neutralized by the **conjugate base component** ($\text{CO}_3^{2-}$):
$$\text{CO}_3^{2-}(aq) + \text{H}^+(aq) \to \text{HCO}_3^-(aq)$$

*Key move: Weak acid neutralizes added base; conjugate base neutralizes added acid. Never mix up the two directions.*

---

**Example 11 — Henderson-Hasselbalch with $K_b$ given (Progress Check Q2 type):**
Buffer: $[\text{CO}_3^{2-}] = 0.100\ M$, $[\text{HCO}_3^-] = 0.150\ M$. $K_b(\text{CO}_3^{2-}) = 2.1 \times 10^{-4}$.

Convert $K_b$ to $K_a$:
$$K_a(\text{HCO}_3^-) = \frac{K_w}{K_b} = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{2.1 \times 10^{-4}} = 4.76 \times 10^{-11}$$
$$\text{p}K_a = -\log(4.76 \times 10^{-11}) = 10.32$$

Apply Henderson-Hasselbalch ($[\text{A}^-] = [\text{CO}_3^{2-}]$; $[\text{HA}] = [\text{HCO}_3^-]$):
$$\text{pH} = 10.32 + \log\frac{0.100}{0.150} = 10.32 + \log(0.667) = 10.32 - 0.18 = 10.14$$

**Answer: pH ≈ 10.14**

> Trap: Putting $[\text{HCO}_3^-]$ in the numerator. In H-H, the numerator is always the **conjugate base** ($\text{CO}_3^{2-}$, the species with the extra negative charge). Inverting gives 10.50.

---

**Example 12 — Same pH despite different concentrations (Progress Check Q3 type):**
Buffer 2: $[\text{CO}_3^{2-}] = 0.200\ M$, $[\text{HCO}_3^-] = 0.300\ M$.
Buffer 3: $[\text{CO}_3^{2-}] = 0.100\ M$, $[\text{HCO}_3^-] = 0.150\ M$.

Why do they have the same pH?

Both have $[\text{CO}_3^{2-}]/[\text{HCO}_3^-] = 0.200/0.300 = 0.100/0.150 = 2/3$. Since H-H depends only on this ratio and $\text{p}K_a$, the pH is identical for both.

**Answer:** Same ratio → same log term → same pH. Absolute concentrations determine buffer capacity, not pH.

> Trap: Thinking higher concentrations give different pH. Diluting a buffer by 2× divides both components by 2; the ratio is unchanged; pH is unchanged.

**Example 13 — Partial neutralization to form a buffer (Progress Check Q14 type):**
$500.\ \text{mL}$ of $0.150\ M\ \text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$ ($\text{p}K_a = 4.76$) + $0.0200\ \text{mol NaOH}$.

Step 1: Moles of acid before reaction.
$$n(\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}) = 0.500\ \text{L} \times 0.150\ \text{M} = 0.0750\ \text{mol}$$

Step 2: After neutralization.
$\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$ remaining $= 0.0750 - 0.0200 = 0.0550\ \text{mol}$
$\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-$ formed $= 0.0200\ \text{mol}$

Step 3: Henderson-Hasselbalch (moles work since same volume).
$$\text{pH} = 4.76 + \log\frac{0.0200}{0.0550} = 4.76 + \log(0.364) = 4.76 - 0.439 \approx 4.32$$

**Answer: pH ≈ 4.32**

> Trap: Using initial concentrations without accounting for the NaOH neutralization. You must first do the stoichiometry, then apply H-H to the resulting moles.

## Topic 8.5: Acid-Base Titrations

**Example 14 — Finding unknown concentration from titration (Progress Check Q16 type):**
$60.0\ \text{mL}$ of NaOH (unknown concentration) is titrated with $0.10\ M\ \text{HCl}$; equivalence point at $30.\ \text{mL HCl}$.

At equivalence: moles HCl = moles NaOH.
$$n(\text{HCl}) = 0.030\ \text{L} \times 0.10\ \text{M} = 0.0030\ \text{mol}$$
$$[\text{NaOH}] = \frac{0.0030\ \text{mol}}{0.060\ \text{L}} = 0.050\ M$$

**Answer: $[\text{NaOH}] = 0.050\ M$**

> Trap: Dividing by the HCl volume (30 mL) instead of the NaOH volume (60 mL). The $M_{\text{unknown}}$ is found by dividing moles of titrant added by the volume of the **analyte** (the unknown solution).

---

**Example 15 — Dominant species at a specific titration volume (Progress Check Q17 type):**
$10.0\ \text{mL}$ of $0.10\ M\ \text{HNO}_2$ titrated with $0.10\ M\ \text{KOH}$; $6.0\ \text{mL}$ KOH added. Which species is in greatest concentration?

Step 1: Find moles.
$n(\text{HNO}_2) = 0.0100\ \text{L} \times 0.10 = 0.0010\ \text{mol}$
$n(\text{KOH}) = 0.0060\ \text{L} \times 0.10 = 0.00060\ \text{mol}$

Step 2: After neutralization.
$\text{HNO}_2$ remaining $= 0.0010 - 0.00060 = 0.00040\ \text{mol}$
$\text{NO}_2^-$ formed $= 0.00060\ \text{mol}$

Step 3: Compare.
$n(\text{NO}_2^-) = 0.00060 > n(\text{HNO}_2) = 0.00040$

The half-equivalence point is at $5.0\ \text{mL}$; at $6.0\ \text{mL}$ we're past it — the conjugate base now dominates.

**Answer: $\text{NO}_2^-$ is the dominant species.**

> Trap: Choosing $\text{HNO}_2$ (weak acid). Before the half-equivalence point, acid > conjugate base. Past the half-equivalence, conjugate base > acid. At $6.0\ \text{mL}$ (past $5.0\ \text{mL}$ half-equiv), $\text{NO}_2^-$ dominates.

**Example 16 — Reading $\text{p}K_a$ from a titration curve (Progress Check Q18 type):**
A titration curve shows the equivalence point at $V_e = 20.0\ \text{mL}$ and pH = 8.2 at the equivalence. The pH at $10.0\ \text{mL}$ (half-equivalence) reads as $5.4$. What is $\text{p}K_a$?

At the half-equivalence point: $[\text{HA}] = [\text{A}^-]$ → $\log(1) = 0$ → $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a$.

$$\text{p}K_a = 5.4$$

**Answer: $\text{p}K_a \approx 5.4$**

> Trap: Reading the pH at the equivalence point (8.2) as $\text{p}K_a$. The equivalence point pH > 7 for a weak acid/strong base titration because the conjugate base hydrolyzes. The $\text{p}K_a$ is always read at the half-equivalence volume ($V_e/2$).

## Topic 8.6: Molecular Structure of Acids and Bases

**Example 17 — Inductive effect: halogenated acetic acids (Progress Check Q19 type):**
$\text{CHBrCl·COOH}$ has $K_a = 1.5 \times 10^{-3}$; $\text{CH}_2\text{Cl·COOH}$ has $K_a = 1.4 \times 10^{-3}$. The acid with two electronegative substituents is stronger. Explain.

More electronegative atoms on the alpha carbon pull electron density **inductively** through the C–C bond from the carboxyl group. This weakens the O–H bond and, more importantly, stabilizes the carboxylate anion (conjugate base) by dispersing the negative charge. Greater dispersion → more stable conjugate base → lower $\text{p}K_a$ → stronger acid.

**Answer:** More electronegative substituents stabilize the conjugate base through inductive electron withdrawal, increasing acid strength. The effect is additive: each electronegative substituent contributes.

> Trap: Attributing the effect to resonance. Inductive withdrawal through the alpha carbon is the mechanism, not resonance. Resonance would require direct delocalization into the carboxylate, not through a saturated C–C bond.

---

**Example 18 — Oxyacid comparison across period (Progress Check Q20 type):**
Compare acid strengths of $\text{H}_2\text{SeO}_4$ and $\text{H}_2\text{TeO}_4$.

Both have the same oxyacid structure with the same number of oxygen atoms. The difference is the central atom: Se (Group 16, period 4) vs. Te (Group 16, period 5).

Se is more electronegative than Te. Greater electronegativity of the central atom → greater inductive withdrawal of electron density from the O–H bond → weaker O–H bond → proton released more readily → stronger acid.

**Answer: $\text{H}_2\text{SeO}_4 > \text{H}_2\text{TeO}_4$ in acid strength.** Se is more electronegative than Te.

> Trap: Thinking the larger atom (Te) always gives the stronger acid. The "larger atom = stronger acid" rule applies to **binary acids** (H–X), where bond strength is the dominant factor. For oxyacids, electronegativity of the central atom governs strength.

**Example 19 — Substituent effect on base strength (Progress Check Q21 type):**
Compare $\text{NH}_3$ and $\text{NF}_3$ as bases.

In $\text{NH}_3$: N has a lone pair readily available; H is less electronegative than N.
In $\text{NF}_3$: three F atoms (highly electronegative) withdraw electron density from N through induction, reducing the availability of N's lone pair for proton acceptance.

$\text{NF}_3$ is a far weaker base because its N lone pair is less available.

**Answer: $\text{NF}_3$ is the weaker base.** Electronegative F atoms inductively withdraw electron density from N, making its lone pair less available for bonding with H⁺.

> Trap: Citing steric hindrance from F atoms. The primary reason is electronic (inductive withdrawal), not steric. AP expects an electronegativity/electron density explanation.

## Topic 8.7: pH and pKa

**Example 20 — HClO/NaClO buffer: ratio from pH–pKa difference (Progress Check Q22 type):**
$\text{HClO}/\text{ClO}^-$ buffer, $\text{pH} = 8.40$, $\text{p}K_a(\text{HClO}) = 7.40$. Compare $[\text{ClO}^-]$ and $[\text{HClO}]$.

$$\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\text{ClO}^-]}{[\text{HClO}]}$$
$$8.40 = 7.40 + \log\frac{[\text{ClO}^-]}{[\text{HClO}]}$$
$$\log\frac{[\text{ClO}^-]}{[\text{HClO}]} = 1.00$$
$$\frac{[\text{ClO}^-]}{[\text{HClO}]} = 10^1 = 10$$

**Answer: $[\text{ClO}^-] = 10 \times [\text{HClO}]$** — the conjugate base is 10 times more concentrated than the weak acid because pH is 1 unit above $\text{p}K_a$.

*Key move: Each unit of pH above $\text{p}K_a$ represents a 10-fold excess of conjugate base. Each unit below $\text{p}K_a$ represents a 10-fold excess of weak acid.*

---

**Example 21 — Identifying dominant species from pH vs. pKa (Progress Check Q23/Q24 type):**
Propanoic acid ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{COOH}$), $\text{p}K_a = 4.25$. At which measured pH does the conjugate base dominate?

(A) pH = 3.0; (B) pH = 4.25; (C) pH = 4.0; (D) pH = 5.0.

Rule: conjugate base $[\text{A}^-] > [\text{HA}]$ only when $\text{pH} > \text{p}K_a = 4.25$.

- (A) 3.0 < 4.25 → acid dominates ✗
- (B) 4.25 = 4.25 → equal concentrations ✗ (not $>$)
- (C) 4.0 < 4.25 → acid dominates ✗
- (D) 5.0 > 4.25 → conjugate base dominates ✓

**Answer: (D) pH = 5.0** — the only choice above $\text{p}K_a$.

*Key move: Draw the $\text{p}K_a$ as a dividing line. pH above → conjugate base; pH below → weak acid. Apply mechanically.*

## Topic 8.8–8.10: Buffers

**Example 22 — Acetic acid buffer: adding NaOH, finding pH (Progress Check Q27 type):**
$1.0\ \text{L}$ of $0.30\ M\ \text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$ ($\text{p}K_a = 4.76$) + $0.10\ \text{mol NaOH}$. Find pH.

Step 1: Stoichiometry.
$\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$ remaining $= 0.30 - 0.10 = 0.20\ \text{mol}$
$\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-$ formed $= 0.10\ \text{mol}$

Step 2: Henderson-Hasselbalch.
$$\text{pH} = 4.76 + \log\frac{0.10}{0.20} = 4.76 + \log(0.50) = 4.76 - 0.301 = 4.46$$

**Answer: pH ≈ 4.46**

> Trap: Using $\log(0.20/0.10)$ — inverting the ratio. H-H always has the conjugate base ($\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-$, formed by the NaOH reaction) in the numerator. The inverted ratio gives 5.06.

**Example 23 — Buffer composition error: less NH₄Cl added (Progress Check Q28 type):**
A student intends to make $1.00\ M\ \text{NH}_3 / 1.00\ M\ \text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$ buffer ($\text{p}K_a(\text{NH}_4^+) = 9.25$) but adds only $0.50\ M\ \text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$ by mistake.

(a) Is the actual pH higher or lower than intended?
(b) Does the buffer have greater capacity for added acid or added base?

(a) With $[\text{NH}_4^+] = 0.50\ M$ and $[\text{NH}_3] = 1.00\ M$:
$$\text{pH} = 9.25 + \log\frac{1.00}{0.50} = 9.25 + 0.301 = 9.55 > 9.25$$
The actual pH is **higher** than intended.

(b) The buffer has more $\text{NH}_3$ (base) relative to $\text{NH}_4^+$ (acid). More $\text{NH}_3$ → more capacity to neutralize added **acid** (H⁺ reacts with NH₃). Less $\text{NH}_4^+$ → less capacity to neutralize added **base**.

**Answers: (a) pH is higher; (b) greater capacity for added acid.**

> Trap: Choosing "greater capacity for added base." The conjugate acid ($\text{NH}_4^+$) neutralizes added base; the base ($\text{NH}_3$) neutralizes added acid. With less $\text{NH}_4^+$, the base-neutralizing capacity decreases.

**Example 24 — Buffer overwhelmed by excess strong base (Progress Check Q29 type):**
A small buffer made from $\text{HC}_3\text{H}_5\text{O}_2$ (propanoic acid) receives 10× more concentrated NaOH than planned. What happens to pH?

A buffer has finite capacity. The excess NaOH first consumes all the weak acid (propanoic acid) component. Once the weak acid is exhausted:
- No more buffer action — the system is no longer a buffer.
- Excess NaOH remains as free $\text{OH}^-$.
- pH rises sharply, far above the intended buffer range.

**Answer:** The pH is much higher than intended. The buffer is destroyed by the excess NaOH; the remaining free $\text{OH}^-$ raises pH dramatically.

> Trap: Assuming "the buffer just resists the change more." Buffers only work within their capacity. Once the neutralizing component is consumed, the buffer fails and pH responds like an unbuffered solution.

**Example 25 — Unrinsed pH probe — direction of systematic error (Progress Check Q30 type):**
A student measures pH of buffer samples in order. Sample 3 has low pH (acidic). The probe is not rinsed before measuring Sample 4. What systematic error occurs in Sample 4's reading?

Residual acidic solution from Sample 3 transfers to Sample 4. The transferred acid lowers the apparent $[\text{H}^+]$ in Sample 4's measurement environment → the measured pH of Sample 4 is **lower than its true value**.

**Answer:** Sample 4's measured pH is lower than its true pH due to contamination from Sample 3's acidic residue. The error is systematic: the direction is always to bias the reading toward the contaminating sample's pH.

*Key move: Contamination biases the reading toward the previous sample's pH. Acid residue → lower apparent pH for next sample; base residue → higher apparent pH.*

## Topic 8.11: pH and Solubility

**Example 26 — Cloudiness from common-ion base (Progress Check Q31 type):**
Saturated $\text{Mg(OH)}_2$ solution: $\text{Mg(OH)}_2(s) \rightleftharpoons \text{Mg}^{2+}(aq) + 2\text{OH}^-(aq)$.

Adding KOH makes the solution cloudier. Explain.

KOH dissolves to give additional $\text{OH}^-$ (a common ion — product of $\text{Mg(OH)}_2$ dissolution). Added $\text{OH}^-$ raises $Q$ above $K_{sp}$ → equilibrium shifts **left** → more $\text{Mg(OH)}_2$ precipitates → more solid → cloudier.

**Answer:** KOH provides $\text{OH}^-$ (common ion), suppressing dissolution and causing more $\text{Mg(OH)}_2$ to precipitate, increasing cloudiness.

> Trap: Thinking adding acid makes it cloudier. Adding acid ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$) removes $\text{OH}^-$ by neutralization, shifting equilibrium right → more dissolves → less cloudy.

---

**Example 27 — Acid effect on CaF₂ solubility (Progress Check Q32 type):**
$\text{CaF}_2(s) \rightleftharpoons \text{Ca}^{2+}(aq) + 2\text{F}^-(aq)$.

Explain why $\text{CaF}_2$ is more soluble at lower pH.

Step 1: Identify the relevant reaction.
$\text{F}^-$ is the conjugate base of the weak acid HF. In acidic solution:
$$\text{H}^+(aq) + \text{F}^-(aq) \to \text{HF}(aq)$$

Step 2: Apply Le Chatelier.
$\text{H}^+$ removes $\text{F}^-$ (a product of dissolution) from equilibrium. This shifts the dissolution equilibrium right → more $\text{CaF}_2$ dissolves.

**Answer:** Lower pH means more $\text{H}^+$ present. $\text{H}^+$ reacts with $\text{F}^-$ to form $\text{HF}$ (a weak acid), removing a product of dissolution and driving further dissolution. Solubility increases with decreasing pH.

*Key move: Identify whether the anion of the salt is a conjugate base of a weak acid. If yes, acid dissolves it more readily. If no (strong acid anion like $\text{Cl}^-$), pH has no effect.*

**Example 28 — Cu(OH)₂ absorbance and pH (Progress Check Q33 type):**
$\text{Cu(OH)}_2(s) \rightleftharpoons \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + 2\text{OH}^-(aq)$.

A graph shows absorbance increases as pH decreases. Explain.

Step 1: Lower pH means more $\text{H}^+$.
$\text{H}^+$ neutralizes $\text{OH}^-$: $\text{H}^+(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq) \to \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$.

Step 2: Removing $\text{OH}^-$ (a product) shifts dissolution equilibrium right → more $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ dissolves.

Step 3: Beer's Law.
$\text{Cu}^{2+}$ is the colored species. Higher $[\text{Cu}^{2+}]$ → higher absorbance ($A = \varepsilon lc$; $c$ increases).

**Answer:** Lower pH increases $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ dissolution by removing $\text{OH}^-$ through neutralization, which shifts equilibrium right. Higher $[\text{Cu}^{2+}]$ gives higher absorbance via Beer's Law.

> Trap: Thinking higher pH means more $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ (more basic = more dissolved). At higher pH, excess $\text{OH}^-$ acts as a common ion, suppressing dissolution and reducing $[\text{Cu}^{2+}]$ — the opposite.

**Example 29 — FRQ-style: complete buffer system analysis (AP FRQ quality):**
A student prepares a buffer from $0.200\ \text{mol}\ \text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$ and $0.200\ \text{mol}\ \text{CH}_3\text{COONa}$ in $500\ \text{mL}$ total volume. $\text{p}K_a = 4.76$.

(a) Calculate the initial pH.
(b) $0.010\ \text{mol}$ NaOH is added. Calculate the new pH.
(c) Why does the buffer resist a large pH change?

(a) $[\text{A}^-]/[\text{HA}] = 0.200/0.200 = 1.00$; $\log(1.00) = 0$.
$$\text{pH} = 4.76 + 0 = 4.76$$

(b) After adding NaOH: HA remaining $= 0.200 - 0.010 = 0.190\ \text{mol}$; $\text{A}^-$ formed $= 0.200 + 0.010 = 0.210\ \text{mol}$.
$$\text{pH} = 4.76 + \log\frac{0.210}{0.190} = 4.76 + \log(1.105) = 4.76 + 0.043 = 4.80$$

(c) The weak acid ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$) reacts with added $\text{OH}^-$, converting strong base to weak base. This prevents the free $\text{OH}^-$ from dramatically raising pH. Similarly, the conjugate base would absorb any added $\text{H}^+$. The buffer system converts strong acid/base into weak acid/base, which have a much smaller effect on pH.

**Answers: (a) 4.76; (b) 4.80; (c) buffer components absorb added strong acid/base by converting them to weak acid/base.**

*FRQ-quality answer includes: moles stated, stoichiometry shown, H-H formula written, ratio clearly calculated, mechanistic explanation (not "because it's a buffer").*

**Example 30 — "What can/cannot be concluded" about buffer pH (AP exam style):**
Two $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}/\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-$ buffers have the same ratio but different total concentrations. What can and cannot be concluded about their pH?

**Can conclude:** pH is identical for both (same ratio → same log term → same pH).

**Cannot conclude:** buffer capacity is the same. Higher total concentration = more moles of each component = greater capacity to absorb added acid or base before pH changes significantly.

*Key move: pH depends on ratio only. Buffer capacity depends on total concentration. These are independent properties — don't confuse them.*
