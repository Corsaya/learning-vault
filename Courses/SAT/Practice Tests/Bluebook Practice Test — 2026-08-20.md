## Patterns worth acting on

### 1. Word in context — 4 misses, the single biggest cluster

M1 Q3, M2 Q2, M2 Q3, M2 Q4. Every one is "most logical and precise word or phrase" or
"what does X most nearly mean." In each, the chosen word was **topically plausible but
logically wrong** — it fit the subject matter, not the sentence's argument.

- Q3: the text sets up a **paradox**, so the blank needs a negative ("hampered"). *Reflected*
  is neutral and describes the topic, not the contradiction.
- M2 Q4: "manifest" in the Beckett passage means **perceptible**, not *realized* — the
  everyday sense was chosen over the one the sentence's grammar supports.

**Fix (decision rule):** cover the choices. Predict the word yourself from the logic of the
sentence — especially any *although / but / paradox / yet* signal. Then match. Never read the
four choices first; that's what makes the topically-plausible one look right.

### 2. Standard English Conventions — the rule is known, the execution slipped

All 3 misses are the same underlying question: **is this a second independent clause?**

- M2 Q20 and Q21 are the *identical* trap — the sentence already has a main verb, so the blank
  needs a **participle** (*prioritizing*, *searching*), not a finite verb (*had prioritized*,
  *were searching*). A finite verb there makes a comma splice.
- M1 Q19 is the **matched-pair** rule — an em dash already opened the interrupter earlier in
  the sentence, so the blank must close with a dash, not a colon.

This is good news, not bad: it's the exact rule already at the top of
[[Quick Reference — Math Formulas and Grammar Rules]], applied under time pressure. Both rules
are now in [[English Rule Vault]].

### 3. Table-data completion — 2 for 2 wrong

M1 Q13 (sheep fiber diameters) and M2 Q12 (TRAPPIST-1 orbital periods). Both say "which choice
most effectively uses data from the table." Both were missed.

**Fix:** read the sentence *before* the blank to work out what claim the data has to support,
then go to the table with a specific number in mind. Answers that quote real numbers from the
table but support the wrong claim are the trap in both cases.

### 4. Advanced Math — confirmed, but one of the three is arithmetic

Q16 and Q21 are genuine rational-function and polynomial-form gaps. **Q15 is not a knowledge
gap** — the method was right through the entire chain and the final division used 9 instead of
81. Filed as careless, not conceptual. Don't re-teach rational exponents on the strength of it.

### 5. "At least one solution" (Math M2 Q19)

`2x + 9y = 7` paired with `3x + 13.5y = 10.5` is the **same line** (×1.5), which means
infinitely many solutions — which still counts as "at least one." Answer was I and II, not
II only. The slope-comparison logic was applied but the identical-line case was excluded.

### Calibration is good — that is the most useful thing here

**When you were certain, you were right 61 times out of 66 (92%).** Only 5 confident misses on
the whole test. Self-knowledge is not the problem, which means the study list can be built from
a small, specific set instead of re-covering domains.

**But the "leaning" bucket is upside down.** On the 14 questions rated 3 — narrowed to two,
picking the more likely one — you went **4 for 14 (29%)**. A genuine coin flip between two
survivors would be 50%. The sample is small enough that this could be noise, but the direction
is worth an execution rule: **a 3 means your lean is not evidence.** Go back to the text and
find one concrete line that decides it, or flag it and come back. Don't go with the feeling.
