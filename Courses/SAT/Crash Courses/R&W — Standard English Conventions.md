# R&W — Standard English Conventions Crash Course

**This is the highest-priority crash course of the 8.** Standard English Conventions is
~26% of the R&W section (11–15 questions) per
[[../Research/Official SAT Structure and Content Research (2026-08-07)]], and it's your
**confirmed weakest content area** — both the Dec/Mar official reports and the 2026-08-07
diagnostic agree on this. Covers C1–C11 from
[[../Foundations Knowledge Check#Reading & Writing — Standard English Conventions (~26%, 11–15 Q)|the Foundations Knowledge Check]].
Every rule below gets a precise statement, correct/incorrect examples, and the specific
trap the SAT loves to build into this rule — read all of it, not just the ones that feel
unfamiliar, since several of these have sneaky exceptions even when the base rule feels
obvious.

### C1 — Subject-verb agreement, incl. interrupting phrases

Already fully covered — don't re-derive it, just use the existing lesson:
[[../Diagnostic Gap Lessons (2026-08-07)#Lesson 3 — Standard English Conventions: subject-verb agreement with interrupting phrases]]

Quick summary: the verb agrees with the **true subject**, not the noun sitting closest to
it. Phrases like *along with, as well as, together with, in addition to, accompanied by*
sit between subject and verb without changing the subject's number — mentally delete the
whole phrase and check what's left. "And" is the only thing that genuinely makes a
compound subject plural.

**Worked example**

The senator, along with several key advisors, ______ expected to arrive before noon.
A) are
B) is
C) were
D) have been

**Reasoning:** The true subject is "senator" (singular). "Along with several key advisors"
is an interrupting phrase — delete it and you're left with "The senator ______ expected,"
which needs a singular verb.

- **A) are** — plural; wrongly treats "along with several key advisors" as adding to the subject.
- **C) were** — plural and past tense; same subject-count error, wrong tense too.
- **D) have been** — plural auxiliary; same subject-count error.

**Trips people up:** "along with" phrases feel like "and," pulling your ear toward a plural
verb — but only "and" actually creates a compound subject.

**Worked example 2**

The results of the survey — despite what the initial headline suggested — ______
consistent with prior research.
A) is
B) was
C) are
D) has been

**Reasoning:** The true subject is "results" (plural), and the dash-set-off clause is an
interrupting phrase, not part of the subject — delete it and you get "The results ______
consistent," which needs a plural verb.

- **A) is** — singular; likely picked because "headline suggested" sits right before the blank.
- **B) was** — singular and past tense; same subject-count error.
- **D) has been** — singular auxiliary; same subject-count error.

**Trips people up:** interrupters can be set off with dashes, not just commas — don't let a
singular noun/verb inside the interruption pull your ear toward a singular main verb.

### C2 — Comma splice / run-on identification and fix

**What this actually tests:** whether two independent clauses (each one a complete
sentence on its own) have been joined with strong-enough punctuation.

**The rule:** Two independent clauses (IC + IC) can be joined only by:
1. A period — `IC. IC.`
2. A semicolon — `IC; IC.`
3. A comma + a FANBOYS conjunction (**F**or, **A**nd, **N**or, **B**ut, **O**r, **Y**et, **S**o) — `IC, and IC.`

A comma *alone* between two ICs is a **comma splice**. No punctuation at all between two
ICs is a **run-on / fused sentence**. Both are errors.

- **Correct (comma + FANBOYS):** "I finished my homework, so I went outside to play."
- **Correct (semicolon):** "I finished my homework; I went outside to play."
- **Incorrect (comma splice):** "I finished my homework, I went outside to play."
- **Incorrect (run-on):** "I finished my homework I went outside to play."

**Trips people up:** a comma splice *reads* fine out loud — your ear doesn't flag it the
way a run-on does — so you can't proofread this by ear alone; check whether both halves
could stand as their own sentence, and if so, confirm the punctuation between them is
strong enough.

**Worked example 2**

The concert sold out within minutes, fans had lined up outside since dawn.
Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?
A) NO CHANGE
B) minutes; fans had lined up outside since dawn.
C) minutes fans had lined up outside since dawn.
D) minutes, and, fans had lined up outside since dawn.

**Reasoning:** Both halves are independent clauses ("The concert sold out within minutes" /
"fans had lined up outside since dawn"), so they need a semicolon, a period, or comma +
FANBOYS — a semicolon is the cleanest fix here.

- **A) NO CHANGE** — a comma alone between two ICs is a comma splice.
- **C)** — no punctuation at all between two ICs is a run-on/fused sentence.
- **D)** — the stray comma after "and" breaks the comma+FANBOYS pattern; it's not valid punctuation.

**Trips people up:** a semicolon fix only works when both sides are genuinely independent
clauses — always test each half on its own before reaching for the semicolon.

**Worked example 3**

The library closes at nine tonight the study rooms will reopen tomorrow morning.
Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?
A) NO CHANGE
B) tonight, the study rooms will reopen tomorrow morning.
C) tonight; the study rooms will reopen tomorrow morning.
D) tonight the study rooms, will reopen tomorrow morning.

**Reasoning:** "The library closes at nine tonight" and "the study rooms will reopen
tomorrow morning" are both complete sentences with no punctuation between them — a
run-on — so a semicolon is needed to join them correctly.

- **A) NO CHANGE** — no punctuation between two ICs is a run-on/fused sentence.
- **B)** — a comma alone between two ICs is a comma splice; still an error.
- **D)** — the comma is misplaced mid-clause and does nothing to fix the run-on.

**Trips people up:** short, closely related clauses sitting side by side don't sound jarring
when read quickly — the run-on can hide in plain sight unless you deliberately check for a
second subject-verb pair.

### C3 — Independent vs. dependent clause: the 4 IC/DC punctuation rules

**What this actually tests:** whether the punctuation between clauses matches whether each
clause could stand alone as a full sentence.

An **independent clause (IC)** has a subject and verb and is a complete thought on its own.
A **dependent clause (DC)** has a subject and verb too, but starts with a word that makes it
incomplete on its own (*because, although, when, since, if, while,* etc.) — it needs an IC
attached to make sense.

**The 4 rules:**
1. `IC. IC.` — two complete sentences, period between them.
2. `IC; IC.` — semicolon between two closely related complete sentences.
3. `IC, FANBOYS IC.` — comma + coordinating conjunction.
4. `DC, IC.` when the dependent clause comes **first** (needs a comma) — but `IC DC.` when
   the dependent clause comes **second** (usually **no** comma).

- **Correct:** "Although the storm ended, we stayed inside." (DC first → comma)
- **Correct:** "We stayed inside although the storm continued." (DC second → no comma)
- **Incorrect:** "Although the storm ended we stayed inside." (missing comma after leading DC)
- **Incorrect:** "We stayed inside, because the storm continued." (unnecessary comma before a trailing DC)

**Trips people up:** the comma rule flips depending on clause order — DC-first always
wants a comma, but DC-second (especially after *because*) almost never does. The SAT
loves inserting an unneeded comma right before a trailing "because" clause.

**Worked example 2**

Since the museum was undergoing renovations the west wing remained closed to visitors.
Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?
A) NO CHANGE
B) renovations, the west wing remained closed to visitors.
C) renovations the west wing, remained closed to visitors.
D) renovations; the west wing remained closed to visitors.

**Reasoning:** "Since the museum was undergoing renovations" is a dependent clause coming
first, so it needs a comma before the independent clause that follows.

- **A) NO CHANGE** — missing the required comma after a leading DC.
- **C)** — the comma is misplaced inside the independent clause instead of after the DC.
- **D)** — a semicolon requires an independent clause on both sides; the first part is a DC, not an IC.

**Trips people up:** don't just check *whether* there's a comma — check that it's in the
right spot, right at the DC/IC boundary, not wedged somewhere else in the sentence.

**Worked example 3**

The flight was delayed, since a mechanical issue was discovered during inspection.
Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?
A) NO CHANGE
B) delayed since a mechanical issue was discovered during inspection.
C) delayed; since a mechanical issue was discovered during inspection.
D) delayed. Since a mechanical issue was discovered during inspection.

**Reasoning:** Here the dependent clause ("since a mechanical issue was discovered during
inspection") comes second, after the independent clause — trailing DCs usually take no comma.

- **A) NO CHANGE** — unnecessary comma before a trailing DC.
- **C)** — a semicolon needs an IC on both sides, but "since a mechanical issue..." is a DC, not an IC.
- **D)** — a period creates a sentence fragment out of the DC, which can't stand alone.

**Trips people up:** this is the flip side of the leading-DC rule — a trailing DC (especially
after *since*, *because*, *although*) almost never wants a comma, even though your instinct
after a full clause is to pause and add one.

### C4 — Apostrophes: possessive vs. plural, its/it's, whose/who's

**What this actually tests:** whether an apostrophe signals possession/contraction, versus
being wrongly added to a simple plural.

**The rules:**
- Possessive singular: add `'s` — "the dog's bone" (one dog owns the bone).
- Possessive plural (word already ends in s): add just `'` — "the dogs' bones" (multiple dogs own bones).
- Plain plural: **no apostrophe** — "I saw three dogs."
- `its` = possessive ("belonging to it") — never has an apostrophe.
- `it's` = contraction of "it is" or "it has" — always has an apostrophe.
- `whose` = possessive ("belonging to whom").
- `who's` = contraction of "who is" or "who has."

- **Correct:** "The company announced its earnings." / "It's raining."
- **Incorrect:** "The company announced it's earnings." (should be *its*)
- **Correct:** "Whose book is this?" / "Who's coming to the party?"
- **Incorrect:** "Who's book is this?" (should be *whose*)

**Trips people up:** `its`/`it's` and `whose`/`who's` are the two most-tested apostrophe
pairs precisely because the "add apostrophe = possessive" instinct is backwards for these
words — for pronouns, the apostrophe version is always the contraction, never the possessive.

**Worked example 2**

The two companies announced that ______ merger would close by the end of the quarter.
A) their's
B) theirs
C) there
D) their

**Reasoning:** "Their" is the possessive form needed to modify "merger" — possessive
pronouns (my, your, his, her, its, our, their) never take an apostrophe.

- **A) their's** — not a real word; possessive pronouns never take an apostrophe.
- **B) theirs** — this is the standalone/absolute possessive ("the merger is theirs"), not the adjective form needed before a noun like "merger."
- **C) there** — wrong homophone entirely (location word, not possessive).

**Trips people up:** unlike noun possessives ("the dog's bone"), possessive pronouns never
get an apostrophe — "their's," "her's," and "your's" are never correct spellings.

**Worked example 3**

All three ______ luggage was searched at the checkpoint.
A) passenger's
B) passengers'
C) passengers
D) passenger's's

**Reasoning:** "Passengers" (plural, already ending in s) needs just an apostrophe to show
plural possession: "passengers'."

- **A) passenger's** — singular possessive; wrong since there are three passengers, not one.
- **C) passengers** — plain plural, no possession shown at all, but the sentence needs to show whose luggage it is.
- **D) passenger's's** — not a real construction; never double up an apostrophe-s.

**Trips people up:** for a plural noun that already ends in *s*, resist the urge to add `'s`
— just add the apostrophe after the existing s.

### C5 — Pronoun-antecedent agreement, incl. singular "they"

**What this actually tests:** whether a pronoun matches its antecedent (the noun it refers
back to) in number, and — for the digital SAT specifically — whether you know singular
"they" is accepted, not treated as an error.

**The rule:** A pronoun must match its antecedent's number. Plural antecedent → plural
pronoun (*they/their*). Singular antecedent → singular pronoun. **Important exception the
digital SAT follows:** for a singular but gender-unspecified/generic antecedent (*everyone,
someone, a person, each student, each employee*), **"their/they" is accepted as correct** —
you do not need "his or her." The one thing that still matters is **consistency**: don't
refer to the same antecedent with different pronouns mid-sentence, and don't use "they/their"
for a specific, known, non-person antecedent (a company, a book, a committee acting as one
unit still typically takes "it/its").

- **Correct (singular they, accepted):** "Each employee is required to submit their timesheet by Friday."
- **Correct (true plural):** "The scientists published their findings."
- **Incorrect (inconsistent):** "A person should always trust their own instincts, but they must also listen to it." (switches from *they* to *it* for the same antecedent)
- **Incorrect (wrong pronoun for a non-person singular):** "The committee submitted their final report." (a committee acting as one body takes *its*)

**Trips people up:** don't reflexively mark "their" as wrong just because the antecedent is
grammatically singular — check whether the antecedent is a generic/indefinite person (their
is fine) versus a specific singular thing/group-as-a-unit (needs *it/its*).

**Worked example 2**

If a customer has a complaint, ______ should contact support within 30 days.
A) it
B) they
C) we
D) I

**Reasoning:** "A customer" is a singular, generic, gender-unspecified antecedent — singular
"they" is accepted on the digital SAT.

- **A) it** — "customer" refers to a person, not a thing; "it" is wrong for a person.
- **C) we** — shifts to first-person plural, which doesn't match "a customer" at all.
- **D) I** — shifts to first-person singular, doesn't match the antecedent either.

**Trips people up:** don't assume the SAT wants "he or she" here — for a generic,
gender-unspecified singular antecedent like "a customer," singular "they" is the
digital-SAT-correct choice, not an error to fix.

**Worked example 3**

The nonprofit organization updated ______ mission statement to reflect its new community
programs.
A) their
B) its
C) it's
D) they're

**Reasoning:** "The nonprofit organization" is a specific singular entity acting as one
unit, so it takes "its" — not "their," which is reserved for generic/indefinite person
antecedents.

- **A) their** — over-applies the singular-they exception to a specific organization, not a generic person.
- **C) it's** — contraction of "it is," wrong part of speech (needs a possessive, not a contraction).
- **D) they're** — contraction of "they are," wrong pronoun and wrong part of speech.

**Trips people up:** the singular-they exception is specifically for generic/indefinite
*person* antecedents (everyone, a customer, each employee) — a specific company,
organization, or object still takes "it/its," even though it's singular.

### C6 — Pronoun case (who/whom, I/me, subjective/objective)

**What this actually tests:** whether a pronoun is doing the action (subjective case) or
receiving it (objective case).

**The rule:**
- **Subjective** (subject of a verb): I, he, she, we, they, **who**.
- **Objective** (object of a verb or preposition): me, him, her, us, them, **whom**.
- `who` = subject ("Who called you?" — *who* does the calling).
- `whom` = object ("To whom did you give the letter?" — *whom* is the object of "to").

- **Correct:** "She and I went to the store." (subject of *went*)
- **Incorrect:** "Her and me went to the store."
- **Correct:** "The gift is between you and me." (object of preposition *between*)
- **Incorrect:** "The gift is between you and I."

**Trips people up:** in a compound ("Sam and I" / "my colleague and me"), isolate just the
pronoun and drop the other person — "I went to the store" sounds right, "Me went to the
store" sounds wrong — your ear catches it once the compound is stripped away, even when the
full compound sentence tricks it.

**Worked example 2**

______ do you think will win the scholarship this year?
A) Who
B) Whom
C) Whoever
D) Whomever

**Reasoning:** The pronoun is the subject of "will win" ("[He] will win the scholarship"),
so the subjective case "Who" is correct.

- **B) Whom** — objective case; wrong because the pronoun performs the action, it doesn't receive it.
- **C) Whoever** — subjective case, but the wrong form for a direct question like this one.
- **D) Whomever** — objective case; same error as B), compounded with the wrong form.

**Trips people up:** to check, answer the embedded question with a plain pronoun — "He will
win" (not "Him will win") confirms subjective case, so "who," not "whom," is correct.

**Worked example 3**

After the ceremony, the award was given to my brother and ______.
A) I
B) myself
C) me
D) mine

**Reasoning:** The pronoun is the object of the preposition "to" ("to my brother and me"),
so objective case "me" is correct.

- **A) I** — subjective case; wrong for an object position.
- **B) myself** — reflexive pronouns need an earlier "I" in the same clause to reflect back to, which isn't present here.
- **D) mine** — possessive; changes the meaning entirely (would mean the award itself belongs to the speaker).

**Trips people up:** "myself" gets overused as a "fancier-sounding" substitute for "me" —
but reflexives only work when the same person already appears earlier in the clause as the
subject.

### C7 — Verb tense consistency across a sentence/passage

**What this actually tests:** whether verb tenses shift without a logical, signaled reason.

**The rule:** Stay in one tense unless the timeline of events genuinely requires a shift —
and if it does, the sequence has to make logical sense (e.g., past perfect for something
completed *before* another past event).

- **Correct:** "She walked to the store and bought milk." (past, past — consistent)
- **Incorrect:** "She walked to the store and buys milk." (past then present — unjustified shift)
- **Correct (justified shift, past perfect before simple past):** "By the time the guests arrived, Maria had prepared the entire meal." (*had prepared* happened before *arrived*)

**Trips people up:** not every tense shift is an error — if the sentence has a real reason
for two different times (an earlier-completed action needs past perfect, or context words
like "yesterday"/"tomorrow" signal a real time change), the shift is correct. Only flag a
shift that has no logical basis.

**Worked example 2**

Every morning, the baker ______ fresh bread before the shop opens.
A) baked
B) bakes
C) will bake
D) was baking

**Reasoning:** The sentence describes a habitual action, and "opens" is present tense, so
the verb needs to match in present tense: "bakes."

- **A) baked** — past tense; doesn't match the present-tense "opens."
- **C) will bake** — future tense; doesn't fit a routine, ongoing habit.
- **D) was baking** — past progressive; wrong timeframe for a present-tense habitual action.

**Trips people up:** habitual/routine actions ("every morning," "every year") default to
simple present tense — don't let the past tense of a nearby, unrelated verb pull you into
the wrong tense.

**Worked example 3**

By next summer, the construction crew ______ the new bridge, and traffic will finally flow
smoothly again.
A) completes
B) completed
C) will have completed
D) is completing

**Reasoning:** "By next summer" signals an action that will be finished *before* another
future point — that calls for future perfect: "will have completed."

- **A) completes** — simple present; doesn't signal completion relative to a future point.
- **B) completed** — simple past; wrong timeframe, the action hasn't happened yet.
- **D) is completing** — present progressive; suggests an action still in progress, not one completed by a future deadline.

**Trips people up:** "by [future date]" is the signal phrase for future perfect — the same
logic as past perfect ("had ___") for a pre-past action, just shifted one timeframe later.

### C8 — Modifier placement (dangling/misplaced modifiers)

**What this actually tests:** whether an introductory modifying phrase is immediately
followed by the noun it's actually describing.

**The rule:** A modifying phrase at the start of a sentence (usually ending in a comma)
must be followed directly by the noun performing the action described in that phrase. If
the noun right after the comma couldn't logically do what the phrase describes, the
modifier is "dangling."

- **Correct:** "Exhausted after the long flight, Jenna found the hotel bed incredibly comfortable." (*Jenna* is the one who's exhausted)
- **Incorrect (dangling):** "Exhausted after the long flight, the hotel bed felt incredibly comfortable to Jenna." (a bed can't be exhausted)

**Trips people up:** check literally who/what sits right after the introductory comma — if
that noun can't logically perform the action in the modifying phrase, it's wrong no matter
how natural the sentence sounds when read quickly.

**Worked example 2**

Hoping to catch the sunrise, ______.
A) the campers set an early alarm
B) an early alarm was set by the campers
C) the mountain came into view for the campers
D) it was hoped that campers would set an early alarm

**Reasoning:** "Campers" are the ones hoping to catch the sunrise, so "the campers" must sit
immediately after the introductory comma.

- **B)** — "an early alarm" is the noun right after the comma, and an alarm can't hope anything — dangling.
- **C)** — "the mountain" is the noun right after the comma, and a mountain can't hope anything — dangling.
- **D)** — the vague "it" has no logical connection to "hoping" at all.

**Trips people up:** passive-voice rewrites (B) often *sound* fine because the real doer is
mentioned later in the sentence — but the modifier check only cares about the noun
immediately after the comma, not whether the doer shows up eventually.

**Worked example 3**

Rushing to catch the last train, ______.
A) the platform was crowded with commuters
B) Daniel's briefcase was left behind
C) Daniel nearly tripped over his own suitcase
D) it seemed like everyone was late

**Reasoning:** Daniel is the one rushing, so "Daniel" needs to be the noun immediately
following the introductory comma.

- **A)** — "the platform" can't rush; dangling.
- **B)** — the noun right after the comma is "Daniel's briefcase," a thing, not Daniel himself — still dangling even though "Daniel" appears in the sentence.
- **D)** — the vague "it" has no logical link to "rushing."

**Trips people up:** having the right person's *name* appear somewhere in the sentence
isn't enough (see B) — that person specifically has to be the noun sitting right after the
introductory comma, not buried inside a possessive.

### C9 — Parallel structure in a list or comparison

**What this actually tests:** whether every item in a list (joined by *and/or*) or a
comparison (joined by *than/as*) shares the same grammatical form.

**The rule:** All items in a list or comparison must match grammatically — all gerunds
(*-ing*), all infinitives (*to + verb*), all nouns, etc. Mixing forms breaks parallelism.

- **Correct:** "She likes hiking, swimming, and biking." (all gerunds)
- **Incorrect:** "She likes hiking, swimming, and to bike." (gerund, gerund, infinitive — mismatched)
- **Correct:** "It is better to give than to receive." (both infinitives)
- **Incorrect:** "It is better to give than receiving." (infinitive vs. gerund)

**Trips people up:** the SAT usually plants the mismatch in the **last** item of the list —
check the final item's grammatical form against the first two before assuming the sentence
is fine.

**Worked example 2**

The coach explained that winning requires discipline, patience, and ______.
A) staying focused
B) to stay focused
C) focus
D) being focused on things

**Reasoning:** The first two list items ("discipline," "patience") are plain nouns, so the
third item needs to match: "focus" is also a plain noun.

- **A) staying focused** — a gerund phrase, mismatched with the noun-form list.
- **B) to stay focused** — an infinitive, mismatched with the noun-form list.
- **D) being focused on things** — a wordy gerund phrase, both mismatched and awkward.

**Trips people up:** parallel-structure errors aren't always *-ing* vs. *to* — a list of
plain nouns can just as easily get broken by a gerund or infinitive phrase tacked onto the
end.

**Worked example 3**

Learning to code is often less about memorizing syntax than ______ how to solve problems.
A) understanding
B) to understand
C) you understand
D) the understanding of

**Reasoning:** This is a comparison ("less about X than Y"), and X is the gerund phrase
"memorizing syntax" — so Y needs to match with a gerund: "understanding."

- **B) to understand** — an infinitive, breaks parallel with the gerund "memorizing."
- **C) you understand** — a full clause, doesn't match the gerund-phrase structure at all.
- **D) the understanding of** — a noun phrase, non-parallel and awkward.

**Trips people up:** comparisons using *than* or *as* need parallel structure just like
lists do — check the grammatical form on both sides of the comparison word, not just items
in an *and/or* list.

### C10 — Non-essential vs. essential clause commas (which/that, appositives)

**What this actually tests:** whether a describing clause is *essential* (needed to
identify which noun is meant) or *non-essential* (extra info that could be deleted), since
that determines both punctuation and word choice.

**The rule:**
- **Essential/restrictive** (identifies *which one* — can't be removed without losing
  meaning): **no commas**, use **that** for things, **who** for people.
- **Non-essential/nonrestrictive** (adds extra info about something already identified):
  **commas required**, use **which** for things, **who** for people. This also covers
  appositives (a renaming phrase) — non-essential ones get commas.

- **Correct (essential):** "Employees who complete the training by Friday will receive a certificate." (no commas — specifies *which* employees)
- **Correct (non-essential):** "My uncle Robert, who moved to Denver last year, visits us every summer." (commas — there's only one uncle Robert; the clause is extra info)
- **Incorrect:** "Employees, who complete the training by Friday, will receive a certificate." (commas wrongly suggest *all* employees complete it — changes the meaning)
- **Incorrect:** "The book which I borrowed is on the table." (no commas + *which* — should be *that*, no commas, since it's essential)

**Trips people up:** *which* is only correct **with** commas, and *that* never takes commas
— if you see "which" with no commas, or "that" with commas, that's almost always the error.
Test it by asking: can I delete this clause without losing track of which noun we mean? If
yes → non-essential (commas, *which/who*). If no → essential (no commas, *that/who*).

**Worked example 2**

The novel ______ won the award last year has just been adapted into a film.
A) which
B) that
C) , which,
D) , that,

**Reasoning:** The clause "won the award last year" is essential — it identifies *which*
novel is meant (out of possibly many) — so it needs no commas and takes "that."

- **A) which** — "which" used without commas is wrong; "which" only appears with commas.
- **C) , which,** — setting the clause off with commas would make it sound non-essential, but the sentence needs it to specify which novel, so this changes the meaning.
- **D) , that,** — "that" never takes commas, essential or not.

**Trips people up:** even when "which" would otherwise fit semantically, the punctuation
rule is absolute — "which" without commas is always wrong on the SAT.

**Worked example 3**

My favorite teacher ______ Mrs. Alvarez taught chemistry for over twenty years.
A) NO CHANGE
B) teacher, Mrs. Alvarez, taught
C) teacher Mrs. Alvarez, taught
D) teacher, Mrs. Alvarez taught

**Reasoning:** "Mrs. Alvarez" is an appositive renaming "my favorite teacher" — there's only
one favorite teacher, so the name is extra, non-essential information and needs commas on
**both** sides.

- **A) NO CHANGE** — no commas at all around the appositive, missing punctuation.
- **C)** — only the second comma is present; unbalanced, still an error.
- **D)** — only the first comma is present; unbalanced, still an error.

**Trips people up:** non-essential appositives and clauses need commas on **both** sides —
a single comma on only one side is a common half-fix that's still wrong.

### C11 — Colon and semicolon usage (beyond IC/DC — lists, explanations)

**What this actually tests:** whether a colon is set up correctly (complete sentence before
it) and whether a semicolon is used to separate list items that already contain commas.

**The rule:**
- **Colon:** what comes *before* it must be a complete independent clause. What follows can
  be a list, an explanation, or an elaboration — but never put a colon directly after
  "such as," "including," or a verb like "was" with no object yet.
- **Semicolon (beyond joining two ICs):** also used to separate items in a list when the
  items themselves already contain commas, to avoid confusion.

- **Correct (colon):** "The chef's philosophy was simple: use fresh ingredients and never rush the process." ("The chef's philosophy was simple" is a complete IC on its own)
- **Incorrect (colon):** "She packed: a passport, a map, and sunscreen." ("She packed" is not complete without an object — not a full IC)
- **Correct (semicolon in a complex list):** "I've lived in Portland, Oregon; Austin, Texas; and Reno, Nevada." (semicolons separate city/state pairs that already have internal commas)

**Trips people up:** always check that the words immediately before a colon could stand
alone as a full sentence — if they can't, the colon is wrong no matter how natural the list
after it looks.

**Worked example 2**

The team's strategy relied on three things ______ speed, precision, and communication.
A) :
B) ;
C) , such as,
D) (no punctuation)

**Reasoning:** "The team's strategy relied on three things" is a complete independent
clause, so a colon can correctly introduce the list that follows.

- **B) ;** — a semicolon needs an independent clause on both sides; what follows the blank is just a list, not an IC.
- **C) , such as,** — ungrammatical; "such as" doesn't pair with this kind of setup, and a colon-style introduction shouldn't also have "such as."
- **D) (no punctuation)** — reads as run-together and unclear without any separator.

**Trips people up:** phrases like "three things," "the following," or "as follows" right
before the blank are a strong signal a colon is coming — but you still need to confirm the
words before it form a complete sentence.

**Worked example 3**

The award finalists were Maria Chen, a novelist; Tomás Reyes, a poet; and Priya Nair, a
playwright.
Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?
A) NO CHANGE
B) Maria Chen, a novelist, Tomás Reyes, a poet, and Priya Nair, a playwright.
C) Maria Chen a novelist; Tomás Reyes a poet; and Priya Nair a playwright.
D) Maria Chen, a novelist: Tomás Reyes, a poet: and Priya Nair, a playwright.

**Reasoning:** Each list item already contains an internal comma (name + appositive
describing them), so semicolons are needed between items to keep the groupings clear.

- **B)** — using only commas makes it unclear how many finalists there are or where one item ends and the next begins.
- **C)** — drops the commas that set off each appositive ("a novelist," "a poet," "a playwright"), losing needed punctuation.
- **D)** — colons are the wrong punctuation for separating list items; colons introduce, they don't separate parallel items.

**Trips people up:** when list items already contain their own internal commas, semicolons
— not commas — are what keep the groupings unambiguous.

## Mini-Diagnostic

One question per skill, C1–C11, real digital-SAT Conventions format. Answer key with
reasoning is below — don't peek until you've answered all 11.

**C1.** The museum's collection of rare manuscripts, alongside its extensive photography archive, ______ far larger than most visitors realize.
A) are
B) is
C) were
D) have been

**C2.** The bridge was closed for repairs, drivers had to take a long detour.
Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?
A) NO CHANGE
B) repairs; drivers had to take a long detour.
C) repairs drivers had to take a long detour.
D) repairs, drivers, had to take a long detour.

**C3.** Because the rain continued through the night the outdoor concert was cancelled.
Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?
A) NO CHANGE
B) night, the outdoor concert
C) night the, outdoor concert
D) night; the outdoor concert

**C4.** The company reported that ______ profits had doubled since last year.
A) it's
B) its
C) its'
D) it is

**C5.** Each employee is required to submit ______ timesheet by Friday.
A) their
B) them's
C) it's
D) there

**C6.** The award was presented to my colleague and ______ after months of hard work.
A) I
B) me
C) myself
D) mine

**C7.** By the time the guests arrived, Maria ______ the entire meal.
A) prepares
B) has prepared
C) had prepared
D) will prepare

**C8.** Exhausted after the long flight, the hotel bed felt incredibly comfortable to Jenna.
Which choice best fixes the underlined portion?
A) NO CHANGE
B) Exhausted after the long flight, Jenna found the hotel bed incredibly comfortable.
C) The hotel bed, exhausted after the long flight, felt incredibly comfortable to Jenna.
D) Jenna's hotel bed felt incredibly comfortable, exhausted after the long flight.

**C9.** The workshop taught participants how to write clear emails, how to organize meetings efficiently, and ______.
A) how to give effective presentations
B) giving effective presentations
C) effective presentations were given
D) to give effective presentations well

**C10.** Employees ______ complete the training by Friday will receive a certificate.
A) who
B) whom
C) , who,
D) , which,

**C11.** The chef's philosophy was simple ______ use fresh ingredients and never rush the process.
A) :
B) ;
C) ,
D) (no punctuation, just a space)

### Answer key

- **C1 — B) is.** True subject is "collection" (singular); "alongside its extensive photography archive" is a non-essential interrupting phrase, not part of the subject.
- **C2 — B) repairs; drivers had to take a long detour.** The two clauses are both independent, so they need a semicolon (or period, or comma+FANBOYS) — a plain comma (A) is a splice, no punctuation (C) is a run-on, and an extra comma (D) doesn't fix the splice.
- **C3 — B) night, the outdoor concert.** A dependent clause ("Because the rain continued through the night") coming first needs a comma before the independent clause that follows.
- **C4 — B) its.** Possessive form of "it" — never takes an apostrophe.
- **C5 — A) their.** "Each employee" is a singular, generic antecedent — singular "their" is accepted; the other options are either not real words or the wrong part of speech.
- **C6 — B) me.** Object of the preposition "to" ("to my colleague and me") — objective case, not subjective ("I") or reflexive ("myself").
- **C7 — C) had prepared.** Past perfect is needed for an action (preparing the meal) completed before another past action (the guests arriving).
- **C8 — B).** Fixes the dangling modifier by putting "Jenna" — the person who is exhausted — immediately after the introductory phrase.
- **C9 — A) how to give effective presentations.** Matches the parallel "how to ___" structure of the first two list items.
- **C10 — A) who.** The clause is essential (it identifies *which* employees get the certificate — not all of them), so no commas and "who" (not "whom," which would need to be an object, not the subject of "complete").
- **C11 — A) :.** "The chef's philosophy was simple" is a complete independent clause, so a colon can introduce the explanation that follows.
