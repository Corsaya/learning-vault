# R&W — Expression of Ideas Crash Course

Expression of Ideas is ~20% of the Reading & Writing section (8–12 questions
per [[../Research/Official SAT Structure and Content Research (2026-08-07)]]).
Unlike Standard English Conventions, none of this is a hard grammar rule with
a single right/wrong answer mechanically derived — these are judgment calls
about clarity, logic, and structure. The good news: each one still has a
repeatable process, it's just "evaluate against a goal" instead of "apply a
rule." Covers E1–E4 from [[../Foundations Knowledge Check]].

---

### E1 — Transition word selection (contrast, cause/effect, continuation, etc.)

**What this actually tests:** whether you can identify the logical
relationship between two ideas — usually two sentences, sometimes two
clauses — and pick the transition word/phrase that correctly signals that
relationship. On the digital SAT this shows up as a sentence or two of
context followed by a blank, with four transition words as answer choices.

**What the question looks like:** a short passage (2–4 sentences), one blank
(sometimes with an underlined transition to replace), four choices like
"However," "Therefore," "In addition," "For example." No "compute" step —
it's entirely about reading the logic between the two ideas.

**Strategy:**
1. Cover the blank. Read the sentence *before* it and the sentence *after*
   it as two separate claims.
2. Name the relationship in your own words first, before looking at the
   choices: are they saying the same kind of thing (continuation), opposite
   things (contrast), is the second a result of the first (cause/effect), is
   the second an example of the first (illustration), or is the second
   correcting/qualifying the first (concession)?
3. Match that relationship to a category, then pick the choice from that
   category. Categories to know cold:
   - **Continuation/addition:** furthermore, moreover, in addition, similarly
   - **Contrast:** however, nevertheless, on the other hand, in contrast, yet
   - **Cause/effect:** therefore, thus, consequently, as a result
   - **Concession:** although, granted, admittedly, even so
   - **Illustration:** for example, for instance, specifically
4. If two choices seem to fit the same category, look for a finer
   distinction (e.g., "however" vs. "although" — "however" starts a new
   sentence, "although" needs a dependent clause attached).

**Worked example:**
"The lab's initial results seemed to confirm the hypothesis. ____, a later
review of the data revealed a calibration error that had skewed every
reading."

Cover the blank: sentence 1 says results *seemed* to confirm the hypothesis.
Sentence 2 says a later review found an error that skewed everything — this
undercuts sentence 1, it doesn't continue or explain it. That's contrast.
"Therefore" (cause/effect) and "For example" (illustration) are out
immediately. "Furthermore" (continuation) is out — sentence 2 doesn't add
onto sentence 1, it reverses it. "However" fits: it signals the reversal
cleanly and works as a sentence-starter. Answer: **However**.

**Trips people up:** picking a word that "sounds right" without first
stating the logical relationship in your own words — the answer choices are
often four grammatically fine words, so the only way to filter them is
logic, not ear.

**Worked example 2:**
"The city council debated the new zoning proposal for nearly six hours.
____, they adjourned without reaching a final vote."

A) Similarly
B) Ultimately
C) For instance
D) In contrast

Cover the blank: sentence 1 says the council debated for six hours.
Sentence 2 says they adjourned without voting — this is the outcome/result
of the long debate, not a new example, a similarity, or an opposite idea.
"For instance" (illustration) is out — sentence 2 isn't an example of
sentence 1, it's what happened next. "Similarly" (continuation) is out —
nothing is being compared as alike. "In contrast" is out — there's no
opposing idea, just a consequence. "Ultimately" signals the end result of
a process, which is exactly the relationship here. Answer: **B**.

- (A) Similarly — wrongly treats sentence 2 as a parallel idea rather than
  a result.
- (C) For instance — there's no illustrative example being given.
- (D) In contrast — nothing in sentence 2 opposes sentence 1.

**Trips people up:** confusing "result" transitions like "ultimately" or
"consequently" with "contrast" transitions when the second sentence
describes an ending or outcome that feels anticlimactic — an
unsatisfying result is still a result, not a contrast, unless the outcome
directly contradicts an expectation stated in sentence 1.

**Worked example 3:**
"Many students assume that speed-reading techniques improve comprehension.
____, several controlled studies have found that faster reading speeds are
associated with lower retention of detail."

A) Granted
B) In fact
C) Additionally
D) As a result

Cover the blank: sentence 1 states a common assumption. Sentence 2 presents
research that undercuts or corrects that assumption — not simply
contrasting it, but revealing the assumption to be wrong. "Additionally"
(continuation) is out — sentence 2 doesn't add a new, separate point, it
challenges sentence 1. "As a result" (cause/effect) is out — the studies
aren't a consequence of the assumption. "Granted" (concession) is out — a
concession would acknowledge the assumption has some merit before pivoting,
but this sentence flatly contradicts it instead. "In fact" is the classic
signal for "here's the truth that corrects the preceding misconception."
Answer: **B**.

- (A) Granted — implies partial agreement before a "but," which isn't the
  structure here; there's no concessive clause to complete it.
- (C) Additionally — treats the studies as a bonus point rather than a
  correction.
- (D) As a result — misreads the relationship as causal instead of
  corrective.

**Trips people up:** treating "correcting a misconception" the same as
plain "contrast" and defaulting to "however" — "in fact" specifically
signals that the second statement is the truth that replaces a wrong
belief, a distinct flavor of contrast worth recognizing on sight.

### E2 — Rhetorical synthesis — combine bullet-point notes to meet a stated goal

**What this actually tests:** this is a digital-SAT-only format (it didn't
exist on the paper SAT) — whether you can select, from a set of true facts,
which single sentence best accomplishes a *specific stated purpose*. It is
not testing whether you know the facts (they're all given to you and all
true) — it's testing whether you can match content to a goal.

**What the question looks like, exactly:** a short setup line ("While
researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:"), then 4–6
bullet points of short factual notes, then a stated goal in a sentence
("The student wants to emphasize a contrast between X and Y" / "...wants to
introduce the topic to an audience unfamiliar with it" / "...wants to
summarize the main finding of the study"), then: **"Which choice most
effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this
goal?"** — followed by four full-sentence answer choices, each built from a
different (or overlapping) subset of the bullet points.

**Strategy:**
1. Read the bullet points once, quickly — don't try to memorize them, just
   get a sense of what facts exist.
2. Read the **goal** carefully and restate it in your own words. This is the
   single most important step — most wrong answers are true, well-written
   sentences that just answer a *different* goal than the one stated.
3. Identify which bullet points are actually relevant to that goal. Some
   bullets exist purely as distractors for other goal-variants of the same
   question bank.
4. Go through the four choices and eliminate any that:
   - Answer a different goal (e.g., goal says "contrast," choice only lists
     similarities)
   - Introduce information not in the bullets (never allowed — every choice
     must only use given facts)
   - Are true and relevant but incomplete relative to the goal (e.g., goal
     says "emphasize a contrast" but the choice states both facts without
     any contrastive framing/connector)
   - Are grammatically awkward or run-on (still eliminates on the digital SAT)
5. The correct answer is usually the one that uses the *fewest* bullet
   points necessary while still fully satisfying the goal — synthesis
   questions reward precision, not maximal inclusion.

**Worked example:**

*While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:*
- The peregrine falcon can reach speeds over 240 mph in a hunting dive.
- The cheetah is the fastest land animal, reaching about 70 mph.
- The peregrine falcon's dive is called a "stoop."
- Cheetahs can only sustain top speed for about 20–30 seconds before
  overheating.
- Peregrine falcons are found on every continent except Antarctica.

*Student's goal: The student wants to emphasize a contrast between the
peregrine falcon and the cheetah in terms of speed.*

Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to
accomplish this goal?

A) The peregrine falcon is found on every continent except Antarctica, and
it hunts using a dive called a "stoop."

B) While the cheetah is the fastest land animal at about 70 mph, the
peregrine falcon can reach over 240 mph in its hunting dive.

C) The cheetah can only sustain its top speed of about 70 mph for 20–30
seconds, unlike the peregrine falcon, which lives on nearly every continent.

D) Both the peregrine falcon and the cheetah are remarkable for their speed
and hunting ability.

Walkthrough: goal = contrast, specifically about *speed*.
- (A) never mentions the cheetah or speed comparison at all — wrong goal,
  eliminate.
- (C) starts on-topic (cheetah's sustained speed) but pivots to the
  falcon's *habitat*, not its speed — the contrast breaks down, eliminate.
- (D) is a similarity statement ("both... remarkable"), the opposite of a
  contrast — eliminate.
- (B) states the cheetah's speed, then contrasts it directly with the
  falcon's much higher speed, using "While" to frame it as a contrast. This
  is the only choice that stays on the stated goal (speed) and correctly
  frames it as a contrast rather than a similarity. Answer: **B**.

**Trips people up:** choosing the answer with the most facts crammed in, or
the most "impressive-sounding" sentence, instead of checking it against the
exact wording of the goal — reread the goal one more time right before
picking, not just once at the start.

**Worked example 2:**

*While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:*
- The Wright brothers made their first powered flight in 1903 at Kitty
  Hawk, North Carolina.
- The flight lasted 12 seconds and covered 120 feet.
- Orville and Wilbur Wright ran a bicycle shop before working on aviation.
- Their design used a lightweight gasoline engine they built themselves.
- By 1905, their aircraft could fly for over 30 minutes.

*Student's goal: The student wants to introduce the topic to an audience
unfamiliar with the Wright brothers' achievement.*

Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to
accomplish this goal?

A) The Wright brothers, Orville and Wilbur, achieved the first powered
flight in 1903 at Kitty Hawk, North Carolina.

B) By 1905, the Wright brothers' aircraft, powered by a self-built gasoline
engine, could stay airborne for over 30 minutes.

C) Before turning to aviation, Orville and Wilbur Wright ran a bicycle
shop, where they developed mechanical skills.

D) The Wright brothers' first flight in 1903 covered just 120 feet in 12
seconds, a modest start to aviation history.

Walkthrough: goal = introduce the topic to a new audience, meaning give the
core, most identifiable fact first.
- (B) jumps to 1905 details without ever establishing the foundational
  1903 first-flight fact — an unfamiliar reader has no anchor, eliminate.
- (C) is interesting background but never mentions that they achieved
  powered flight at all — doesn't introduce the actual achievement,
  eliminate.
- (D) mentions the 1903 flight but frames it as "modest," which requires
  context the reader doesn't have yet and buries the headline fact under a
  qualifier — eliminate.
- (A) states who they were, what they achieved, and where/when — the
  cleanest, most complete introduction for someone who knows nothing about
  it. Answer: **A**.

**Trips people up:** picking the choice with the most vivid or specific
detail (like the exact 12 seconds/120 feet) over the choice that actually
orients a reader with zero prior context — "introduce to an unfamiliar
audience" wants the headline, not the trivia.

**Worked example 3:**

*While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:*
- Octopuses have three hearts and blue, copper-based blood.
- Two of the hearts pump blood to the gills; one pumps it to the rest of
  the body.
- The body-pumping heart stops beating when the octopus swims, which is
  why they prefer crawling.
- Octopuses can change both the color and texture of their skin in under a
  second.
- Octopuses have no bones, allowing them to squeeze through tiny gaps.

*Student's goal: The student wants to explain why octopuses tend to crawl
rather than swim.*

Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to
accomplish this goal?

A) Octopuses have three hearts and blue, copper-based blood, which is
unusual among marine animals.

B) Because swimming causes one of an octopus's three hearts to stop
beating, octopuses generally prefer to crawl.

C) With no bones in their bodies, octopuses can squeeze through
remarkably small gaps while crawling along the seafloor.

D) Octopuses can rapidly change their skin's color and texture, a
behavior often used for camouflage while crawling.

Walkthrough: goal = explain the cause of crawling preference specifically.
- (A) states a true, interesting fact (three hearts) but never connects it
  to swimming vs. crawling — no causal link, eliminate.
- (C) explains a benefit of crawling (fitting through gaps) but not *why*
  they prefer it over swimming — doesn't address the swimming heart issue,
  eliminate.
- (D) is about camouflage, unrelated to the swim/crawl mechanism,
  eliminate.
- (B) directly states the cause (swimming stops the body-pumping heart)
  and the effect (preference for crawling) — the only choice that
  actually explains the "why." Answer: **B**.

**Trips people up:** selecting a true, on-topic-sounding fact about
octopuses in general rather than checking whether it specifically supplies
the *causal mechanism* the goal is asking for — "explain why" demands a
cause-and-effect link, not just a related detail.

### E3 — Identify the most logical placement for a sentence in a paragraph

**What this actually tests:** whether you understand how a paragraph is
organized — topic → support → elaboration/example → conclusion, roughly —
well enough to know where a *given* sentence fits, or whether an
already-placed sentence should move.

**What the question looks like:** a paragraph with numbered sentences (or
blanks between sentences), plus one target sentence to place (either given
separately or already inserted somewhere and flagged as possibly
misplaced). Choices are usually "before sentence 1," "after sentence 2,"
etc., or "Sentence 3 should be placed (A) where it is now (B) before
sentence 1 (C) after sentence 4..." style.

**Strategy:**
1. Read the whole paragraph first, ignoring the sentence to be placed —
   figure out its natural flow: what's the topic sentence, what order do
   the supporting ideas go in (chronological? general-to-specific?
   cause-then-effect?).
2. Read the target sentence and identify what it *does*: does it introduce
   a new idea, provide an example, state a result, contrast with something,
   or wrap up/conclude?
3. Look for anchor words in the target sentence — pronouns ("this,"
   "these," "it"), transition words ("however," "as a result"), or specific
   nouns that must refer back to something already mentioned. A sentence
   starting with "This effect..." cannot go first — it needs an antecedent.
4. Test-fit the sentence at each candidate position and check: does
   everything right before it make sense leading into it, and does
   everything right after it still make sense following it? Only one
   position should pass both checks cleanly.

**Worked example:**

Paragraph: (1) Urban beekeeping has grown rapidly in the last decade. (2)
Rooftop hives now exist in cities from New York to Tokyo. (3) City
temperatures tend to be a few degrees warmer than surrounding rural areas,
extending the foraging season for bees. (4) As a result, urban honey yields
can sometimes exceed those of traditional rural apiaries.

Target sentence: "This urban heat-island effect also reduces winter die-off
rates in hives."

Where does it best fit?
A) Before sentence 1
B) After sentence 2
C) After sentence 3
D) After sentence 4

Walkthrough: the target sentence starts with "This urban heat-island
effect" — that phrase needs an antecedent describing a heat-island effect.
Sentence 3 is the only sentence that describes warmer city temperatures
(the heat-island effect) without naming it. Placing the target right after
sentence 3 lets "this...effect" refer back cleanly, and it still flows into
sentence 4's "As a result" (which needs *something* about longer foraging
or milder winters to justify "higher yields"). Before sentence 1 or after
sentence 2 both fail — there's no heat-island concept established yet.
After sentence 4 fails — "As a result" in sentence 4 would then dangle
before its cause is introduced. Answer: **C**.

**Trips people up:** placing a sentence based on topic similarity alone
instead of checking pronoun/reference words — two sentences can be "about
the same thing" and still be in the wrong order if a pronoun or transition
doesn't have anything to point back to.

**Worked example 2:**

Paragraph: (1) The public library began offering a "seed library" program
in 2019. (2) Patrons can borrow packets of vegetable and flower seeds at no
cost. (3) At the end of the growing season, gardeners are encouraged to
save seeds from their strongest plants and return them. (4) The program has
grown from 200 seed packets in its first year to over 3,000 today.

Target sentence: "This return-and-replenish cycle keeps the seed library
stocked without relying on outside purchases."

Where does it best fit?
A) Before sentence 1
B) After sentence 1
C) After sentence 3
D) After sentence 4

Walkthrough: the target sentence opens with "This return-and-replenish
cycle" — that phrase needs an antecedent that actually describes a
return-and-replenish process. Sentence 3 is the only sentence that
describes gardeners returning saved seeds after borrowing them, which is
the cycle being referenced. Placing the target right after sentence 3 lets
"this cycle" point back cleanly, and the target's point (the library stays
stocked without outside purchases) logically precedes sentence 4's growth
statistics, which make more sense once the reader understands the program
is self-sustaining. Before sentence 1 and after sentence 1 both fail —
neither borrowing nor returning has been described yet, so "this cycle"
has nothing to refer to. After sentence 4 fails — it would interrupt the
natural close of the paragraph on the growth statistic with an
explanatory aside that reads better earlier. Answer: **C**.

- (A) Before sentence 1 — no prior sentence exists to supply "this cycle."
- (B) After sentence 1 — only the founding year is known, not the
  borrow/return mechanism.
- (D) After sentence 4 — grammatically fine but disrupts the paragraph's
  natural progression toward its growth-statistic conclusion.

**Trips people up:** assuming a sentence belongs wherever it's
"thematically related," rather than tracing exactly which prior sentence
supplies the antecedent for a phrase like "this cycle" — the paragraph can
mention seeds throughout, but only one sentence actually establishes the
specific process being referenced.

**Worked example 3:**

Paragraph: (1) Many mountain towns rely on ski tourism for the bulk of
their annual revenue. (2) Warmer winters and shorter snow seasons have
reduced the number of skiable days at lower-elevation resorts. (3) Some
towns have begun marketing themselves as year-round destinations, adding
hiking trails, mountain biking, and summer festivals. (4) Diversifying
their tourism base has helped several towns offset the revenue lost from
shrinking ski seasons.

Target sentence: "As a result, resort revenue tied strictly to snowfall has
become increasingly unreliable."

Where does it best fit?
A) After sentence 1
B) After sentence 2
C) After sentence 3
D) After sentence 4

Walkthrough: the target sentence opens with "As a result," which needs a
preceding cause, and it describes snow-dependent revenue becoming
unreliable. Sentence 2 is the only sentence that states a cause of
declining skiable days (warmer winters, shorter snow seasons) — that's the
cause the target's "as a result" is pointing back to. Placing the target
right after sentence 2 also sets up sentence 3's "Some towns have begun
marketing themselves as year-round destinations" as the direct response to
the unreliability just described. After sentence 1 fails — no cause has
been given yet, just the general fact that towns rely on ski tourism.
After sentence 3 and after sentence 4 both fail — by then the paragraph
has already moved on to the solution (diversifying), so inserting the
unreliability point there would double back and break the cause-then-
response flow. Answer: **B**.

- (A) After sentence 1 — nothing yet explains why revenue would become
  unreliable.
- (C) After sentence 3 — the solution is already being described; the
  problem statement now reads out of order.
- (D) After sentence 4 — the paragraph has already reached its concluding
  point about successful diversification.

**Trips people up:** treating "as a result" placement questions the same
as pronoun-reference ones — with cause/effect transitions, the check isn't
"what noun does this point to" but "has the specific cause already been
stated," and inserting the sentence too late (after the response to the
problem) is just as wrong as inserting it too early.

### E4 — Concision — recognize and cut redundant/wordy phrasing

**What this actually tests:** whether you can spot when an underlined
portion says the same thing twice, uses more words than necessary, or
includes a phrase that adds zero new information — and pick the shortest
answer choice that preserves full meaning.

**What the question looks like:** an underlined portion of a sentence, four
answer choices that are different-length rewrites of that portion
(including the original as one choice), asking which "most effectively
combines the sentences" or "revises the underlined portion" — often with no
explicit "concision" language in the prompt itself, you just have to notice
the pattern.

**Strategy:**
1. On the SAT, when all four choices are grammatically correct, **shorter
   is almost always right** — this is the single most reliable tiebreaker
   for these questions specifically (does not apply to grammar questions
   generally, only to concision/combination ones).
2. Check for **redundant pairs**: two words/phrases that mean the same
   thing stacked together ("each and every," "basic fundamentals,"
   "past history," "completely eliminate," "advance warning," "unexpected
   surprise," "final outcome").
3. Check for **redundant restatement**: a phrase that repeats information
   already given elsewhere in the sentence (e.g., naming a date, then later
   saying "at that point in time").
4. Don't cut so far that meaning is lost — the shortest choice is only
   correct if it's still a complete, accurate, grammatical sentence. A
   choice can be short *and* wrong if it drops necessary information or
   creates a fragment.
5. Read the winning choice back into the full sentence once before
   finalizing — concision errors sometimes hide grammar errors introduced
   by the cut.

**Worked example:**

"The committee members discussed the proposal for two full hours, and at
the end of that two-hour discussion, they voted to approve it."

Which choice best revises the underlined portion?
A) NO CHANGE
B) and after they had finished discussing it for that period of time
C) and afterward
D) and after two hours of discussion had taken place

Walkthrough: "two full hours" is already established; "at the end of that
two-hour discussion" repeats the same two-hour fact a second time — pure
redundancy. (A) keeps the redundant restatement. (B) is even longer and
still redundant ("that period of time" still refers back needlessly). (D)
restates "two hours" again — still redundant, just reworded. (C)
"afterward" fully replaces the redundant clause with a single word that
preserves the meaning (something happened, then they voted) without
repeating the hour count. Answer: **C**.

**Trips people up:** assuming the shortest choice is automatically correct
without checking that it's still grammatical and hasn't deleted a fact the
sentence actually needs — concision is "cut what's redundant," not "cut as
much as possible."

**Worked example 2:**

"The museum's new exhibit includes a wide variety of many different
artifacts from ancient Mesopotamia."

Which choice best revises the underlined portion?
A) NO CHANGE
B) a wide and extensive variety of many different
C) a variety of
D) a diverse and varied assortment of numerous different

Walkthrough: "a wide variety," "many different," and "artifacts" are all
gesturing at the same idea — that there are numerous distinct types of
artifacts — stacked into one redundant pile. (A) keeps both "wide variety"
and "many different" describing the same plurality twice. (B) makes it
worse by adding a third redundant descriptor ("extensive"). (D) stacks
"diverse," "varied," and "numerous different" — four words doing the job
of one. (C) "a variety of" already implies multiple different types on its
own — nothing else needs to be added. Answer: **C**.

- (A) NO CHANGE — "wide variety" and "many different" both signal
  plurality/diversity redundantly.
- (B) — adds yet another synonym ("extensive") on top of the existing
  redundancy instead of removing it.
- (D) — replaces one redundant stack with an even longer one.

**Trips people up:** seeing a list of near-synonyms ("diverse," "varied,"
"numerous," "different") and assuming more descriptive words make the
sentence stronger — on concision questions, stacked synonyms are a
red flag, not a feature.

**Worked example 3:**

"Due to the fact that the bridge was closed for repairs, commuters had to
find an alternate route to get to their destination."

Which choice best revises the underlined portion?
A) NO CHANGE
B) Owing to the fact that
C) Because
D) In light of the fact that, and as a consequence of the closure,

Walkthrough: "due to the fact that" is a wordy four-word stand-in for a
single word that means the same thing. (A) keeps the wordy phrase as-is.
(B) swaps one wordy phrase for an equally wordy one — "owing to the fact
that" is no shorter or clearer. (D) is far longer and adds a second,
redundant reference to the closure that's already named later in the
sentence ("bridge was closed for repairs"). (C) "Because" expresses the
identical causal relationship in one word, with nothing lost. Answer: **C**.

- (A) NO CHANGE — "due to the fact that" is classic padded phrasing for
  "because."
- (B) — trades one wordy causal phrase for a near-identical one.
- (D) — adds a second, unnecessary reference to the cause already stated
  in the sentence, making it longer and redundant.

**Trips people up:** not recognizing "due to the fact that," "owing to the
fact that," and "in light of the fact that" as a family of interchangeably
wordy causal phrases that should almost always collapse to "because" or
"since" — these show up disguised as different-sounding choices that are
all equally guilty.

## Mini-Diagnostic

**E1.** Researchers initially assumed the compound was inert. ____, later
trials showed it reacted strongly with several common solvents.

A) Similarly
B) However
C) Consequently
D) For example

**E2.** *While researching a topic, a student has taken the following
notes:*
- The Great Barrier Reef stretches over 1,400 miles along Australia's
  coast.
- It is composed of nearly 3,000 individual reef systems.
- Rising ocean temperatures have caused repeated coral bleaching events
  since 2016.
- Coral bleaching occurs when heat-stressed coral expels the algae that
  gives it color and nutrients.
- Some reef sections have shown signs of recovery in cooler years.

*Student's goal: The student wants to explain the biological cause of
coral bleaching to an audience unfamiliar with the term.*

Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to
accomplish this goal?

A) The Great Barrier Reef stretches over 1,400 miles and is made up of
nearly 3,000 individual reef systems.

B) Coral bleaching, a process in which heat-stressed coral expels the
algae that gives it color and nutrients, has occurred repeatedly since
2016.

C) Some sections of the reef have shown signs of recovery in cooler years,
offering hope for its long-term survival.

D) Rising ocean temperatures are the primary threat facing the Great
Barrier Reef today.

**E3.** (1) Sourdough bread relies on a fermented starter rather than
commercial yeast. (2) The starter is a live culture of wild yeast and
bacteria that must be fed regularly. (3) This slower fermentation process
gives sourdough its characteristic tangy flavor. (4) Bakers often keep the
same starter alive for years, even generations. Where does the sentence
"Wild yeast ferments more slowly than commercial yeast" best fit?

A) Before sentence 1
B) After sentence 2
C) After sentence 3
D) After sentence 4

**E4.** "The new policy will completely eliminate all instances of the
previous fee structure entirely." Which choice best revises the underlined
portion?

A) NO CHANGE
B) will completely and entirely eliminate
C) will eliminate
D) will eliminate, once and for all, every last instance of

---

### Answer key

- **E1 — B.** The first sentence states an assumption ("assumed... inert");
  the second reverses it with contradicting evidence ("reacted strongly")
  — that's a contrast relationship, not addition, result, or example.
- **E2 — B.** The goal is explaining the *biological cause* of bleaching to
  a new audience — only choice B defines the mechanism (heat stress →
  algae expelled). A and D are true but off-goal (size/threat, not cause);
  C is about recovery, not cause.
- **E3 — C.** "Wild yeast ferments more slowly" needs sentence 2 (which
  establishes wild yeast as the starter's ingredient) already stated, and
  it directly sets up sentence 3's "This slower fermentation process" —
  placing it right before sentence 3 gives "this" a clear antecedent.
