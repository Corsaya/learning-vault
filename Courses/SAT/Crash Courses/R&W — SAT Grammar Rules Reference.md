## 0. The Two Terms Everything Depends On

Get these right and 60% of the section falls out automatically.

| Term | Test | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Independent clause (IC)** | Could it be a sentence by itself, with a period? | *The rain stopped.* |
| **Dependent clause (DC)** | Has subject + verb, but a starter word makes it incomplete | *Because the rain stopped* |
| **Phrase** | No subject-verb pair at all | *after the storm* / *running quickly* |

**DC starter words** (subordinating conjunctions): *because, although, though, since, while,
when, whenever, if, unless, until, after, before, as, whereas, whether, that, which, who.*

> [!tip] The one habit that fixes everything
> On every punctuation question, cover the answer choices and ask: **what's on the left of
> the blank, and what's on the right?** IC or not-IC. Answer that first, and the choices
> eliminate themselves.

## 1. Punctuation

### 1.1 The four ways to join two independent clauses

**Rule:** `IC + IC` may be joined **only** by one of these four:

| # | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | `IC. IC.` (period) | *The lab closed early. The samples stayed refrigerated.* |
| 2 | `IC; IC.` (semicolon) | *The lab closed early; the samples stayed refrigerated.* |
| 3 | `IC, FANBOYS IC.` (comma + coordinating conj.) | *The lab closed early, so the samples stayed refrigerated.* |
| 4 | `IC: IC.` (colon — only when clause 2 explains/answers clause 1) | *The reason was simple: the power had failed.* |

**FANBOYS** = **F**or, **A**nd, **N**or, **B**ut, **O**r, **Y**et, **S**o.

**Which are interchangeable — this is the highest-value fact on the whole test:**
Period and semicolon are **grammatically identical** to the SAT. So are all four when the
meaning fits. Therefore:

> [!important] The Two-Identical-Answers elimination
> If two answer choices do the exact same grammatical job, **both must be wrong** — there is
> only one correct answer. If choice A is a period and choice C is a semicolon between the
> same two clauses, **cross out both immediately**, without even reading the sentence.
> This single move solves several questions per test in about five seconds each.

- ✅ *The trail was steep; we climbed it anyway.*
- ❌ *The trail was steep, we climbed it anyway.* (comma splice)

**The trap:** the SAT offers period + semicolon as two separate choices and hopes you'll
agonize over which is "better." Neither. The answer is one of the other two.

### 1.2 Comma splices and run-ons — spotting them in one pass

**Rule:** A comma alone between two ICs is a **comma splice**. Nothing at all between two
ICs is a **run-on / fused sentence**. Both are always wrong.

**One-pass detection:** scan for a **second subject-verb pair** that isn't introduced by a
DC starter word. If you find one, the punctuation between them must be from the table in 1.1.

- ✅ *The film ran long, but nobody left.*
- ❌ *The film ran long, nobody left.*

**The trap:** comma splices **sound fine out loud** — your ear will not flag them. Run-ons
your ear catches; splices it does not. Never proofread this rule by ear.

**Also a splice** (the sneaky version): a comma + a **transition word** is still a splice,
because transition words are not FANBOYS.

- ❌ *The data was incomplete, however, the team published anyway.*
- ✅ *The data was incomplete; however, the team published anyway.*
- ✅ *The data was incomplete. However, the team published anyway.*

**The trap:** *however, therefore, moreover, nevertheless, consequently, thus, indeed,
furthermore, instead, for example* are **conjunctive adverbs**, not conjunctions. They
cannot hold two ICs together with a comma. FANBOYS can; these cannot.

### 1.3 Never separate a subject from its verb, or a verb from its object

**Rule:** A **single** comma may never fall between subject and verb, or between verb and
its object/complement. (A *pair* of commas around an interrupter is fine — that's 1.6.)

- ❌ *The scientists who ran the trial, published their results.* (subject | verb)
- ✅ *The scientists who ran the trial published their results.*
- ❌ *She admitted, that the schedule was unrealistic.* (verb | object)
- ✅ *She admitted that the schedule was unrealistic.*

**The trap:** a long subject makes you want to "breathe." The SAT sets this up with subjects
five or ten words long, banking on you inserting a pause-comma. **Length never licenses a
comma.** Also watch the comma before *that* — almost never correct.

### 1.4 Colons

**Rule:** Everything **before** a colon must be a complete independent clause. What comes
**after** can be anything: a list, a single word, a phrase, or another full clause.

- ✅ *She packed three things: a passport, a map, and sunscreen.*
- ❌ *She packed: a passport, a map, and sunscreen.* ("She packed" is incomplete)
- ✅ *The finding surprised no one: the drug worked.*
- ❌ *The ingredients include: flour, sugar, and salt.*

**Never put a colon after:** *such as, including, like, for example, are, was, consists of* —
these all leave the clause grammatically unfinished.

**The trap:** a beautiful, clean-looking list after the colon distracts you from checking the
left side. **Always look left of the colon, never right.**

### 1.5 Dashes

**Rule (three legal uses):**
1. **A pair of dashes** around a supplement mid-sentence — same job as a pair of commas.
2. **A single dash** before a break at the **end** of a sentence — same job as a colon there.
3. Dashes may enclose a supplement that itself contains commas, where commas would confuse.

**Matched-pair rule (heavily tested):** an interrupter must be enclosed by **two of the same
mark**. Dash…dash or comma…comma. **Never dash…comma or comma…dash.**

- ✅ *The proposal — drafted in a single night — passed unanimously.*
- ✅ *The proposal, drafted in a single night, passed unanimously.*
- ❌ *The proposal — drafted in a single night, passed unanimously.*
- ✅ *Only one thing stopped her — money.* (single dash, end break)

**The trap:** the SAT plants a dash *before* the interrupter in the non-underlined text and
offers a comma for the closing mark. Answer choices only ever show you one end. **Always look
outside the underline for the opening mark**, then match it exactly.

### 1.6 Restrictive vs. nonrestrictive — the comma test

**Rule:** Information that **identifies which one** you mean is **restrictive/essential** →
**no commas**. Information that is **extra**, removable without losing which one → **nonrestrictive** →
**commas on both sides** (or dashes, or parentheses — matched).

**The deletion test:** cover the phrase. Does the sentence still tell you *which one*?
- Still clear → it was extra → **commas**.
- Now vague or changed → it was essential → **no commas**.

| | Essential | Nonessential |
|---|---|---|
| Things | **that**, no commas | **, which**, commas |
| People | **who**, no commas | **, who**, commas |
| Names/appositives | no commas | commas both sides |

- ✅ *The report that arrived Monday was incomplete.* (which report — essential)
- ✅ *The report, which arrived Monday, was incomplete.* (only one report — extra)
- ❌ *The report which arrived Monday was incomplete.*
- ✅ *My oldest sister, Dana, lives in Chicago.* (only one oldest sister → extra)
- ✅ *The novelist Zadie Smith spoke last.* (name specifies which novelist → essential)

**The trap #1:** **"which" without commas is essentially always wrong on the SAT.** If you
see bare *which*, it's the error. Conversely, *that* essentially never takes a comma before it.

**The trap #2 (the half-fix):** the SAT gives you a choice with the **opening comma only**
or the **closing comma only**. One comma around an interrupter is still wrong. Both or neither.

### 1.7 Apostrophes

**Rule set:**

| Case | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Singular possessive | `'s` | *the student's essay* (one student) |
| Plural possessive (plural ends in -s) | `s'` | *the students' essays* (many students) |
| Irregular plural possessive (no -s) | `'s` | *the children's books*, *the women's team*, *the men's room* |
| Singular noun already ending in -s | `'s` (SAT-accepted) | *the class's schedule* |
| Plain plural — no ownership | **no apostrophe** | *three students arrived* |
| Possessive **pronouns** | **never an apostrophe** | *its, hers, theirs, ours, yours, whose* |
| Contractions | apostrophe = missing letters | *it's = it is/has*, *who's = who is/has*, *they're = they are* |

- ✅ *The company raised its forecast.* / ❌ *The company raised it's forecast.*
- ✅ *Whose data is this?* / ❌ *Who's data is this?*
- ✅ *the children's museum* / ❌ *the childrens' museum*

**Why possessive pronouns never take one:** the apostrophe marks either possession-on-a-noun
or a deleted letter. Pronouns already carry possession in their own form (*its, hers, theirs*),
so an apostrophe there can only mean a contraction. *It's* can therefore only ever mean *it is*.

**The trap:** the SAT's favorite apostrophe question is not *its/it's* — it's **singular vs.
plural possessive of the same noun** (*the scientist's findings* vs. *the scientists' findings*).
The choice depends entirely on **how many owners the passage established**, which is usually
stated one sentence earlier. **Read the previous sentence before answering.**

### 1.8 Lists and items in a series

**Rule:** Three or more items → comma after each, **including the Oxford comma before the
final *and/or***. Two items joined by *and* → **no comma**.

- ✅ *We studied lakes, rivers, and wetlands.*
- ❌ *We studied lakes, rivers and, wetlands.*
- ✅ *We studied lakes and rivers.* / ❌ *We studied lakes, and rivers.*
- **Semicolons in a list** when items already contain internal commas:
  ✅ *The stops were Lima, Peru; Quito, Ecuador; and Bogotá, Colombia.*

**The trap:** the SAT rarely tests whether the Oxford comma exists — it tests **stray commas
inside items**, like *lakes, rivers, and, wetlands*. Also: **no comma after the last item**
before the verb (that would violate 1.3).

### 1.9 Introductory elements and transitional words

**Rule:** An introductory word, phrase, or dependent clause is followed by a **comma**.

- ✅ *After the storm, the roads reopened.*
- ✅ *However, the results were inconclusive.*
- ✅ *Because funding fell through, the project stalled.*
- ❌ *Because funding fell through the project stalled.*

**Clause-order flip (tested constantly):**
- `DC, IC.` → **comma required**. *Although it rained, we went.*
- `IC DC.` → **usually no comma**. *We went although it rained.*

**A transition word inside a sentence** gets commas on both sides:
✅ *The results, however, were inconclusive.*

**The trap:** the unnecessary comma before a **trailing** *because/since/although*. Your ear
wants a pause after a complete clause. Don't give it one.

## 2. Subject-Verb Agreement (your known gap — read this twice)

**Core rule:** the verb agrees with the **true subject** — never with the nearest noun.

> [!important] The universal procedure — run this every single time
> 1. Find the **verb** (the word that changes with time).
> 2. Ask **"who or what [verb]s?"** → that's the true subject.
> 3. **Cross out everything between** the subject and the verb.
> 4. Read subject + verb alone, adjacent. *Collection… is.* *Results… are.*
>
> Step 3 is the whole game. Physically strike through the middle on scratch/screen.

**Reminder on endings** (backwards from nouns, which is why it feels wrong):
verbs ending in **-s are singular** (*he runs*); verbs **without -s are plural** (*they run*).

### 2.1 Buried subject — prepositional phrases (the #1 case)

**Rule:** The subject is **never** inside a prepositional phrase. Delete every
`preposition + noun` chunk between the subject and the verb before deciding.

Common buriers: *of, in, on, with, for, from, at, between, among, under, through, along with,
during, including*.

- ✅ *The **box** of old letters **was** in the attic.* (box was, not letters were)
- ❌ *The box of old letters were in the attic.*
- ✅ *The **effects** of the new policy on rural schools **remain** unclear.*
- ❌ *The effects of the new policy on rural schools remains unclear.*

**The trap:** the SAT stacks **two or three** prepositional phrases so the noun touching the
verb is the opposite number from the true subject. That's not an accident — it's the entire
design of the question. The noun right before the verb is the **decoy by default**.

### 2.2 Buried subject — "along with" phrases (do NOT create plurals)

**Rule:** Only the word **and** makes a compound subject plural. These do **not**:
*along with, as well as, in addition to, together with, accompanied by, including, besides,
rather than, as much as, not to mention*.

- ✅ *The **senator**, along with three aides, **is** attending.*
- ❌ *The senator, along with three aides, are attending.*
- ✅ *The **manuscript**, as well as its illustrations, **was** donated.*

**The trap:** these phrases **mean** "and" but don't **function** as "and." Semantics is the
bait; grammar is the answer. If the phrase sits between commas, it's an interrupter — delete it.

### 2.3 Buried subject — interrupting clauses and dashes

**Rule:** Nonessential clauses set off by **commas, dashes, or parentheses** are also invisible
to agreement. Delete them too.

- ✅ *The **findings** — which contradicted earlier work — **were** replicated.*
- ❌ *The findings — which contradicted earlier work — was replicated.*
- ✅ *The **teacher** who trained the debaters **coaches** the team.*

**The trap:** the interrupter contains its own subject and verb (*which contradicted*), and
your ear latches onto the last verb it heard. Dashes disguise interrupters better than commas
do — the SAT knows this and uses dashes for exactly this reason.

### 2.4 Inverted sentences — there is / there are, here is, and questions

**Rule:** In an inverted sentence the subject comes **after** the verb. *There* and *here*
are **never** subjects. Flip the sentence to find the real one.

- ✅ *There **are** several **reasons** for the delay.* (reasons are)
- ❌ *There is several reasons for the delay.*
- ✅ *There **is** one **reason** for the delay.*
- ✅ *Among the exhibits **stands** a bronze **statue**.* (a statue stands)
- ✅ *Here **are** the **files** you requested.*

**Also inverted:** sentences opening with a prepositional or place phrase —
*In the drawer **were** three **keys**.*

**The trap:** *there is* sounds acceptable before almost any noun in speech. It is **not** on
the SAT. Whenever you see *there*, immediately locate the noun after the verb and count it.

### 2.5 Compound subjects — "and" vs. "or"

**Rule:**
- Joined by **and** → **plural**. *Coffee and tea **are** available.*
- Joined by **or / nor / either…or / neither…nor** → the verb agrees with the **noun closest
  to it**. (Proximity rule — the one place "nearest noun" is actually correct.)

- ✅ *Neither the players nor the **coach was** available.*
- ✅ *Neither the coach nor the **players were** available.*
- ❌ *Neither the coach nor the players was available.*

**Exception to "and":** when *and* joins two names for **one thing/idea**, the subject is
singular. *Macaroni and cheese **is** my favorite.* / *My friend and mentor **has** retired.*
(one person, one *my*).

**The trap:** the SAT flips the order of the two nouns around *or/nor* between the passage and
the answer choices. The rule depends on **which noun ended up last**, not which felt more
important.

### 2.6 Collective nouns

**Rule:** Collective nouns — *team, committee, jury, group, family, staff, audience, class,
company, government, number, majority* — are treated as **singular** on the SAT when acting as
one unit, which is nearly always.

- ✅ *The **committee has** reached a decision.*
- ✅ *The **team is** traveling tomorrow.*
- ❌ *The team are traveling tomorrow.*
- ✅ Plural only when the members are explicitly acting separately (rare on the SAT):
  *The committee **were** divided among themselves.*

**Special pair worth memorizing:**
- **the number of** → **singular**. *The number of applicants **has** risen.*
- **a number of** → **plural**. *A number of applicants **have** withdrawn.*

**The trap:** *team, staff, jury* all denote many people, so your ear hears plural. On the
SAT, default to **singular** for collectives unless the sentence forces otherwise.

### 2.7 Indefinite pronouns

**Always singular** (this is the memorize-list):

> **-one:** anyone, everyone, someone, no one, one
> **-body:** anybody, everybody, somebody, nobody
> **-thing:** anything, everything, something, nothing
> **plus:** each, either, neither, another, much, little

**Always plural:** *both, few, many, several, others.*

**Depends on the object of the following "of" phrase** (the only such case — *SANAM*:
**S**ome, **A**ny, **N**one, **A**ll, **M**ost):
- *Most of the **cake is** gone.* / *Most of the **cookies are** gone.*
- *All of the **water was** drained.* / *All of the **samples were** tested.*

- ✅ *Each of the students **has** a locker.* (each… has)
- ❌ *Each of the students have a locker.*
- ✅ *Neither of the proposals **addresses** the cost.*
- ✅ *Everyone in the classes **was** notified.*

**The trap:** *each of / either of / neither of / one of* are followed by a **plural** noun
almost every time — that plural is inside a prepositional phrase and does not count. Delete
*of the students* and you're left with *each… has*. This is the single most-tested SVA
construction on the digital SAT.

**Related trap — "one of the [plural] who":** the verb inside the *who* clause is **plural**,
because *who* refers to the plural noun:
✅ *She is one of the **students who commute** daily.*

### 2.8 Nouns that look plural but aren't (and vice versa)

- **Singular despite -s:** *news, mathematics, physics, economics, politics, ethics, statistics
  (the field), the United States, measles.* → *Economics **is** required.*
- **Plural despite no -s:** *people, children, men, women, media (formal), criteria, phenomena,
  data (SAT usually accepts plural).* → *The criteria **were** revised.*
- **Titles and quoted names are singular:** *"Leaves of Grass" **is** his best-known work.*

**The trap:** *phenomena/phenomenon* and *criteria/criterion* — the -a form is the plural.

### 2.9 Gerund and clause subjects are singular

**Rule:** An *-ing* phrase or a *that*-clause acting as the subject is **singular**.

- ✅ ***Reading** long articles **improves** comprehension.*
- ❌ *Reading long articles improve comprehension.*
- ✅ *That the results **were** replicated **matters** greatly.*

**The trap:** the plural noun inside the gerund phrase (*articles*) sits right next to the
verb. Same decoy structure as 2.1.

## 3. Pronouns

### 3.1 Pronoun-antecedent agreement

**Rule:** A pronoun matches its antecedent in **number**. Singular thing → *it/its*. Plural →
*they/their*.

- ✅ *Each **student** must bring **their** own laptop.* (generic person → singular *they* is accepted)
- ✅ *The **company** revised **its** policy.*
- ❌ *The company revised their policy.*
- ❌ *A **researcher** must document **his or her** findings, and then they publish it.* (inconsistent)

**Digital SAT specifics:** singular **they** is **correct**, not an error, for generic or
gender-unspecified people (*a student, everyone, a customer*). But **organizations, companies,
committees, and teams take *it/its***, not *their*.

**The trap:** having correctly learned "singular they is fine," students over-apply it to
companies. *The company… their* is still wrong.

### 3.2 Ambiguous pronoun reference

**Rule:** A pronoun must have **exactly one** possible antecedent. If two nouns could be the
referent, the sentence is wrong even though every word is grammatical.

- ❌ *When Maya met Priya, **she** was nervous.* (who?)
- ✅ *When Maya met Priya, **Maya** was nervous.*
- ❌ *The board rejected the proposal because **it** was flawed.* (board or proposal?)

**The trap:** the SAT gives you a "cleaner, shorter" choice with a pronoun and a "clunkier"
choice repeating the noun. **The repetitive-sounding one is correct** when the pronoun is
ambiguous. Concision only wins after clarity.

**Also tested:** *this/that/which* pointing at a whole previous clause rather than a noun —
usually the error.

### 3.3 Pronoun case (subject vs. object)

| Subject | Object | Possessive |
|---|---|---|
| I, he, she, we, they, who | me, him, her, us, them, whom | my, his, her, our, their, whose |

**Test:** delete the other person from the compound.
- ✅ *The award went to Ravi and **me**.* (…went to me)
- ❌ *The award went to Ravi and I.*
- ✅ ***She** and I finished first.*

**who/whom:** substitute *he/him*. *He* → **who**. *Him* → **whom**.
- ✅ *the scientist **who** discovered it* (he discovered it)
- ✅ *the scientist **whom** we interviewed* (we interviewed him)

**The trap:** *between you and I* sounds educated and is always wrong — *between* is a
preposition, so it takes objects: *between you and me*. Likewise *myself* is not a fancy *me*;
reflexives require the same person as the subject earlier in the clause.

## 4. Verbs & Tense

### 4.1 Tense consistency

**Rule:** Don't shift tense without a reason in the passage. **Match the surrounding
sentences** — the correct tense is usually visible in the non-underlined text.

- ✅ *She collected the samples and analyzed them.*
- ❌ *She collected the samples and analyzes them.*

**The trap:** the answer choices offer four tenses and the sentence itself is neutral. The
evidence is in the **adjacent sentences**, not the one being tested. Look one sentence back.

### 4.2 Sequence of tenses

| Situation | Tense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Earlier of two **past** events | past perfect (*had* + p.p.) | *By the time we arrived, the show **had ended**.* |
| Past action continuing to now | present perfect (*has/have* + p.p.) | *She **has worked** here since 2019.* |
| Finished **before a future** point | future perfect (*will have* + p.p.) | *By June they **will have finished**.* |
| Habitual / general truth | simple present | *Water **boils** at 100 °C.* |

**Signal words:** *by the time, before, after, previously, earlier* → past perfect.
*since, for, so far, already, over the past decade* → present perfect.
*by [future date]* → future perfect.

**The trap:** past perfect is only for the **earlier** of two past events. If there's only one
past event, *had* is wrong. Overusing *had* is as much an error as omitting it.

### 4.3 Common irregular participles worth a glance

*has **written*** (not wrote) · *has **taken*** (not took) · *has **gone*** (not went) ·
*has **begun*** (not began) · *has **drunk*** · *has **risen*** · *has **chosen*** ·
*has **broken*** · *has **spoken*** · *has **shown***.

- ❌ *The team has went to regionals.* / ✅ *The team has **gone** to regionals.*

## 5. Modifiers

### 5.1 Dangling modifiers

**Rule:** An opening modifying phrase followed by a comma must describe the **noun that comes
immediately after the comma** — no exceptions, no "but the real subject is mentioned later."

- ✅ *Trained as a chemist, **Rosalind Franklin** used X-ray diffraction.*
- ❌ *Trained as a chemist, **X-ray diffraction** was used by Rosalind Franklin.*
- ❌ *Having finished the exam, **the room** felt suddenly quiet.*

**Procedure:** read the opening phrase, ask **"who did this?"**, then check the very first
noun after the comma. Mismatch → eliminate.

**The trap #1:** **passive voice** rewrites push the real actor to the end and sound fine.
They're still dangling.
**The trap #2:** a **possessive** doesn't count. *Rushing to leave, **Daniel's coat** was
forgotten* is wrong — *Daniel's coat* is a coat, not Daniel.

### 5.2 Misplaced modifiers

**Rule:** Descriptive phrases go **next to** what they describe.

- ❌ *She served cake to the guests **on paper plates that had melted**.*
- ✅ *She served the guests cake **that had melted** on paper plates.*
- ❌ *Covered in dust, he found the old journal.* → ✅ *He found the old journal, **covered in dust**.*

**The trap:** *only, almost, nearly, just, even* change meaning by position.
*She **only** ate the salad* ≠ *She ate **only** the salad.*

## 6. Parallel Structure

### 6.1 Lists and comparisons

**Rule:** Items joined by *and, or, than,* or *as* must share the same grammatical form —
all nouns, all gerunds, all infinitives, all clauses.

- ✅ *The job requires **writing**, **editing**, and **proofreading**.*
- ❌ *The job requires **writing**, **editing**, and **to proofread**.*
- ✅ *It is easier **to ask** than **to answer**.*

**The trap:** the break is nearly always in the **last** item. Read item 1's form, then jump
straight to the last item and compare.

### 6.2 Correlative conjunctions

**Rule:** Correlatives come in fixed pairs, and the structure after each half must match.

| Pair | Correct | Wrong |
|---|---|---|
| **not only … but also** | *not only **cheap** but also **fast*** | not only cheap but also it is fast |
| **either … or** | *either **by train** or **by bus*** | either by train or a bus |
| **neither … nor** | *neither **loud** nor **clear*** | neither loud or clear |
| **both … and** | *both **the cost** and **the delay*** | both the cost as well as the delay |
| **not … but** | *not **a setback** but **a redirection*** | — |

- ✅ *She is not only a chemist but also a novelist.*
- ❌ *She is not only a chemist but also writes novels.*

**The trap #1:** wrong partner — *neither…or*, *not only…but as well*, *both…as well as* are
all errors.
**The trap #2:** the words must sit in the **same position** relative to the verb.
*She **not only** wrote **but also** edited* ✅ vs. *She **not only** wrote **but** she **also**
edited* ✗ (parallelism broken by the added subject).

## 7. Comparisons & Word Choice

### 7.1 Countable vs. uncountable

| Countable | Uncountable |
|---|---|
| **fewer** books | **less** water |
| **number** of items | **amount** of sugar |
| **many** cars | **much** traffic |

- ✅ *Fewer students enrolled.* / ❌ *Less students enrolled.*

### 7.2 between vs. among

**between** = two things (or distinct individual items). **among** = three or more, as a group.
- ✅ *between the two options* / ✅ *among the five finalists*

### 7.3 Comparing like things — "that of / those of"

**Rule:** You must compare **things of the same kind**. A thing to a thing, not a thing to a
person or a category.

- ❌ *The **climate** of Peru is milder than **Chile**.* (climate vs. country)
- ✅ *The **climate** of Peru is milder than **that of** Chile.*
- ❌ *Her **paintings** sold for more than **Rivera**.*
- ✅ *Her **paintings** sold for more than **those of** Rivera.* (or *…than **Rivera's***)

**The trap:** these read perfectly naturally in speech. If a comparison appears and one
answer choice contains *that of / those of / those in / [Name]'s*, that choice is very often
correct — check what's actually being compared.

### 7.4 Comparative vs. superlative

**Two** things → comparative (*-er / more*). **Three or more** → superlative (*-est / most*).
- ✅ *the **taller** of the two* / ✅ *the **tallest** of the three*
- ❌ *more better*, *most fastest* (never double up)

### 7.5 Frequently confused words

| Word | Means | Quick check |
|---|---|---|
| **their** | possessive | *their car* |
| **there** | place / *there is* | contains "here" |
| **they're** | they are | expand it |
| **its** | possessive | *its tail* |
| **it's** | it is / it has | expand it |
| **whose** | possessive | *whose turn* |
| **who's** | who is / who has | expand it |
| **your / you're** | possessive / you are | expand it |
| **than** | comparison | *bigger **than*** |
| **then** | time / sequence | *and **then*** |
| **affect** | verb, to influence | **A**ction = **A**ffect |
| **effect** | noun, a result | *the* **e**ffect (takes *the*) |
| **accept / except** | receive / excluding | |
| **complement / compliment** | completes / praises | |
| **principal / principle** | main, head person / a rule | |
| **lose / loose** | to misplace / not tight | |
| **to / too / two** | direction / also, excessive / 2 | |

**The rare exception worth knowing:** *effect* can be a verb meaning "to bring about"
(*to **effect** change*), and *affect* a noun in psychology (*flat **affect***). The SAT
tests the common pair far more often, but don't be startled by *effect change*.

## 8. Transitions — Reference Table

Technically Expression of Ideas, not Conventions, but it's the same lookup reflex, and
transitions are ~2–4 questions per module. See [[R&W — Expression of Ideas]] for drills.

| Relationship | Words | Use when… |
|---|---|---|
| **Contrast** | however, nevertheless, nonetheless, still, conversely, on the other hand, in contrast, by contrast, yet, instead, rather, whereas, alternatively | Sentence 2 opposes or qualifies sentence 1 |
| **Cause** | because, since, as, due to, owing to | Sentence 2 gives the reason |
| **Effect / result** | therefore, thus, consequently, as a result, hence, accordingly, so | Sentence 2 is the outcome |
| **Addition** | moreover, furthermore, in addition, additionally, also, similarly, likewise | Sentence 2 adds a same-direction point |
| **Example** | for example, for instance, specifically, notably, to illustrate, in particular | Sentence 2 illustrates sentence 1 |
| **Sequence / time** | first, next, then, subsequently, meanwhile, previously, finally, eventually, afterward | Ordering steps or events |
| **Concession** | admittedly, granted, to be sure, of course, although, while, despite | Conceding a point before pushing back |
| **Emphasis** | indeed, in fact, above all, certainly, undeniably | Sentence 2 intensifies sentence 1 |
| **Restatement** | in other words, that is, put differently, namely | Sentence 2 rephrases sentence 1 |
| **Conclusion / summary** | in conclusion, in short, ultimately, overall, to summarize | Wrapping up |

**Procedure for a transition question:**
1. **Cover the choices.** Read the sentence **before** and the sentence **after**.
2. In your own words, name the relationship: *same direction / opposite / result / example*.
3. Only then look at the choices, and pick the one in that row.
4. If two choices are in the **same row** (e.g. *therefore* and *consequently*), neither can be
   the answer — same elimination logic as identical punctuation.

**The trap:** the SAT loads three plausible-sounding transitions from the *wrong* category and
one plain one from the right category. Never pick by which sounds most sophisticated.

**Note:** *however, therefore, moreover* etc. are not conjunctions — see 1.2 for their
punctuation.

## 9. Rules the SAT Does NOT Test

Do not lose time on these, and **do not eliminate a correct answer** because of one:

- **Split infinitives** — *to boldly go* is fine. Not tested, not an error.
- **Ending a sentence with a preposition** — *the theory she relied on* is fine.
- **Starting a sentence with a conjunction** — *But the results differed.* is fine.
- **Starting with *because*** — fine, as long as an IC follows: *Because it rained, we left.*
- **Singular *they*** — accepted for generic/unspecified people. Not an error.
- **Passive voice** — not an error by itself. Only wrong when it creates a dangling modifier
  or genuine ambiguity.
- **The Oxford comma** — the SAT uses it; it is never the thing being tested for or against.
- ***Which* vs. *that* as a style preference** — what's tested is the **comma**, not taste.
- **Spelling, capitalization, and vocabulary difficulty** — not part of Conventions.
- **Contractions being "informal"** — *it's, don't* are fine when correct.
- **Sentence length or "wordiness" in Conventions questions** — concision is an Expression of
  Ideas concern, and never beats grammatical correctness.
- ***Whom* being required everywhere** — it's only required in object position; *who* as a
  subject is correct even after a preposition-heavy sentence.

## 11. The Punctuation Decision Procedure

Run this on any Conventions question whose four choices differ only in punctuation. It is a
**fixed algorithm** — same steps, same order, every time. Do not read the choices for
"sound."

### Step 0 — Identify the question type (2 seconds)
Look at what varies across the four choices.
- Punctuation only → **run this procedure.**
- Verb forms → subject-verb or tense; run the SVA procedure ([[#2. Subject-Verb Agreement (your known gap — read this twice)|§2]]).
- Pronouns → agreement/case ([[#3. Pronouns|§3]]).
- Transition words → [[#8. Transitions — Reference Table|§8]] procedure.

### Step 1 — Read the whole sentence with the blank ignored
Get the structure, not the meaning. You need the shape of the sentence.

### Step 2 — Classify both sides of the blank
Cover the choices. Ask two questions:
- **Left of the blank: is it a complete independent clause?** (Y/N)
- **Right of the blank: is it a complete independent clause?** (Y/N)

Write **Y/N** or **IC/frag** in the margin. Do not skip this — this classification alone
determines the answer in most questions.

### Step 3 — Apply the grid

| Left | Right | What's legal |
|---|---|---|
| **IC** | **IC** | period · semicolon · comma+FANBOYS · colon (if right explains left) · single dash |
| **IC** | fragment / list | colon · single dash · comma (if the fragment is a modifier) |
| DC/frag | **IC** | **comma** (leading DC) — never a semicolon, never a colon |
| **IC** | DC (*because/since/although*) | **no punctuation**, usually |
| — | — | **Interrupter mid-sentence** → matched pair (comma…comma or dash…dash) or none at all |

### Step 4 — Kill duplicates
Any two choices that do the **same grammatical job** are both wrong. Cross both out.
- period ≡ semicolon
- comma…comma ≡ dash…dash (around the same interrupter)
- two choices with the same mark in the same slot differing only elsewhere → compare the
  difference, not the sameness

This usually leaves two choices.

### Step 5 — Apply the never-list
Cross out any surviving choice that does one of these:
- single comma between **subject and verb**, or **verb and object**
- **colon** without a complete clause before it
- **mismatched pair** (dash…comma, comma…dash) or a **single** comma around an interrupter
- **bare *which*** with no comma, or ***that*** preceded by a comma
- **comma + conjunctive adverb** (*however, therefore, moreover*) joining two ICs
- comma before a **trailing** *because/since/although*
- **comma before *that***

### Step 6 — Default to less punctuation
If two choices both survive and you can't distinguish them, take the one with **fewer marks**.
The SAT's default is that punctuation must be *earned* — no rule requiring a mark means no mark.

### Step 7 — Read your answer back into the sentence once
Confirm the sentence still says what the passage means. Then move on. Do not revisit.

> [!important] Under time pressure, the compressed version
> **1. IC or not, on each side. 2. Kill the twins. 3. Kill the never-list. 4. Fewer marks wins.**

### Worked run-through

> The research team, which spent four years cataloging the archive ______ published its
> findings last spring.
> A) , B) ; C) : D) (no punctuation)

- **Step 2:** Left of the blank = *The research team, which spent four years cataloging the
  archive* — that's a subject plus a nonessential clause. **Not a complete IC** (no main verb yet).
  Right = *published its findings last spring* — **not an IC** either (no subject; the main
  verb belongs to *team*).
- **Step 3:** Neither side is independent. This is not a clause-joining question at all — it's
  an **interrupter** question. *which spent four years cataloging the archive* is nonessential
  and already has an opening comma before *which*.
- **Step 4/5:** B) semicolon requires an IC on both sides — dead. C) colon requires an IC on
  the left — dead. D) leaves the interrupter with only one comma — a mismatched half-fix, dead.
- **Answer: A)** — the closing comma of a matched pair.

Note that Step 2 is what saved it: the blank looked like a clause boundary and wasn't. **Always
classify before you choose.**
