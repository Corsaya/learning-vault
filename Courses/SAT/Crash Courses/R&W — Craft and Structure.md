# R&W — Craft and Structure Crash Course

Craft and Structure is the single largest Reading & Writing domain — roughly
**28% of the section, 13–15 questions**, per
[[../Research/Official SAT Structure and Content Research (2026-08-07)|Official SAT Structure and Content Research]].
It covers four skills (R1–R4): Words in Context, Text Structure, Cross-Text
Connections, and Central Ideas/Purpose. None of these are formula-based —
they're all reading-comprehension skills, which means the "method" here is a
disciplined, repeatable *process* for reading and eliminating, not a rule to
memorize. Every worked example below leans hardest on the elimination
reasoning, since that's where points are actually won or lost on this domain.

---

### R1 — Words in context

**What this actually tests:** not vocabulary size — precise *contextual*
meaning. The digital SAT gives you a short passage (~25–150 words) with one
word blanked out or bolded, and asks which answer choice means the same
thing **as it's used in this specific sentence**. The four choices are often
all real synonyms of *some* meaning of the target word — but only one fits
the sentence's actual logic.

**Strategy:**
1. Cover/ignore the answer choices at first. Read the sentence (and the one
   before/after, if there is one) and predict your own simple word for the
   blank — the research pass called this the "blanking" technique: swap in
   something dumb-simple like "important" or "confusing" that captures the
   sense, don't reach for a fancy synonym.
2. *Now* look at the choices and pick whichever one is closest to your
   predicted word — not whichever one you recognize as a "smart" word.
3. Plug your choice back into the sentence and reread it. If it doesn't
   sound like the same idea the sentence was building, it's wrong even if
   it's a real synonym of the target word in some other context.

**Worked example:**

> Although the committee's report was thorough, its conclusions were
> largely **derivative**, restating findings from three earlier studies
> without adding new analysis or challenging any of their assumptions.

As used in the text, "derivative" most nearly means://
A) Financially complex
B) Unoriginal
C) Insulting
D) Poorly organized

Predict first: the sentence says the report "restat[ed] findings... without
adding new analysis" — so the word means something like "copied" or "not
new."

- **(B) Unoriginal** matches that prediction exactly — restating others'
  work without adding anything new is the definition of unoriginal.
- (A) Financially complex is the *finance* meaning of "derivative" (as in a
  derivative security) — a real dictionary meaning of the word, but not the
  one this sentence is using. This is the classic trap choice.
- (C) Insulting has no support — nothing in the sentence says the report was
  rude or offensive, only that it added nothing new.
- (D) Poorly organized is a plausible-sounding negative trait but isn't what
  the sentence describes — "restating without adding new analysis" is about
  originality, not organization.

**Thing that trips people up:** the SAT often picks a word with a common,
unrelated dictionary meaning that *isn't* the one being tested (like
"derivative" the finance term vs. "derivative" meaning unoriginal) — the
most familiar meaning of the word is frequently the wrong-answer trap, not
the right answer. Always predict from context before looking at choices.

**Worked example 2:**

> Critics initially dismissed the sculptor's late work as **austere**,
> noting that she had stripped away the ornamental flourishes of her
> earlier pieces in favor of bare, unadorned geometric forms that left
> little for the eye to linger on.

As used in the text, "austere" most nearly means:

A) Severely plain
B) Expensive
C) Emotionally moving
D) Historically inspired

Predict first: the sentence describes work that "stripped away ornamental
flourishes" in favor of "bare, unadorned" forms — so the word means
something like "plain" or "bare."

- **(A) Severely plain** matches that prediction — stripped-down, unadorned
  work is the definition of severely plain.
- (B) Expensive has no support — nothing in the sentence discusses cost or
  materials.
- (C) Emotionally moving is a plausible reaction to art but isn't what the
  sentence describes — it describes visual simplicity, not emotional
  impact.
- (D) Historically inspired has no support — the sentence contrasts this
  work with the sculptor's *own* earlier pieces, not with history.

**Trips people up:** "austere" carries a harsh, disapproving connotation in
everyday speech, which can push test-takers toward reading the sentence as
more negative than it is. The tested meaning here is neutral — just "plain/
unadorned" — so lean on the sentence's own description, not the word's
usual emotional flavor.

**Worked example 3:**

> Engineers rejected the initial data-storage design because the memory it
> relied on was **volatile**, meaning any information held there vanished
> the instant power to the device was cut, making it unsuitable for
> archiving critical records long-term.

As used in the text, "volatile" most nearly means:

A) Explosive
B) Prone to sudden anger
C) Losing its contents without a continuous power source
D) Rapidly increasing in value

Predict first: the sentence explains its own meaning directly — data
"vanished the instant power... was cut" — so the word means something like
"not permanent" or "erasable."

- **(C)** matches that built-in definition exactly.
- (A) Explosive is the chemistry/hazardous-materials meaning of "volatile,"
  a real dictionary sense but not the one this sentence describes.
- (B) Prone to sudden anger is the personality-trait meaning of "volatile"
  — a trap for a word with several unrelated senses.
- (D) Rapidly increasing in value is the finance/stock-market meaning of
  "volatile," also unrelated to what this sentence is describing.

**Trips people up:** "volatile" has several common, unrelated technical
meanings (chemistry, temperament, finance, computing). When a passage
explains its own term in-line, as this one does ("meaning any information
held there vanished..."), that explanation overrides every other sense of
the word you might know.

### R2 — Text structure

**What this actually tests:** whether you can see the *function* each part
of a passage is playing — is a sentence introducing a claim, complicating
it, offering evidence, conceding a counterpoint, resolving a tension? These
questions ask something like "which choice best describes the overall
structure of the text" or "how does the underlined sentence function in the
passage."

**Strategy:**
1. As you read, mentally label each sentence or chunk with its job: Claim?
   Example? Complication/twist? Counterargument? Resolution? You don't need
   fancy terms — just track "what is this sentence *doing*" alongside "what
   is this sentence *saying*."
2. For "how does this function" questions, predict the function in your own
   words before reading choices (same predict-first discipline as R1).
3. Eliminate any choice that mislabels the *role* — a choice can accurately
   summarize the sentence's content and still be wrong if it gets the
   function wrong (e.g., calls a piece of supporting evidence a
   "counterargument").

**Worked example:**

> For decades, marine biologists assumed deep-sea vents supported little
> life due to extreme pressure and total darkness. In 1977, researchers
> diving near the Galápagos Rift found dense colonies of tube worms, clams,
> and crabs thriving around the vents. These organisms, it turned out, rely
> not on sunlight but on bacteria that convert chemicals in the vent fluid
> into energy — a process called chemosynthesis.

Which choice best describes the function of the second sentence ("In 1977...
thriving around the vents") in the text as a whole?

A) It summarizes the passage's main argument.
B) It presents a discovery that contradicts the assumption stated earlier.
C) It offers a hypothesis the rest of the passage goes on to disprove.
D) It restates the first sentence in different words.

- **(B)** is correct: the first sentence sets up an assumption ("little
  life"), and the second sentence directly reports a finding that
  contradicts it (dense colonies thriving there).
- (A) is wrong because the second sentence is a specific example/finding,
  not a summary of the whole passage's argument — that would need to
  encompass the chemosynthesis explanation too.
- (C) is wrong because the sentence reports an actual finding, not a
  hypothesis, and nothing in the passage disproves it — the passage instead
  explains *why* it's true (chemosynthesis).
- (D) is wrong because the second sentence's content (a specific 1977
  discovery) is different information from the first sentence (a general
  prior assumption), not a restatement.

**Thing that trips people up:** structure questions often have a choice
that's factually accurate about the sentence's content but assigns it the
wrong *job* in the passage — read for role, not just for meaning.

**Worked example 2:**

> The printing press is often credited with single-handedly launching the
> Reformation. Historians note, however, that literacy rates across
> sixteenth-century Europe remained low, and that many reform ideas spread
> for years through sermons and word of mouth well before printed pamphlets
> became widely available.

Which choice best describes the function of the second sentence
("Historians note... available") in the text as a whole?

A) It provides additional evidence supporting the first sentence's claim.
B) It complicates the claim made in the first sentence.
C) It restates the first sentence using different terminology.
D) It offers a hypothesis that later research will confirm.

- **(B)** is correct: the first sentence credits the printing press with
  "single-handedly" launching the Reformation; the second sentence
  undercuts that with counter-evidence (low literacy, ideas already
  spreading by sermon before pamphlets existed).
- (A) is wrong because the second sentence pushes against the first
  sentence's claim rather than reinforcing it.
- (C) is wrong because the second sentence introduces new information
  (literacy rates, sermons) not present in the first sentence — it isn't a
  rewording.
- (D) is wrong because historians "note" this as an established
  observation, not a hypothesis, and no later sentence confirms it.

**Trips people up:** a sentence that starts with an attribution like
"Historians note, however," is a strong structural signal (the "however"
flags a pivot) — missing small transition words like this is a common way
to misjudge a sentence's function.

**Worked example 3:**

> Solar power generation peaks at midday, but electricity demand typically
> peaks in the early evening. Grid-scale battery storage addresses this
> mismatch by capturing excess midday solar output and releasing it several
> hours later, smoothing supply to match when it's actually needed.

Which choice best describes the function of the second sentence
("Grid-scale battery storage... needed") in the text as a whole?

A) It introduces a new problem unrelated to the first sentence.
B) It describes a solution to the timing mismatch identified in the first
   sentence.
C) It contradicts the claim made in the first sentence.
D) It provides a historical example of the problem discussed in the first
   sentence.

- **(B)** is correct: the first sentence sets up a timing mismatch between
  solar generation and demand, and the second sentence explains how battery
  storage resolves that specific mismatch.
- (A) is wrong because the second sentence directly addresses the problem
  from sentence one — it doesn't introduce an unrelated one.
- (C) is wrong because the second sentence builds on the first sentence's
  premise rather than disputing it.
- (D) is wrong because the sentence describes a present-day mechanism, not
  a historical example, and it's a solution, not another instance of the
  problem.

**Trips people up:** when a passage states a problem and then immediately
pivots to a fix, it's easy to mislabel the fix as "more evidence of the
problem" rather than recognizing the shift from problem to solution.

### R3 — Cross-text connections

**What this actually tests:** two short, related passages (Text 1 and Text
2), usually presenting different perspectives, findings, or emphases on the
same topic. The question asks how the two relate — do they agree, conflict,
does one complicate the other, would one author agree or disagree with a
claim in the other?

**Strategy:**
1. Read Text 1 and reduce its core claim to one short phrase in your head.
2. Read Text 2 and do the same.
3. Before looking at choices, name the *relationship* explicitly: do they
   agree, contradict, does Text 2 add a caveat/exception to Text 1's claim,
   does Text 2 offer evidence for or against Text 1?
4. Eliminate any choice that's really just a summary of *one* passage rather
   than a statement about the *relationship between* them — this is the
   single most common trap on these questions.

**Worked example:**

**Text 1:** Economist Rina Okoye argues that remote work permanently
reduced urban commercial rents by shrinking daytime office demand, and that
this effect will persist even as more employers require in-office days.

**Text 2:** A 2026 real estate survey found that commercial rents in
several major cities have rebounded to near pre-2020 levels as return-to-
office mandates expanded, though rents remain below pre-2020 levels in
cities where remote work stayed most common.

Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to
Okoye's claim in Text 1?

A) By agreeing completely, since both texts describe falling commercial
   rents.
B) By disagreeing that remote work affects rents at all.
C) By suggesting the effect Okoye describes is not as permanent or uniform
   as she claims, since rents have rebounded where offices reopened.
D) By arguing that rents fell for reasons unrelated to remote work.

- **(C)** is correct: Text 2's rebound data complicates Okoye's
  "permanent... even as more employers require in-office days" claim —
  showing rents recovered specifically where offices reopened is direct
  evidence against permanence.
- (A) is wrong because it ignores the relationship — Text 2 doesn't
  uniformly show falling rents, it shows a rebound, which is closer to the
  opposite of Okoye's "permanent decline" claim.
- (B) overstates the disagreement — Text 2 doesn't deny remote work affects
  rents at all (it explicitly notes rents stayed lower where remote work
  persisted); it only challenges the *permanence* claim.
- (D) is unsupported — Text 2 never proposes an alternative cause for the
  rent changes.

**Thing that trips people up:** cross-text questions are about the
*relationship* between the two claims, not a report card on each passage
individually — a choice that accurately restates Text 1 or Text 2 alone,
but says nothing about how they connect, is always wrong.

**Worked example 2:**

**Text 1:** Paleontologist Marcus Webb argues that the mass extinction of
large Ice Age mammals in North America was caused primarily by rapid
climate warming, which destroyed the grasslands they depended on.

**Text 2:** An analysis of fossil sites found that many large mammal
populations began declining several thousand years before the climate
warming Webb identifies, coinciding instead with the arrival of human
hunters in the region.

Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to
Webb's claim in Text 1?

A) By agreeing that climate warming was the sole cause of the extinction.
B) By suggesting that a different factor, human hunting, better explains
   the timing of the decline than climate warming does.
C) By denying that any large mammal extinction occurred.
D) By arguing that grassland loss had no effect on the mammals.

- **(B)** is correct: Text 2's timing evidence — decline beginning before
  the warming and lining up with human arrival instead — points to a
  different likely cause than the one Webb identifies.
- (A) is wrong because it's the opposite of what Text 2's timing evidence
  suggests.
- (C) is wrong because Text 2 itself documents a population decline; it
  doesn't deny the extinction happened.
- (D) is wrong because Text 2 never discusses grassland loss or its
  effects — that's outside what the passage addresses.

**Trips people up:** Text 2 doesn't have to explicitly say "Webb is wrong"
— it can challenge Text 1 just by supplying evidence (here, timing) that
points toward a different cause. Don't wait for direct disagreement
language; infer the relationship from what the evidence implies.

**Worked example 3:**

**Text 1:** A literary critic argues that a novel's fragmented timeline
reflects the protagonist's psychological trauma, calling it a deliberate
craft choice by the author.

**Text 2:** A scholar's analysis of the author's early drafts shows the
timeline was originally told in linear order and was reorganized late in
the editing process for pacing reasons, with no documented connection to
trauma-themed intentions.

Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to
the claim in Text 1?

A) By fully endorsing the trauma-based interpretation, since both texts
   discuss the novel's timeline.
B) By questioning whether the fragmented structure was actually intended to
   reflect trauma, given evidence that it resulted from pacing-driven edits.
C) By arguing that the novel has no meaningful structure at all.
D) By claiming the author never revised the manuscript.

- **(B)** is correct: Text 2's draft evidence (originally linear,
  reorganized for pacing) undercuts the claim that the fragmentation was a
  deliberate trauma-reflecting choice.
- (A) is wrong because Text 2's evidence works against, not for, the
  trauma-based reading.
- (C) is wrong and far too extreme — Text 2 discusses *why* the structure
  changed, not whether the novel has structure at all.
- (D) is wrong because Text 2's evidence (early drafts vs. the final,
  reorganized version) implies revision did happen.

**Trips people up:** cross-text science/history examples aren't the only
flavor tested — the same "does Text 2 support, complicate, or contradict
Text 1" logic applies to humanities topics like literary criticism just as
often.

### R4 — Central claim/purpose

**What this actually tests:** can you compress an entire short passage into
its single main point or the author's reason for writing it. Distinct from
R2 (structure of parts) — this is about the *whole* passage's takeaway, not
what any one sentence is doing.

**Strategy:**
1. After reading the whole passage once, close-paraphrase it in one short
   sentence in your own words *before* looking at answer choices.
2. Match your paraphrase to the closest choice.
3. Eliminate choices that are true but too narrow (restate only one detail
   or example rather than the whole passage), and choices that are too
   broad (claim something the passage doesn't actually support).

**Worked example:**

> Urban planners have long favored wide, multi-lane roads to reduce traffic
> congestion. Yet a growing body of research on "induced demand" suggests
> that adding lanes tends to increase total traffic volume over time, as
> the added capacity draws more drivers who previously avoided the route
> or traveled at off-peak hours. Some cities have responded by narrowing
> roads or removing lanes altogether, reporting little long-term increase
> in congestion despite the reduced capacity.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

A) To argue that all cities should immediately narrow their major roads.
B) To describe one city's experience removing a traffic lane.
C) To present evidence that expanding road capacity may not reduce traffic
   the way planners have traditionally assumed.
D) To explain the history of urban traffic planning.

- **(C)** is correct — it captures the whole arc: the traditional
  assumption (wider roads reduce congestion), the complicating research
  (induced demand), and the supporting evidence (cities narrowing roads
  without worse congestion).
- (A) is too strong/broad — the passage reports evidence and a trend, it
  never argues "all cities should immediately" act.
- (B) is too narrow — the "some cities" detail is one supporting example,
  not the passage's main purpose.
- (D) is too broad and inaccurate — the passage isn't a history, it's built
  around a specific claim and counter-evidence about induced demand.

**Thing that trips people up:** wrong choices for main-purpose questions
usually fail by being either too narrow (just one detail/example dressed up
as "the point") or too broad (a claim bigger than what the passage actually
supports) — the correct choice sits at exactly the passage's own scope, no
wider, no narrower.

**Worked example 2:**

> Early spellcheck programs simply compared each word against a fixed
> dictionary, flagging any it didn't recognize. Modern predictive-text
> systems instead model the probability of word sequences, allowing them to
> catch errors a fixed dictionary would miss, such as a correctly spelled
> word used in the wrong context. This shift from static lookup to
> probabilistic modeling has made contemporary writing tools dramatically
> better at catching subtle errors, though not infallible.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

A) To provide a complete history of every spellcheck program ever released.
B) To explain how a shift from dictionary lookup to probabilistic modeling
   improved writing tools' ability to catch subtle errors.
C) To argue that predictive-text systems are completely error-free.
D) To describe one specific error a spellchecker failed to catch.

- **(B)** is correct — it captures the whole arc: the old method (fixed
  dictionary), the new method (probabilistic modeling), and the resulting
  improvement in catching subtle errors.
- (A) is too broad and inaccurate — the passage compares two approaches, it
  isn't a complete product history.
- (C) is too strong — the passage explicitly says "though not infallible,"
  directly contradicting a claim of complete error-free performance.
- (D) is too narrow — no single specific error is described; the passage
  discusses the general *type* of error the newer approach catches.

**Trips people up:** a passage that ends on a hedge ("though not
infallible") is signaling that the correct main-purpose choice should stay
measured too — an answer choice that overclaims certainty is a common trap
right after this kind of qualifier.

**Worked example 3:**

> For much of the twentieth century, archaeologists assumed that
> large-scale monument building required a centralized state with forced
> labor. Excavations at several early monumental sites, however, have found
> no evidence of social stratification, permanent rulers, or coerced
> workers — instead suggesting the structures were built through seasonal,
> voluntary communal labor. These findings have pushed researchers to
> reconsider long-held assumptions about the political conditions necessary
> for large-scale construction.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

A) To describe the physical construction techniques used at ancient
   monumental sites.
B) To argue that all ancient societies were free of political hierarchy.
C) To present findings that challenge the assumption that centralized state
   power was necessary for large-scale monument building.
D) To summarize a complete timeline of monument-building throughout
   history.

- **(C)** is correct — it captures the passage's full arc: the old
  assumption (centralized state required), the new evidence (no
  stratification or coerced labor found), and the resulting reconsideration
  of that assumption.
- (A) is too narrow — construction technique is never discussed; the
  passage is about the *social/political conditions* behind construction.
- (B) is too broad — the findings concern specific excavated sites, not a
  sweeping claim about "all ancient societies."
- (D) is too broad and inaccurate — the passage isn't a timeline, it's
  built around one specific reconsidered assumption.

**Trips people up:** a choice like (B) can feel right because it's "in the
spirit" of the passage's finding, but it stretches a claim about particular
excavated sites into a claim about every ancient society — watch for
answer choices that generalize past what the evidence in the passage
actually covers.

## Mini-Diagnostic

One question per skill. Different passages from the worked examples above —
answer key with reasoning is at the bottom.

**R1.** Words in context

> The city council's plan to redesign the waterfront was initially met with
> skepticism, but residents who attended the design workshops described the
> final proposal as remarkably **judicious**, balancing new commercial
> development with preservation of public green space.

As used in the text, "judicious" most nearly means:

A) Legally binding
B) Well-balanced and sensible
C) Financially expensive
D) Widely publicized

**R2.** Text structure

> Cats were first domesticated roughly 10,000 years ago in the Near East,
> likely as an incidental byproduct of early agriculture. Grain stores
> attracted rodents, rodents attracted wildcats, and the wildcats that
> tolerated human proximity gained a reliable food source. Over generations,
> this mutual convenience — rather than deliberate breeding — shaped the
> domestic cat we know today.

Which choice best describes the function of the second sentence ("Grain
stores attracted rodents... reliable food source") in the text as a whole?

A) It introduces a counterargument to the claim made in the first sentence.
B) It explains the mechanism behind the process introduced in the first
   sentence.
C) It summarizes the passage's conclusion.
D) It presents a hypothesis that the final sentence goes on to reject.

**R3.** Cross-text connections

**Text 1:** Nutrition researcher Elena Vasquez contends that intermittent
fasting produces meaningful long-term weight loss for most adults,
citing several clinical trials showing reduced body weight after six
months.

**Text 2:** A meta-analysis of fasting studies found that while
participants did lose weight during trial periods, most regained a
substantial portion of that weight within a year after the trial ended,
regardless of which fasting schedule they followed.

Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to
Vasquez's claim in Text 1?

A) By agreeing fully, since both texts report weight loss during fasting
   trials.
B) By questioning whether the weight loss Vasquez cites persists in the
   long term.
C) By arguing that fasting causes no weight loss at all.
D) By proposing an entirely different explanation for the initial weight
   loss.

**R4.** Central claim/purpose

> Honeybee colonies communicate the location of food sources through a
> "waggle dance," in which a foraging bee moves in a figure-eight pattern
> whose angle and duration encode direction and distance relative to the
> sun. Decades of research have confirmed that other bees observing the
> dance can locate the food source with notable accuracy, even when it lies
> miles from the hive. This discovery reshaped scientists' understanding of
> insect cognition, suggesting that complex symbolic communication is not
> unique to vertebrates.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

A) To describe the physical steps of the waggle dance in full detail.
B) To argue that bees are more intelligent than most vertebrates.
C) To explain how the discovery of the waggle dance changed scientific
   views on the limits of insect communication.
D) To summarize the history of honeybee research.

---

### Answer key

**R1 — B.** The sentence describes the proposal as "balancing" two
competing goals — that's the definition of well-balanced/sensible.
(A), (C), (D) are all plausible dictionary associations with "judicious"-
sounding contexts but none matches "balancing" from the text.

**R2 — B.** The second sentence lays out the cause-and-effect chain (grain
→ rodents → wildcats → tolerance) that explains *how* domestication in
sentence one actually happened — it's the mechanism, not a counterargument,
summary, or rejected hypothesis.

**R3 — B.** Text 2's finding — that weight is regained within a year — 
directly challenges the "meaningful long-term" part of Vasquez's claim,
without denying that fasting produces weight loss during the trial itself.

**R4 — C.** The passage's arc is entirely about the discovery reshaping
scientific understanding of insect cognition/communication — that's the
main point, not the dance's mechanics (A, too narrow), a comparison claim
the passage never makes (B, unsupported), or a history of the field (D, too
broad/inaccurate).
