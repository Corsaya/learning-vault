## R&W Module 1, Q3 — Word in context ("hampered")

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162304.png]]

**You picked A (reflected). Correct: B (hampered).**

The sentence: *"attempted to resolve a long-standing **paradox**: **although** water is critical
to most reactions that enable life, during the earliest stages … the presence of water likely
_____ the formation of proteins…"*

**Steps**
1. Find the logic word. Here there are two: **paradox** and **although**.
2. A paradox = two halves that clash. Half one is stated: *water is critical to life's reactions.*
3. So half two must be the **opposite**: water **hurt** the process.
4. Predict your own word before looking: *blocked / stopped / hurt.*
5. Match. "Hampered" = hindered. Done.

**Trap:** *Reflected* is **topically** plausible — water and biomolecules do go together — but it's
**neutral**. A paradox blank can never take a neutral word.

**Tell:** the words *paradox, although, but, yet, despite, however, surprisingly, counterintuitively*
mean the blank flips direction. Circle them before you read the choices.

## R&W Module 1, Q6 — Function of the underlined portion

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162523.png]]

**You picked C. Correct: D — "To identify a comparable circumstance that helps justify LaBerge and team's conclusion."**

**Steps**
1. Re-read the question: it says **function**, and it says **in the text as a whole**. That means
   "what job does this sentence do," not "what does it say."
2. Map the paragraph in three beats: (1) setup — why they studied lucid dreamers;
   (2) **underlined** — the finding: dreaming eyes tracked objects *the same way* awake eyes do;
   (3) conclusion — so dream objects aren't pure imagination.
3. The underlined sentence is beat 2, and it works by **comparison** (dream vs. awake).
4. Pick the choice with a **verb that matches the job**: *identify a comparable circumstance*.

**Trap:** C ("real-world implication") describes beat 3, not beat 2. Function questions are almost
always missed by picking the neighbouring beat.

**Easy tell:** function-question answers start with an infinitive — *To offer, to show, to illustrate,
to identify*. Read only the **verb** first and throw out any verb the sentence doesn't do.

## R&W Module 1, Q7 — Cross-text connections

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162657.png]]

**You picked A. Correct: D — "the kinds of reactions controversial public artworks often receive aren't exclusively the result of attributes inherent in the works themselves."**

**Steps**
1. Write one sentence for **Text 1's underlined claim only**: *bland-free public art provokes backlash
   because of what the art is.*
2. Write one sentence for **Text 2's critics**: *backlash happens when art isn't well integrated
   into its surroundings* — i.e. **context**, not the art itself.
3. Name the relationship in plain words: Text 2 says *"it's not only the art — it's the setting."*
4. Find the choice that says exactly that. D says the reactions "aren't **exclusively**" about the works.

**Trap:** A overshoots. Text 2's critics say the art's unconventionality is **not the whole story**,
not that it's **unrelated**. Extreme words — *unrelated, only, never, entirely* — are how cross-text
answers get made wrong.

**Method:** never answer a cross-text question before you can say each text's position in your own
words. Ten seconds of summarizing beats a minute of re-reading choices.

## R&W Module 1, Q8 — Inference from a literary text (*Evelina*)

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162712.png]]

**You picked B. Correct: D — "He is overly protective of Evelina, who would likely benefit from a greater variety of experiences than she has had thus far."**

**Steps**
1. The question is *"Lady Howard would most likely agree with which statement **about Reverend Villars**."*
   So you need **Lady Howard's view of Villars**, not the letter's general mood.
2. Find where she addresses him directly: *"Do not start at this proposal"* — she expects him to object.
   That tells you she thinks he's **protective**.
3. Find her argument: young people *"too rigidly sequestered"* develop unrealistic ideas; shown the world
   properly they see it as it is. That's the **benefit of exposure**.
4. Combine: he's over-protective + Evelina would benefit from more experience → **D**.

**Trap:** B swaps who holds the idealistic view. Lady Howard says **sheltered young people** become
idealistic — she never says **Villars** is idealistic or passed that on. Watch for answers that
relocate a trait from one person to another.

## R&W Module 1, Q13 — Complete the text using table data (sheep fiber)

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162754.png]]

**You picked D. Correct: A.**

## R&W Module 1, Q19 — Punctuation, matched dash pair

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162805.png]]

**You picked C (cooperation: this). Correct: B (cooperation—this).**

The sentence: *"Walston's findings indicate that**—**assuming solar panel installers' _____ practice has the potential to…"*

**Steps**
1. Look **backwards** from the blank for an opening dash, comma, or parenthesis. There's a dash right
   after *"that."*
2. That dash opened an interrupter (*"assuming solar panel installers' cooperation"*).
3. An interrupter must be closed with the **same** mark it opened with: dash → dash, comma → comma,
   paren → paren.
4. Only B closes with a dash.

**Trap:** the colon in C *sounds* fine because what follows is an explanation. Punctuation on the SAT is
decided by **structure**, not by sound. **A dash and a colon are never a pair.**

**Tell:** the instant you see one dash in the sentence, scan for its partner. This is a pure-recognition
point — a free one.

## R&W Module 2, Q2 — Word in context ("ubiquity")

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162837.png]]

**You picked A (heterogeneity). Correct: B (ubiquity).**

**Steps**
1. Find the repeated idea. The sentence says *"a **plethora** of beads … in **many** MSA sites"* and ends
   with *"the **widespread availability** of T. gibbosula."*
2. The blank has to echo that: **lots of them, everywhere**.
3. Predict: *commonness / everywhere-ness.* Match: **ubiquity**.

**Trap:** *heterogeneity* = variety. The text says the beads were all made from the **same** shell — the
opposite of variety. Beware of picking the "sophisticated-sounding" abstract noun.

**Tell:** word-in-context blanks are almost always **restated somewhere else in the same sentence**.
Find the restatement first; the answer is a synonym of it.

## R&W Module 2, Q3 — Word in context ("elasticity")

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162838.png]]

**You picked A (cohesion). Correct: B (elasticity).**

**Steps**
1. The colon after the blank means **what follows defines the blank**. Read past the colon first.
2. What follows: successful pairs foster *other* pairs' cygnets; **male-male** pairs also rear young.
3. That list is a list of **different arrangements** → the idea is **flexibility**.
4. Match: elasticity = flexibility.

**Trap:** *cohesion* (togetherness) and *reciprocity* (mutual exchange) both sound like nice things a
swan family would have. Neither is what the examples demonstrate. The examples are **varied structures**,
so the word must mean **variety/flexibility**.

**Tell:** **a colon means the answer is defined on the right of it.** Read right of the colon, then answer.

## R&W Module 2, Q4 — Word in context ("manifest", Beckett)

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162839.png]]

**You picked D (Dwindling). Correct: B (Perceptible).**

**Steps**
1. This is the *"what does X most nearly mean"* type — the word is already in the text, so plug each
   choice **into the sentence** and read it aloud in your head.
2. Get the situation: the sun *"already down"* — below the horizon — yet **manifest** *in the tongues of fire*.
3. So: the sun itself is gone, but it's still **visible / detectable** through the light.
4. B = perceptible.

**Trap:** *realized* is the everyday meaning of "manifest," and *dwindling* fits the sunset **mood**.
Both are mood-matching, not grammar-matching. The phrase is *"manifest **in** the tongues of fire"* —
"realized in" and "dwindling in" don't work with that preposition.

**Tell:** on "most nearly means," **the preposition after the word decides it.** Test which choice
survives the preposition.

## R&W Module 2, Q5 — Main purpose

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162840.png]]

**You picked D. Correct: B — "It showcases how Adam's flexibility and consideration strengthen his relationship with his great-uncle Richard."**

**Steps**
1. "Main purpose" = what the text **does**, in one verb + one object.
2. Trace the arc: meeting goes well → Adam accommodates (stay on the ship, any hour, anywhere) →
   his willingness *"won the old man's heart"* → they become *"almost like old friends."*
3. Cause → effect: **Adam's considerateness → closer relationship.** That's the whole arc, so it's the purpose.
4. Throw out any choice that only covers **one beat** of the arc.

**Trap:** D invents a **contrast** ("wary first impressions"). The text opens with *"auspiciously"* —
positive from line one. There is no wariness anywhere. **If a choice describes a change in attitude,
you must be able to point at the "before."**

## R&W Module 2, Q9 — Main idea (spike waves)

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162844.png]]

**You picked B. Correct: D — "when traveling waves intersect in specific ways, the resulting wave may be higher than would be expected based on the properties of traveling waves."**

**Steps**
1. Split the passage into **background** vs. **finding**. Background = traveling waves break past a
   steepness threshold, which limits their height. Finding = *McAllister et al. found spike waves can
   **exceed** that constraint.*
2. Main idea = the **finding**, always. Background is what the wrong answers are built from.
3. Restate the finding simply: *spike waves get taller than traveling-wave rules predict.* → D.

**Trap:** B keeps the finding's shape but re-attaches the threshold: it says spike waves exceed limits
*"if their height-to-length ratio reaches a critical threshold"* — the passage says the **opposite**,
that **other factors** (jet stability, cavity shape) do the work. Wrong answers on main-idea questions
are usually **90% right with one clause reversed.** Read every clause of the surviving choice.

## R&W Module 2, Q12 — Table data, TRAPPIST-1 (**blind spot, you rated this 5**)

**You picked D. Correct: B — "6.1 days, and its rotational period is also 6.1 days."**

## R&W Module 2, Q14 — Most logical completion (Titan methane)

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162849.png]]

**You picked D. Correct: A — "some disagreements between the model's simulations of Titan's precipitation and humidity and the moon's actual precipitation and humidity are to be expected."**

**Steps**
1. Find the **limitation** the passage sets up. Here: observational data are *"too sparse and discrepant"*
   to pin down the real range.
2. Find the **workaround**: the model prescribes **one uniform value** for the lowest atmospheric level.
3. Ask the completion question: *"It is therefore important to note that ____"* — "therefore" means
   **the logical consequence of using one value where reality varies**.
4. One value ≠ reality → model output will **differ from observation** in places. → A.

**Trap:** D sounds mechanistic and science-y, but it says the inconsistencies come from **real variations
in methane** — impossible, because the model **doesn't contain** any variation. A choice can't be
explained by something the passage removed.

**Tell:** "It is therefore important to note that ___" is a **consequence** blank. Ask "so what breaks?"

## R&W Module 2, Q20 — Finite verb vs. participle

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162855.png]]

**You picked A (had prioritized). Correct: D (prioritizing).**

The sentence: *"…ancient Egyptians **slept** on hard, elevated headrests, their design _____ airflow, protection from insects, and hairstyle maintenance over comfort."*

**Steps**
1. Find the **main verb** of the sentence: **slept**. Subject: *ancient Egyptians.* That's a complete clause.
2. Look at what's after the comma: *their design ____ …* — there's no conjunction (*and, but, so*) and
   no semicolon.
3. Therefore this part **cannot** be a second sentence. So the blank **cannot be a finite verb**.
4. Choose the **-ing / -ed participle**: *prioritizing.*

**Trap:** all three wrong choices are finite verbs (*had prioritized, was prioritizing, prioritized*)
and each makes a **comma splice**. When three choices share a property and one is different, the
different one is usually right.

## R&W Module 2, Q21 — Finite verb vs. participle (same trap again)

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162856.png]]

**You picked C (were searching). Correct: B (searching).**

The sentence: *"…scientists _____ for evidence of self-awareness in snakes, species that…, **adapted** the test to foreground smell, modifying…"*

**Steps**
1. Find the main verb: **adapted**. Subject: *scientists.*
2. The blank sits **between the subject and its verb** — that slot is a **modifier**, not a verb slot.
3. So: participle. **searching.**
4. Sanity check by deleting the modifier: *"scientists … adapted the test"* ✅.

**Trap:** identical to Q20 — you know this rule, it just has to survive time pressure.

## R&W Module 2, Q23 — Transition (**blind spot, you rated this 4**)

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162858.png]]

**You picked D (nevertheless). Correct: B (more often).**

**Steps**
1. Before: *historical accounts say people moved from the Great Plains to **California**; however, a 1940
   census study **complicates** that narrative.*
2. After: *migrants in the hardest-hit states **merely relocated within the region**.*
3. The blank sits **after** the "however." The contrast has **already been spent** by "however." The blank's
   job is to say **how** the narrative gets complicated: this happened **more frequently** than the
   California move.
4. Pick the frequency/comparison word: **more often**.

**Trap:** *nevertheless* is a second contrast — but you don't get two contrasts in a row against the same
thing. **Check whether the contrast is already handled by a word before the blank.**

**Tell for both transition misses:** transitions were your only confident misses. Tomorrow, on every
transition, do this literally: **cover the four choices, say the relationship out loud in your own
words (add / oppose / cause / example / restate / compare), then uncover.** Your instinct on transitions
is calibrated badly — the procedure isn't.

## Math Module 1, Q19 — Read an intercept, then divide (star cluster)

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162922.png]]

**You picked B (938). Correct: A (811).**

Total mass of M-type + K-type stars = 127,882 quettagrams. Graph = possible combos of M-type (x) and K-type (y).

**Steps**
1. Ask what "mass of **each M-type** star" needs: a case with **only M-type stars**. That's where y = 0 —
   the **x-intercept**.
2. Read the x-intercept: ≈ **158**.
3. Divide: 127,882 ÷ 158 ≈ **809.4** → closest choice **811**.
4. Sanity check the other intercept so you know which is which: y-intercept ≈ 136 → 127,882 ÷ 136 ≈ 940,
   which is choice **B** — the K-type answer.

**Trap:** the test puts **both** intercept answers in the choices. B isn't a miscalculation, it's the
**other star type**. Whenever a graph has two intercepts, expect both answers to be offered.

**Desmos fix:** don't eyeball gridlines. Type the line into Desmos from two clear points, or type
`y = -136/158 x + 136` and **click the x-intercept** — Desmos labels it exactly. Then `127882/158`.

## Math Module 1, Q20 — SOH-CAH-TOA, solve for the hypotenuse

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162923.png]]

**You picked C (18/cos Q). Correct: D (18/sin Q).**

Right angle at R. SR = 18. Find QS.

**Steps**
1. **Label relative to the named angle.** The angle is **Q**. Redraw or annotate: from Q, the
   **opposite** side is SR, the **adjacent** is QR, and QS is the **hypotenuse**.
2. Write both ratios with QS in them: `sin Q = SR/QS`, `cos Q = QR/QS`.
3. You were **given SR = 18**, so only the **sin** equation contains a known number. That alone picks it.
4. Solve: `sin Q = 18/QS` → `QS·sin Q = 18` → `QS = 18/sin Q`.

**Trap:** the picture is drawn with the right angle at the bottom right, which makes SR *look* adjacent.
The figure says **"not drawn to scale."**

**30-second rule:** on any trig question, first write **O / A / H** next to the three sides **from the
named angle's point of view**. Then pick the ratio that uses the number you were handed. Desmos can't
save you from a mislabeled side — this is the one you fix with a pencil mark, not a tool.

## Math Module 2, Q12 — Probability with a compound condition (**blind spot, you rated this 5**)

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162938-1.png]]

**You picked D (5/16). Correct: A (5/32).**

Set K = positive integers ≤ 160. P(even **and** ≤ 50)?

**Steps**
1. **Denominator:** how many total? Integers 1–160 → **160**.
2. **Numerator, one condition at a time.**
   - ≤ 50 → 50 numbers.
   - of those, **even** → 50 ÷ 2 = **25**.
3. 25/160 = **5/32**.
4. Check: your answer 5/16 = 50/160 — that's "≤ 50" **without** the even filter.

**Trap:** the word **and**. You applied one of two conditions. This is why it felt like a 5 —
the arithmetic was right, one filter was silently dropped.

**Execution rule for tomorrow:** when a probability question contains **and / both / also / neither**,
**underline every condition and count them**, then confirm your numerator survived all of them.

## Math Module 2, Q15 — Rational exponents (SPR)

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162940-1.png]]

**You answered 41/9. Correct: 41/81** (also accepted: .5061, .5062, .506).

Given `∛(p²) = t^(9/7)`, and `t = p^(3n−1)`. Find n.

**Steps**
1. Rewrite the radical: `∛(p²) = p^(2/3)`.
2. Substitute `t = p^(3n−1)` into the right side: `p^(2/3) = p^((9/7)(3n−1))`.
3. Same base, p > 1 → **exponents equal**: `2/3 = (9/7)(3n − 1)`.
4. Clear fractions — multiply both sides by **21**: `14 = 27(3n − 1)`.
5. Distribute: `14 = 81n − 27` → `41 = 81n` → **n = 41/81**.

**Trap:** none conceptually — **your method was right the whole way** and step 5 divided by 9 instead of 81.

**Execution rule:** on a grid-in, the last arithmetic step is where you lose points. Retype the final
line into Desmos: `41/81` and confirm. It costs four seconds. Also: **grid-ins accept decimals**, so if a
fraction feels risky, enter `.5061`.

## Math Module 2, Q16 — Rational function, find a constant ⭐ *(the regression question — see Part 3)*

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162941.png]]

**You picked B (22). Correct: A (23).**

`g(x) = (x² − x − a)/(x³ − x − b)`. Graph passes through (0, 22), and g(−22) = 0. Find b.

**Steps (by hand)**
1. Use the **easy** condition first: `g(0) = 22`. Plug x = 0:
   `(0 − 0 − a)/(0 − 0 − b) = (−a)/(−b) = a/b = 22` → **a = 22b**.
2. Use the **zero** condition: a fraction equals 0 **only when its numerator is 0**. So:
   `(−22)² − (−22) − a = 0` → `484 + 22 − a = 0` → **a = 506**.
3. Back-substitute: `506 = 22b` → **b = 23**.

**Trap:** B (22) is `g(0)` — the number sitting in the question. C (−22) is the *x*-value where g = 0.
Both wrong choices are **numbers printed in the prompt**. On "find the constant" questions, an answer
that's already visible in the question is suspicious.

**The key idea you must not lose:** `g(something) = 0` ⇒ **numerator = 0**. Never touch the denominator.
(Denominator = 0 gives you an **asymptote / undefined**, which is a different question type.)

**Desmos version:** see [[#3.2 Regression as a constant-solver — this is the Q16 trick]].

## Math Module 2, Q17 — Angles at an intersection, "could NOT be"

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162942.png]]

**You picked C. Correct: A — (−18x + 280)°.**

Two lines cross. One angle = (9x − 140)°. Which could **NOT** be the sum of any two of the four angles?

**Steps**
1. Name the four angles using the two facts: **vertical angles are equal**, **adjacent angles sum to 180**.
   - Given angle: `9x − 140`
   - Its vertical twin: `9x − 140`
   - Each adjacent one: `180 − (9x − 140)` = **`−9x + 320`**
2. **List every possible pair-sum** (there are only three distinct ones):
   - given + vertical = `2(9x − 140)` = **`18x − 280`**
   - adjacent + adjacent = `2(−9x + 320)` = **`−18x + 640`**
   - given + adjacent = `(9x − 140) + (−9x + 320)` = **`180`**
3. Compare to the choices. B = `−18x + 640` ✅, C = `18x − 280` ✅, D = `180` ✅.
4. **A is not on the list** → A is the answer.

**Trap:** you **graphed** it. A "could NOT be" question asks you to build a **complete list and find the
missing item** — graphing shows you one case, and one case can never prove impossibility.

**Recognition cue:** *could NOT be / must be / always true / for all values* ⇒ **enumerate, don't
graph**. Write the list of everything possible; the odd one out is the answer.

## Math Module 2, Q19 — System with "at least one solution" (**blind spot, you rated this 4 — and you graphed it**)

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162943-1.png]]

**You picked B (II only). Correct: C (I and II).**

`2x + 9y = 7`. Which could be the other equation if the system has **at least one solution**?
I. `3x + 13.5y = 10.5`  II. `3x − 13.5y = 10.5`

**Steps**
1. Translate the phrase: **"at least one solution" = one solution OR infinitely many.** Only **no
   solution** is excluded.
2. Test I for proportionality: multiply `2x + 9y = 7` by **1.5** → `3x + 13.5y = 10.5`. **Identical line**
   ⇒ infinitely many solutions ⇒ that counts as "at least one." ✅
3. Test II: slopes differ (`+13.5` vs `−13.5`), so the lines cross once ⇒ one solution. ✅
4. Both work → **C**.

**Trap — and this is the one to burn in:** in Desmos, **two identical lines render as one line.** You saw
a single line and read it as "not two lines / no intersection," when it actually means **infinitely
many intersections**.

**Desmos interpretation rule:**
| What you see | What it means |
|---|---|
| Two lines crossing | exactly one solution |
| Two parallel lines, no touch | no solution |
| **Only one line visible after typing two equations** | **same line → infinitely many solutions** |

**Language rule:** *at least one* = 1 or ∞. *Exactly one* = crossing only. *No solution* = parallel,
different intercept. *Infinitely many* = all coefficients proportional (check `a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ = c₁/c₂`).

## Math Module 2, Q21 — Factored form read off a polynomial graph

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162945.png]]

**You picked B (3). Correct: D (−25).**

Graph of `y = g(x)` shown. `g(x + 28) = (1/10)(x − a)²(x − b)²(x − c)`. Which could be b?

**Steps**
1. Read the roots **off the graph of g**: it crosses/touches the x-axis at **−2, 1, 3**.
   So `g(−2) = 0`, `g(1) = 0`, `g(3) = 0`.
2. Now handle the **shift**. The equation defines `g(x + 28)`, not `g(x)`. The right side is zero when
   `x = a`, `x = b`, or `x = c`. At those x-values, `g(x + 28) = 0`.
3. So `x + 28` must be one of g's roots: `x + 28 ∈ {−2, 1, 3}`.
4. Solve each: `x = −30`, `x = −27`, `x = −25`.
5. b must be one of `{−30, −27, −25}`. Only **−25** is a choice. → **D**.

**Trap:** B (3) is a root of **g itself**, i.e. the answer if the graph shown had been `y = g(x + 28)`.
The whole question is the **28 shift**, and 3 is the bait for skipping it.

**Shift rule (memorize the direction):** `g(x + 28)` moves the graph **28 to the LEFT**. So a root at
`x = 3` on g sits at `x = 3 − 28 = −25` on the shifted function. Inside the parentheses, **plus means left**.

**Note:** bounce vs. cross (squared factor vs. single factor) matters for telling a/b from c, but here
every candidate root leads to the same three x-values, so you don't need it. Still worth knowing:
**touch-and-bounce = even power, cross straight through = odd power.**

## Math Module 2, Q22 — Chained percents (SPR)

![[Courses/SAT/Practice Tests/Practice 11/Screenshot_20260820_162947.png]]

**You answered 365. Correct: 60000.**

A is 468% of B. A is 0.780% of C. C is p% of B. Find p.

**Steps**
1. **Turn every percent sentence into an equation immediately.** "X is n% of Y" → `X = (n/100)·Y`.
   - `A = 4.68b`
   - `A = 0.00780c`
   - `c = (p/100)·b`
2. Two expressions for A → set equal: `4.68b = 0.00780c`.
3. Solve for c: `c = 4.68b / 0.00780 = **600b**`.
4. Match to equation 3: `(p/100)·b = 600b` → `p/100 = 600` → **p = 60,000**.
5. Sanity check that the huge answer is fine: A is 468% of B (A is big) but only 0.78% of C (C is
   enormous), so C ≫ B. A percent in the tens of thousands is **expected**, not a red flag.

**Trap:** dividing 468 by 0.780 gives **600** — and 365 came from a different pairing. Also: many
students stop at 600 and grid that. **The question asks for p, and p is a percent — the last step is
×100.**

**Desmos version (one line):** define `b = 1`, then type
`4.68*1 = 0.00780c` — Desmos solves for c and shows `c = 600`. Then `p = 600*100 = 60000`.
Because everything is proportional, **setting b = 1 is always legal here**.

# Part 3 — Desmos as a solver

Your Practice 11 log says it plainly: **Desmos used on 17 of 44 questions, and only 2 of those
17 used it as a solver.** Six were pure arithmetic. That's a four-function calculator with a
graph attached. This section is the upgrade.

## 3.1 The core move: the `~` (tilde) key

In Desmos, `=` **draws** a relationship. `~` **finds the numbers that make it true.**

Type `~` and Desmos creates a **regression**: it takes whatever letters you left undefined and
solves for them, then prints their values in a box under the expression. That's it. That's the trick.

**How to get the data in:**
1. Press the **`+`** button (top-left of the expression list) → **table**.
2. Desmos gives you columns `x₁` and `y₁`. Type the pairs in.
3. On a **new line**, type the model with `~` instead of `=`.

## 3.2 Regression as a constant-solver — this is the Q16 trick

This is the part that generalizes beyond data tables. **Any question that gives you a function
with unknown constants plus enough (input, output) facts is a regression.**

**Q16 worked in Desmos:**

| line | what you type |
|---|---|
| 1 | make a **table**: `x₁` = `0`, `-22`  ·  `y₁` = `22`, `0` |
| 2 | `y_1 ~ (x_1^2 - x_1 - a)/(x_1^3 - x_1 - b)` |

Desmos prints a parameters box: **a ≈ 506, b ≈ 23.** Read off b. Answer **A**.

**Why it works:** "the graph passes through (0, 22)" and "g(−22) = 0" are just two data points.
Two points, two unknowns — the regression has exactly enough to pin them down.

> ⚠️ **Always sanity-check a regression's output.** Regressions on **rational** functions can land
> on a bad local fit if the numbers are ugly. Check by looking at the **residual / RMSE** — it should
> be essentially **0**, and the parameter values should be clean-ish. If the box looks wrong or Desmos
> says it can't converge, fall back to the hand method in the Q16 entry above (it's four lines).
> For **linear, quadratic, and exponential** models the regression is rock solid — no caveat.

**The general recipe**
1. Write the function exactly as the question gives it, with the unknown letters left as letters.
2. Collect every "passes through," "f(3) = 7," "when x = 0 the value is…", "the graph has a
   y-intercept of…" statement — each one is an **(x, y) pair**.
3. Table them. Regress. Read the constant they asked for.
4. Confirm the residual is ~0.

## 3.3 The three regressions you should have in muscle memory

Enter the table first, then one of these:

| Situation in the problem | Type this | Desmos gives you |
|---|---|---|
| Constant **rate** of change, "increases by 4 each year", straight line, line of best fit | `y_1 ~ mx_1 + b` | `m`, `b` |
| Thrown / launched object, height, area, "maximum value", parabola | `y_1 ~ ax_1^2 + bx_1 + c` | `a`, `b`, `c` |
| **Percent** growth or decay, "doubles every", "loses 12% per hour", half-life | `y_1 ~ ab^{x_1}` | `a` = start, `b` = multiplier |

**Reading the exponential result:** `b = 1.07` → **+7% per period**. `b = 0.88` → **−12% per period**.
`b = 2` → doubling. Going the other way: "grows 240%" ⇒ multiplier `3.4` (**add the original 100%** —
this is a standard trap).

**Getting a prediction out of it:** after the regression runs, `a` and `b` are live variables. Just type
`a*b^12` on a new line to get the value at x = 12, or graph `y = ab^x` and click the point.

## 3.4 Desmos moves that would have saved points on this exact test

| Miss | The Desmos move you skipped |
|---|---|
| **M1 Q19** (intercept) | Type the line, **click the x-intercept**. Desmos labels it `(158, 0)` exactly. Never eyeball gridlines. |
| **M2 Q19** (at least one solution) | You *did* graph it — the fix is **interpretation**: one visible line after typing two equations = **same line = infinitely many**. |
| **M2 Q15** (rational exponents) | Retype the **final** arithmetic (`41/81`) instead of doing it in your head. |
| **M2 Q22** (chained percents) | Set `b=1`, type `4.68 = 0.00780c`, click the solution, `×100`. |
| **M2 Q16** (rational function) | The regression above. |
| **M2 Q17** (could NOT be) | ❌ **Don't** graph. Enumerate. Desmos is the wrong tool. |
| **M1 Q20** (SOH-CAH-TOA) | ❌ Desmos can't fix a mislabeled side. Annotate O/A/H by hand first. |

## 3.5 Other Desmos one-liners worth having ready

- **Solve any equation:** type it with `x` (e.g. `3x^2 - 5x = 12`), then click where the curve
  meets the line — or type both sides as two `y=` equations and click the **intersection**. Every
  point Desmos draws can be **clicked** to get exact coordinates.
- **Systems:** type both equations as-is (`2x + 9y = 7` etc.). Desmos handles non-`y=` forms fine.
  Click the intersection.
- **Vertex / max / min:** graph it, click the turning point. Desmos labels it. No `−b/2a` needed.
- **Define a function once, reuse it:** `f(x) = ...` then type `f(7)` on its own line.
- **Sliders for "which value of k…":** type the equation with a `k`, hit **"add slider,"** drag until
  the graph does what the question describes, read k.
- **Mean / median / stats:** `mean(1,4,7,9)`, `median(...)`, `stdev(...)`, `total(...)` all work.
- ⚠️ **Desmos is a graphing calculator, not a proof.** It answers *"what is"* questions. It cannot
  answer *"could not be," "must be true for all," or "which is always"* — those need enumeration.

# Part 4 — How to read an R&W question without getting lost

18 of your 27 misses were R&W, and your average confidence there was 3.41 against Math's 4.14.
The problem isn't the passages — it's that you're reading them **without a question in mind**, so
everything looks relevant and nothing decides the answer.

## 4.1 The order to do things in (never varies)

> **QUESTION → PREDICT → PASSAGE → MATCH → ELIMINATE**

1. **Read the question stem first — before the passage.** Every R&W passage is 3–6 sentences.
   It's short enough that reading it "cold" wastes the read.
2. **Name the question type from the stem** (table in 4.2). The type tells you what to hunt for.
3. **Read the passage hunting for that one thing.** Not for comprehension — for the specific item.
4. **Cover the choices and predict the answer in your own words.** This is the single highest-value
   habit for you: every word-in-context miss came from reading the four choices first, which makes
   the topically-plausible one glow.
5. **Uncover and match** your prediction. Then **eliminate** by finding the *specific broken word* in
   each wrong choice — don't reject on vibe.

## 4.2 What the stem is telling you to hunt for

| Stem says                                               | Type                              | What you actually look for                                                                   | The classic trap                                                                       |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "most logical and precise **word or phrase**"           | Word in context                   | The **restatement** elsewhere in the sentence, and any **logic word** (although/paradox/but) | A word that fits the **topic** but not the **logic**                                   |
| "as used in the text, **most nearly means**"            | Word in context (2nd meaning)     | The **preposition/phrase right after** the word                                              | The **everyday** meaning of the word                                                   |
| "**function** of the underlined portion"                | Craft                             | The **job** it does between the sentence before and after                                    | The job of the **neighbouring** sentence                                               |
| "**main purpose** / main idea of the text"              | Craft / Info                      | The **finding**, not the background; the **arc**, not one beat                               | A choice covering only the setup, or one clause reversed                               |
| "how would [Text 2] **respond to**"                     | Cross-text                        | Each author's **position in one sentence**, then the relationship                            | **Extreme** words: *unrelated, only, never, entirely*                                  |
| "which **quotation** … illustrates the claim"           | Info                              | Split the claim into **2–3 requirements**, use as a checklist                                | A quote that's **on topic** but hits none of them                                      |
| "most effectively **uses data from the [table/graph]**" | Info                              | The **claim before the blank** first; then a **specific number**                             | A real number from the **wrong row or column**                                         |
| "most logically **completes** the text"                 | Info                              | The **limitation** the passage set up → "so what breaks?"                                    | A cause the passage explicitly removed                                                 |
| "conforms to the **conventions of Standard English**"   | Conventions                       | Is there already a **main verb**? Is there an **unclosed dash/comma**?                       | Choices that "sound right"                                                             |
| "most logical **transition**"                           | Expression                        | Summarize before + after in 5 words each; name **add/oppose/cause/example/compare**          | Right direction, **wrong object**; or a contrast already spent by an earlier *however* |
| "**notes**… student wants to…"                          | Expression (Rhetorical Synthesis) | The **stated goal** in the last line — underline it                                          | A true sentence that doesn't do the **stated goal**                                    |

## 4.3 Anti-getting-lost rules

- **Long sentence?** Find the subject and the main verb, then **delete everything between them**.
  (This is exactly the M2 Q20/Q21 fix, and it's the #1 predicted English pattern for tomorrow.)
- **Colon or dash?** Whatever comes after **defines or explains** what came before. On a blank
  before a colon, read past the colon first.
- **Two texts?** Never look at a choice until you can state each text's position in your own words.
- **A choice that's 90% right?** Read **every clause** of it. SAT wrong answers are usually one
  reversed clause inside an otherwise perfect sentence.
- **Confidence 3 = your lean is not evidence.** You went **4-for-14** on questions you rated 3.
  A 3 means: go back to the text and find **one concrete line** that decides it, or **flag and move on.**
  Do not go with the feeling. This is your biggest single execution leak.
- **Pacing:** ~1:11 per R&W question. If you've spent 90 seconds and haven't found the deciding
  line, flag it. You had 6 lucky guesses on Practice 11; time saved on stuck questions is what
  turns those into real points.

# Part 5 — What's worth knowing for tomorrow (2026-08-22)

> **Honest framing first:** nobody knows what's on tomorrow's test. College Board doesn't publish it,
> and "leaked question" posts are noise. What *is* real is that the **question types repeat**, so
> "predictions" from prep sites are really *"the patterns that show up on nearly every form."*
> Treat everything below as high-frequency patterns, not insider information.

## 5.1 The 8 patterns most commonly flagged for the August form

From current prep-site pattern trackers ([Strategic Test Prep](https://www.strategictestprep.com/post/10-august-sat-predictions-what-students-should-be-ready-to-see)),
cross-checked against your own weak spots:

1. **Right square pyramids** — keep base area, slant height, and vertical height separate. **Draw it.**
2. **Factoring with an unknown positive integer** — the "must be a positive integer" condition is what
   picks the answer, not the algebra.
3. **Exponential growth wording** — a **240% increase ⇒ multiply by 3.4**, not 2.4. Add the original 100%.
4. **Multi-part word problems into one equation** — before writing anything, answer: *what does my
   variable represent?*
5. **Random sampling vs. random assignment** — **cause and effect requires random assignment.**
   Sampling only lets you generalize to the population.
6. **Scale factors across dimensions** — linear ×k ⇒ area ×k², volume ×k³.
7. **Working backwards on discount / percent problems** — plug the choices in rather than build the algebra.
8. **Let Desmos handle constants** — regressions, defined functions, intersections, systems (Part 3).
9. **English: the long sentence with a hidden main verb** — subject, main verb, delete the middle.
   ⭐ **This is literally your M2 Q20 and Q21 miss.** If one English prediction pays off tomorrow, it's this.

## 5.3 Grammar rules most likely to earn you points tomorrow

These are ranked by how often they appear × how reliably they're decidable in ten seconds.

1. **Main verb already present ⇒ the blank is a participle (-ing / -ed).** Your two identical misses.
2. **Matched pairs.** A dash opens ⇒ a dash closes. A comma opens ⇒ a comma closes. **Never mix.**
3. **Semicolon = period.** Both sides must be complete sentences. Same for a period.
   **Colon:** left side must be a complete sentence; right side can be anything.
4. **Buried subject–verb agreement.** Delete every prepositional phrase and modifier between the
   subject and verb, then check. *"The **collection** of rare manuscripts and letters **is**…"*
5. **Pronoun and possessive.** *its* (belonging to it) vs. *it's* (it is); *their / there / they're*;
   *whose / who's*. Plural possessive: *installers'*.
6. **Dangling modifier.** An opening *-ing* or participial phrase must describe **the noun right after
   the comma**. *"Having studied the data, **the conclusion** was clear"* ✗ — the conclusion didn't study.
7. **Parallel structure in a list.** All items same form: *airflow, protection, and maintenance* ✅.
8. **Verb tense follows the passage's timeline**, not the sentence in isolation. Look for a date or a
   time marker in a neighbouring sentence.
9. **No comma between a subject and its verb**, ever, unless a matched pair brackets an interrupter.
10. **Transitions:** you don't get two contrasts in a row against the same thing. Check whether an
    earlier *however / but / yet* already spent the contrast.
