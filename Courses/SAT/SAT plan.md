I actually think you’ve identified the key insight.

The SAT is not primarily a knowledge test once you’re around a 1340. It’s becoming a pattern recognition and decision-making test.

I’d describe the progression like this:

- 1000–1200: Learn the content.
- 1200–1350: Reduce mistakes and fill knowledge gaps.
- 1350–1500+: Instantly recognize patterns and execute efficiently.

That’s why your vault idea has so much potential.

Math: I agree this is your highest-upside section

Based on your past scores, I think math offers the biggest opportunity.

The goal shouldn’t be “learn more math.”

The goal should be:

“Can I identify this question type within 5 seconds?”

For every math problem, your brain should run something like:

See problem

  

↓

  

Identify pattern

  

↓

  

Recall algorithm

  

↓

  

Use Desmos if advantageous

  

↓

  

Solve

  

↓

  

Move on

For example:

|   |   |
|---|---|
|You see…|Immediately think…|
|Function table|Find zeros → Flip sign → Factor|
|Quadratic|Vertex? Factored? Quadratic formula?|
|Circle equation|Complete the square|
|Scatterplot|Trend, not exact values|
|Exponential growth|Common ratio|
|System of equations|Desmos or substitution?|
|Absolute value|Split into cases|

You don’t want to “figure it out” from scratch on test day. You want to recognize it.

Desmos

You’re right that mastering Desmos can be a major advantage, but use it strategically.

Think of Desmos as a decision tool, not a crutch.

You should know:

- when graphing is faster than algebra,
- when a table reveals the answer,
- when intersections solve a system instantly,
- when sliders or regressions are useful,
- and when mental math is actually quicker.

The best SAT math students don’t graph everything—they graph the right things.

  

Reading & Writing: the same philosophy

English isn’t about reading beautifully.

It’s about recognizing what the question is testing.

For every question, identify the pattern first.

Grammar

Pattern → Rule

- Comma splice
- Semicolon
- Pronoun agreement
- Verb tense
- Parallel structure
- Modifier placement

Don’t “read until it sounds right.” Know the governing rule.

Reading

Pattern → Evidence

- Main idea
- Inference
- Function of a sentence
- Supporting evidence
- Vocabulary in context
- Transition
- Organization

Again, the first question should be:

What kind of question is this?

Only then should you read with that purpose in mind.

  

The skill you’re really building

This reminds me of something from rowing.

When you’re new, every stroke requires conscious thought.

As you improve, the stroke becomes automatic, and you can focus on race strategy.

The SAT works the same way.

You don’t want to consciously solve every problem.

You want to build automatic recognition so your mental energy is reserved for the truly difficult questions.
